# Day 1 — As a Father Pities His Children

Jasper and Nora had spent the last thirty days of October rebuilding things in their marriage that had worn thin without either of them noticing, and somewhere in that work he'd started thinking of the household as a project nearing completion — the foundation finally solid, the hard part behind him. Then a thunderstorm rolled through at two in the morning and their daughter Ivy, three years old, started screaming his name from her room like the world was ending.

He got up fast, more out of duty than tenderness, already composing the efficient version of comfort in his head: check the window's shut, check the nightlight's on, tell her it's just noise, get her back down, get himself back to sleep before the alarm. He'd provided the house, the roof, the room she was crying in. That felt, to him, like the job. The rest — sitting with her fear instead of managing it, staying until she actually settled instead of until she stopped making noise — felt like something he could get better at once he wasn't this tired, once the kids were old enough to actually talk to.

He opened her door ready to deliver the efficient version. Ivy was standing up in the crib with her arms already reaching, not for a fixed window latch, but for him. Something in that stopped him. He sat down on the floor of her room, pulled her into his lap, and stayed until her breathing slowed on its own, no clock in his head about what came next.

*A good father handles what's necessary and lets the rest wait for an easier season* — Jasper had never said that sentence to himself, but it was the sentence he'd been living by since Ivy was born, the same one that had nearly sent him back to bed with a fixed nightlight and a closed door. Sitting there in the dark, he understood it differently: what she'd reached for in that crib was him, not a fixed latch.

Scripture names exactly what a child is building in a moment like that one, whether the father realizes it or not: "As a father pities his children, so the LORD pities those who fear him" (Psalm 103:13, RSV-CE). The psalm doesn't compare God's compassion to some abstract fatherly ideal. It compares it to an actual father — the ordinary man a child already knows, whose face and arms and patience become, without either of them choosing it, the child's first working model of what "Father" means at all. Ivy wasn't going to remember whether the latch got checked. She was going to remember, at a level beneath memory, whether her cry brought someone who stayed.

That's the ground this whole month stands on. A father who thinks his real job is providing and staying out of the way has the story backward — presence isn't the reward for having handled the essentials; presence is one of the essentials, maybe the one a child needs most and can least explain wanting. Every ordinary moment like a 2 a.m. thunderstorm is quietly teaching a child something about God long before that child ever sits in a pew and hears the word "Father" used for Him.

**Today:** Name one place this week where you've been giving your kids the efficient version of yourself instead of the present one. Go do the slower version today, even for five extra minutes, and say nothing about it — just stay.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've told myself the essentials were enough and left the presence for a season I keep pushing back. Thank You for pitying me the way a father pities his own children, patient with me long before I deserved it. Teach me to stay the way You stay, so my kids learn who You are by learning who I am with them. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
