# Day 23 — Discipline While There Is Hope

Kellan had run out of consequences to take away. Dashiell was seventeen, skipping first period twice a week, caught vaping in the garage a month ago, mouthing off at dinner in a way that used to earn a reaction and now barely got one because Kellan had started expecting it. The car was already gone. The phone plan was already stripped down. Somewhere in the last few months, without deciding to, Kellan had started thinking of his son less as a kid going through something and more as a problem he was running out of ways to solve.

*He's eighteen in a year — at some point this stops being my problem to fix* — Kellan hadn't said that thought out loud to anyone, not even Dashiell's mother, but it had been sitting underneath every escalating punishment for weeks, the quiet math of a man getting ready to write off a relationship he was tired of losing.

Scripture speaks to exactly that temptation, and it's worth reading closely, because it says something narrower than most men expect. "Discipline your son while there is hope; do not set your heart on his destruction" (Proverbs 19:18, RSV-CE). This verse calls a father to keep showing up for his son while there is still hope, not to punish him harder or write him off. "While there is hope" names a window for staying present, not a deadline for cracking down. "Do not set your heart on his destruction" warns against the exact drift Kellan had fallen into — writing a son off in his own heart, deciding he's a lost cause, long before anything is actually settled.

**On St. John Bosco:** in the industrial slums of nineteenth-century Turin, Bosco spent his life among boys other people had already given up on — delinquents, runaways, kids nobody expected to amount to much. He didn't build his approach around harsher punishment. He built what he called a preventive system: stay present with a boy before trouble starts, correct him through reason and kindness rather than the rod, give him something worth doing with his hands and his hope. Bosco kept believing a difficult kid was still reachable long after most adults around that boy had stopped believing it. That persistent belief is what actually reached them.

Kellan didn't have a plan for turning Dashiell around overnight, and this verse didn't hand him one. What it gave him was a different question to ask instead of "what do I take away next" — namely, what would it look like to stay present with his son the way Bosco stayed present with boys nobody else wanted.

**Today:** Instead of another consequence or lecture, spend twenty minutes today doing something your struggling child actually enjoys, with him, before you address anything he's done wrong. Let presence come before correction, just once.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I confess how close I've come to giving up on my own child in my heart, even while still going through the motions of discipline. Keep hope alive in me for him the way Bosco kept it alive for boys everyone else had written off. Show me how to stay present with my son instead of only correcting him. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
