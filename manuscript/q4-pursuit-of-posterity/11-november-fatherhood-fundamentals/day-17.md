# Day 17 — The Cost of Letting Go

Tucker had the internship lined up for months — a real one, with a firm his college roommate ran, the kind of foot-in-the-door he'd spent Fiona's whole senior year arranging behind the scenes on her behalf. Then, three weeks before she was supposed to start, his nineteen-year-old daughter sat across from him at the kitchen table and told him she wanted to spend the summer at a discernment retreat instead, and that if it went the way she suspected it would, she wasn't coming back to finish her business degree at all. She wanted to enter religious formation. She'd been praying about it for over a year without telling him.

Tucker didn't yell. He asked reasonable questions — was she sure, was this really from God or from a hard breakup last spring, didn't she want to see more of the world first — and underneath every question sat the same unspoken math: he'd built a whole picture of Fiona's life, the wedding, the career, the grandkids, and she was about to walk out of that picture into a life he couldn't see the inside of and wouldn't get to visit whenever he wanted.

*Loving my daughter means keeping her on the path where I can see she'll be safe* — that was the belief underneath his questions, reasonable-sounding right up until he noticed it was really about his own need to keep tracking her.

King David prayed a different kind of prayer over his own son. Handing Solomon both the throne and the enormous, costly work of building the temple, David didn't ask God to keep him comfortable or close. He asked: "Grant to Solomon my son that with a whole heart he may keep thy commandments, thy testimonies, and thy statutes, performing all, and that he may build the palace for which I have made provision" (1 Chronicles 29:19, RSV-CE). David's highest prayer for his son was wholeheartedness — that Solomon would do the hard, specific thing God had actually given him to do, whatever it cost either of them.

**On St. Louis Martin:** Louis knew this cost directly. He was already a widower raising his daughters alone when fifteen-year-old Thérèse told him she wanted to enter Carmel — years earlier than church law normally allowed, and years earlier than any father wants to hand his youngest child, the one who ran his household after her mother's death, over to a cloistered life he would see almost nothing of again. Louis wept. He also picked a small white flower from the garden that day and gave it to her as a sign that God's hand was in it, and later walked the pilgrim road with her all the way to Rome, where she knelt in front of Pope Leo XIII and asked, in person, to be let in early. Louis didn't get an easier version of that loss. He gave his blessing anyway, because he understood that his daughter's calling belonged to God before it belonged to him.

Tucker didn't resolve anything that night at the kitchen table. He did stop asking questions designed to talk Fiona out of it, and started asking what she needed from him to take the retreat seriously.

**Today:** Name one place where you're holding a child to your plan for their life rather than asking what God's plan might actually be. Pray with them, out loud, specifically for clarity on that path — not for the outcome you'd prefer.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I confess how much of my love for my children is really a need to keep them where I can see them and manage their path. Give me Louis's kind of love — willing to bless a costly calling instead of steering my children away from it. Grant my children whole hearts for whatever You've actually given them to do. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
