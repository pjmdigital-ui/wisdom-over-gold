# Day 16 — Letting Them See You Apologize

Graham had fifteen minutes before a client call when Iris reached across the kitchen table for a marker and knocked his coffee into the legal pad he needed for the meeting. The words came out before he'd decided to say them — sharp, louder than the moment called for — and he watched his nine-year-old daughter's face fold in on itself before she carried her feet out of the room fast. He made the call. He got through it fine. He spent the rest of the afternoon replaying his own voice instead of the meeting.

By dinner, Iris was polite around him in the careful way kids get when they're managing a parent instead of just being with one. Graham knew exactly what he owed her. He also knew, with a clarity that surprised him, that he wasn't going to say it out loud — not because he didn't feel it, but because some old wiring in him treated an apology to a nine-year-old as a demotion, a crack in whatever authority he was supposed to be holding at the head of the table.

*A father who admits fault to his own kids weakens his standing with them* — nobody taught Graham that sentence directly. He'd absorbed it somewhere, the same place a lot of men absorb it, and it was running the whole evening without his permission: the softer tone at bedtime standing in for the words he wouldn't say, the assumption that sorry spoken to a child costs more than it's worth.

Scripture names the actual cost, and it runs the other direction. "He who conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy" (Proverbs 28:13, RSV-CE). Concealment is the losing move here, not the safe one. Hiding what he did behind a softer tone the next morning doesn't repair anything with Iris; it just teaches her that this is what a grown man does with his own wrongs — manages them privately and moves on. Confession is what actually obtains mercy, and mercy is what Graham wants from his daughter tonight, whether he'd put it in those words or not.

Kids don't need a father who never gets it wrong. They need one who shows them what happens after he does. Every time Graham skips the apology, Iris learns a version of manhood where being wrong is something to outlast rather than name. Every time he says the actual words — I raised my voice at you and I was wrong to do it — she learns something a lot more useful for her own life: that owning a fault out loud is strength, not the loss of it.

**Today:** Go to your child tonight and name specifically what you did wrong — no "but you shouldn't have," no folding it into a general apology for "today." Say the exact thing, and ask forgiveness out loud.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for the times I've let pride keep me from saying sorry to my own children, as if admitting fault to them costs me something I can't afford to lose. Give me the humility to confess plainly tonight, the way You've always been ready to receive it from me. Let my kids learn what repentance looks like by watching me practice it. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
