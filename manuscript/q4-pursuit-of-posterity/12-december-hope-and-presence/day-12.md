# Day 12 — Dwelling Among Them

Bowen's uncle Garrison had been in hospice for six weeks — the cancer had moved fast once it moved — and Bowen had been a model nephew from a distance. He sent flowers. He called Garrison's wife to check in and ask what they needed. He mailed a card with a real, handwritten note inside instead of a generic one from the drugstore rack. What he hadn't done was drive the forty minutes to actually sit in the room.

He had reasons that sounded fine out loud. He didn't want to intrude on family time. He wasn't sure what to say to a dying man. And if he was honest with himself, which he mostly avoided being about this particular thing, the smell of the place unsettled him the one time he'd visited, and some part of him had been managing his own comfort ever since under the cover of managing Garrison's. Cards and phone calls let him care about his uncle without having to be in the room where the disease was doing what it was doing. Love from a safe distance still counted as love, he told himself. It just didn't cost him anything.

St. John opens his Gospel with the boldest claim in Scripture about what real presence costs: "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth; we have beheld his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father" (John 1:14, RSV-CE). God didn't send a message about humanity from a comfortable distance. He became one of us — took on hunger, exhaustion, betrayal, and eventually a slow and brutal death — and dwelt among the very people He'd come to save, not above them or apart from them. The Incarnation is presence at maximum cost, on purpose, as the whole shape of how God chose to love.

**On St. Damien of Molokai:** in 1873, a Belgian priest named Damien volunteered for a leper colony on the Hawaiian island of Molokai, where the sick had been exiled to keep the disease from spreading further. He didn't visit on a schedule and leave. He moved there, built homes and a church with his own hands, ate from the same pot as the patients, and buried the dead himself when no one else would. Sixteen years in, he contracted leprosy and kept working until it killed him. He didn't manage the colony's suffering from a safe distance. Damien became one of them, the way the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and that total presence — not the medicine he lacked, not the sermons he preached — was the thing that told a colony of exiled men and women they hadn't been abandoned by God after all.

Bowen wasn't being asked to move to Molokai. He was being asked to drive forty minutes and sit in a room that smelled like sickness, for his uncle's sake and not his own comfort. He went that Sunday, sat by the bed for two hours, and said almost nothing worth remembering later. Garrison squeezed his hand once. That was the whole visit, and it was the one thing the cards had never actually delivered.

**Today:** Think of someone you've been loving from a comfortable distance — a call, a card, a check-in that costs you nothing. Go be physically present with them this week instead, even if it's uncomfortable and you don't know what to say once you're there.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've let my own comfort decide how close I'm willing to get to someone else's suffering. Give me the willingness to show up in person, even into the hard and unpleasant rooms, the way Your Son showed up for us. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
