# Introduction

If you'd asked me twenty years ago whether I'd one day write a faith-based devotional book, I would have laughed in your face. Honestly, if you'd asked me just a few years ago, I still would have laughed. It would have sounded like the most ridiculous thing I'd ever heard. I was cynical about religion for most of my life, and I was drawn instead to living on the edge — to living dangerously, which is really just a nicer way of saying I made a lot of stupid decisions.

I grew up an only child, in a good family, with two loving parents, on a dead-end block in Brooklyn. That dead end mattered more than you'd think. From the middle of the block down, all of us kids could roam free — no parent worrying we'd wander onto a main avenue and get hit by a car. This was the eighties. We were out from morning until dark, checking in with a call home from a pay phone if we remembered. My mother is one of sixteen siblings, so I have somewhere around forty cousins on her side alone, and for stretches of my childhood some of them, along with my grandparents, lived right there on the block with us. We had block parties that felt like they drew a hundred kids. There was always noise, always family, always someone around.

And still — when I went home at the end of the day, or when it was just me and my parents on a vacation somewhere, I felt alone. Different. A little out of place, even surrounded by people who loved me. I think growing up without siblings in the house meant I never learned, the way kids with brothers and sisters do, the everyday friction of sharing and arguing and making up. I didn't build those muscles early. What I built instead was a hunger for attention — and because I didn't have the normal channels for it, the ways I went looking for it were extreme. In school I was the class clown, and then something more than that: disruptive, a troublemaker.

When I was fifteen, my father had a nervous breakdown. Then another. He'd run his own business for almost thirty years, and at some point he decided to switch into something completely different. It didn't work. The failure sent him into anxiety and depression that eventually spiraled into psychotic episodes and hospitalizations. I watched all of it, and it left one lesson burned into me: I will never let money control me like that. I will never be in that position.

So when I graduated high school with no idea what I wanted to do, I at least knew what I didn't want. I didn't want a regular job. I didn't want to take orders from anybody. I wanted money, fast, and the independence I thought it would buy me. A friend offered me a job on Wall Street as a cold caller, and I took it. Within a week I understood what I'd actually walked into: an illegal boiler room, the kind you've seen in *The Wolf of Wall Street* or *Boiler Room* — because those movies were practically describing my own building. I was seventeen. I knew, and I stayed anyway, for a few months, chasing the promise of making thousands of dollars a month just talking on a phone. I wanted the fast money precisely because I'd watched what the lack of it did to my father.

Then one day it hit me: what if I called him? What if my own father picked up, and I talked him out of money he didn't have to lose? That thought is what got me out. I quit and took a regular job at a law firm in Manhattan.

Two years later, the FBI caught up to me. They knew what I'd done. They had people ready to testify. They also knew I was the lowest man on the totem pole, and they offered me a clean walk if I cooperated against the people who'd run the operation. I refused. I played games with them instead of taking the deal, and I ended up in federal prison at twenty years old — one of the two youngest inmates in the entire unit.

Here's the part that took me years to admit to myself: that experience became an identity I wore on purpose. I started almost glorifying it — the convicted felon, locked up young, out on house arrest, a story nobody else my age had. Looking back, I can see exactly what that was. It was the same hunger for attention I'd had as a kid, just wearing a more dangerous costume. Doing something nobody else has lived through gets you a kind of attention that fitting in never will, and for years I liked how that felt. What it actually built in me, underneath the story I was telling myself, was a man who had made himself the god of his own life.

That's not the same as never believing in God. My grandmother was devoutly Catholic. She took me to Saturday evening Mass with her, and she taught me to pray before I could really understand what I was praying. I've technically prayed my whole life. But the god I believed in was an idea I'd built myself — some infinite intelligence behind everything, impersonal, distant. I wasn't sure I believed the resurrection had actually happened. It felt more like a myth worth respecting than a fact worth building a life on.

For years after prison, I went looking for answers everywhere except the place I eventually found them. Meditation, chakras, energy work, the whole self-help and new-age landscape — I moved through most of it, convinced there was some sacred, hidden knowledge out there that would finally hand me the life I actually wanted. Underneath that search was a quieter belief: that I'd been living a kind of fake life, boxed in by the mistake I made at twenty, a felony and no degree closing off most of the paths a normal career would have offered. I never blamed anyone else for that. I made the decisions; I owned the consequences. But I was always looking to something other than God to fix what those decisions had cost me.

I also refused, on principle, to see myself as a victim. I took full responsibility for everything — which sounds like the healthy version of accountability, and mostly it was. But taken far enough, it flips into its own trap. The usual victim mentality blames someone else for where you've landed. Mine blamed only me, relentlessly, until the enemy in the story was just me, all the time. That produces its own weight — guilt, shame, regret — and for a long stretch of my life, that was the weight I carried, still looking everywhere except God for relief.

By then I'd developed a fairly settled opinion of Christians, especially serious ones: either eccentric zealots pushing their beliefs on everyone around them, or hypocrites — men who preached one thing on Sunday and lived the opposite the other six days. It took three things to crack that opinion open.

First, I almost died three separate times. You don't walk away from that without asking the questions that matter — why am I still here, why do some people die and not others, is there a God, and if there is, what does He actually want from me.

Second, I found myself in a business venture where I met in person with the same group three times a year, most of them Christian. After enough time around them, I couldn't square what I was seeing with the caricature I'd been carrying. They weren't hypocrites. They weren't lunatics. They were genuine, funny, down-to-earth, successful, good people who happened to take their faith seriously — and living, ordinary proof that my picture of Christianity had been wrong.

I believe now that was no accident. I think the Holy Spirit placed me in that room on purpose — put me in front of the same faithful, ordinary, successful people three times a year, year after year, specifically to dismantle a caricature I never would have taken apart on my own. One of those men, Todd Brown, became a mentor to me — someone I could relate to on business and on life, who also turned out to be far ahead of me not just in building companies but in scripture and Catholic apologetics. Watching a man I already respected for entirely secular reasons take his faith that seriously did more to move me than any argument could have.

Third, I came to a plain conclusion that we are living inside real spiritual warfare, whether anyone acknowledges it or not, and that a man has to decide which side of that he's actually standing on. Not someday. Now.

So I started doing my own research, in earnest, for the first time in my life. First: is there a God, an actual intelligent designer behind any of this? I became convinced there was. Then: which God? I watched debate after debate, read what I could, and the road kept leading back to the same place — the Catholic Church, the faith my grandmother had quietly planted in me on all those Saturday nights I hadn't thought much about since.

Since I let that faith take root for real, God has been at work in my life in ways I can't explain away. I feel His presence. I've watched doors open that had no business opening. When I pray for direction, I get it — not always fast, not always the way I'd script it, but real.

Looking back now, I can see something I couldn't see at the time: God never actually left me alone in any of it, even in the decades I didn't believe in Him as a personal God. He gave me two genuinely loving parents. He surrounded me with real friends. When I was locked up at twenty, there were people inside who looked out for me and protected me, with no reason they had to. Twenty years later, when I had a heart attack at forty, my wife called 911 at exactly the right moment — off nothing more than the sound of my voice on the phone, a sound that, twenty years earlier, would never have told her anything was wrong. I don't think that was luck. I think it was the same hand that had been placing the right people around me my whole life, whether I had the eyes to see it or not.

Somewhere in that season I discovered devotional books — first *Jesus Calling*, after hearing about it from Sean Ryan, and then a men's devotional my wife bought me, sensing where I was headed before I fully did. I'm a businessman by nature, always looking for where I can add value, and it wasn't long before the idea took hold: what if I built the devotional I actually needed — for men like me, middle-aged Catholic men trying to be the best father, husband, neighbor, friend, businessman, and Christian we can be? That's what you're holding.

What that search taught me is that living out my faith well comes down to four pursuits, and I'm never finished with any of them — I'm just always in them.

The first is the **pursuit of Piety** — seeking God first, ordering my life around Him instead of myself. Scripture tells us to seek first the Kingdom, so everything I do now gets weighed against one question: does this please and glorify Him? Is this what He made me for? Is this what His Word says will honor Him? That question runs underneath every other one.

The second is the **pursuit of Protection**. Not just my wife and children, though obviously them first. I'm a black belt in Brazilian jiu-jitsu, and I teach kids — which means I'm both protecting them, by making sure what I teach them actually keeps them safe, and providing for them, by giving them something real. I owe that same protection to the people who trust me enough to buy from me or listen to me.

The third is the **pursuit of Provision** — providing real value, real security, a good life for my family, real solutions for the people who depend on me, and yes, for myself too.

The fourth is the **pursuit of Posterity** — legacy in the true sense, the work of building something that outlasts me and leaves the world better for having had me in it.

That's the whole shape of this book. Four pursuits, one foundation underneath all of them — because none of the other three hold up if the first one isn't actually in place. I spent most of my life proving that the hard way: chasing protection, provision, and a name for myself while quietly skipping the one pursuit everything else depends on. I know exactly how far that gets a man. It gets him a life that looks finished from the outside and feels hollow everywhere it actually counts.

I'm not writing to you from a mountaintop, and I'm not finished becoming any of these four things — nobody is, this side of heaven. I still catch myself, more often than I'd like, quietly climbing back onto the throne of my own life. Noticing that, and handing it back to Him, isn't something you do once. It's the whole game, every day, for the rest of your life. That's why this book runs 365 days instead of twelve chapters. Seeking first isn't an achievement you unlock. It's a direction you keep choosing, one morning at a time.

I hope this book helps you the way discovering this same truth has helped me. Let's seek Him first, together, starting today.
