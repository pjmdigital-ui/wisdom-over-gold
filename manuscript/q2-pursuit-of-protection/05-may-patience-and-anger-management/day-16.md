# Day 16 — Anger in Marriage

It started over a calendar mix-up — Roland had told his wife he'd handle picking up their daughter from practice, then forgot, and she'd had to leave a work call early to cover for him. By the time he walked in the door that evening, the conversation had already stopped being about the calendar. She brought up a pattern; he brought up a night three months earlier she still hadn't let go of. She raised her voice; he raised his back, half a step higher, like there was a ceiling somewhere above them and whoever reached it first would finally be heard. Doors got closed harder than they needed to be. He slept on the couch, less from anywhere to sleep than as a statement.

Lying there, Roland replayed his own lines and noticed something: nearly every one of them had been aimed at winning the exchange rather than resolving it — a sharper comeback, a fact she couldn't argue with, an old grievance produced at exactly the moment it would land hardest. He hadn't been trying to fix the calendar problem for the last twenty minutes of that fight. He'd been trying not to lose.

St. Paul names the whole inventory Roland had just run through, item by item, and calls for its removal without qualification: "Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, with all malice" (Ephesians 4:31, RSV-CE). Clamor is the raised voice reaching for the ceiling. Slander is the old grievance, produced as a weapon instead of a topic for actual repair. Malice is the intent underneath both — not a hurt feeling, but a wish to land a blow. Read against Roland's night, the verse isn't abstract; it's a direct inventory of what had just happened in his kitchen, named one item at a time.

The next verse is the part a man in the middle of a fight rarely wants to hear, because it doesn't wait for the other person to go first: "and be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ forgave you" (Ephesians 4:32, RSV-CE). Kindness in that verse isn't conditional on who's more right about the calendar. It's modeled on a forgiveness that was extended before either spouse had earned anything.

Roland's forgotten pickup was a small failure any marriage absorbs constantly. What escalated it wasn't the mistake. It was two people, including him, treating the conversation as a contest with a winner, instead of two people who'd both messed up trying to actually repair something together. A marriage doesn't survive on who wins the argument. It survives on which spouse is willing to stop trying to.

**Today:** Recall the last fight with your wife where you were reaching for the win instead of the repair. Say plainly, to her if you can: "I wasn't trying to fix this — I was trying not to lose. I'm sorry." Have the actual conversation about what still needs resolving, without keeping score this time.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've turned conversations with my wife into contests I was determined to win, and called the wreckage her fault as much as mine. Put away the bitterness and clamor in me, and give me the kindness You showed me before I ever deserved it. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
