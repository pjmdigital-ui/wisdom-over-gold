# Day 20 — The Brotherhood That Costs Time

Corey's father died on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning Blake had rearranged two client calls, driven four hours, and was sitting in Corey's kitchen doing nothing in particular — making coffee, taking out the trash, answering the door when the casseroles started arriving. He hadn't asked Corey if he wanted company. He'd just shown up with a bag packed for three days and let Corey decide later whether that was too much.

Blake almost didn't go. He ran the math in the car before he left: the missed calls, the rescheduled meeting he'd been trying to land for weeks, the fact that Corey hadn't actually asked him to come. It would have been easy to send a card and a nice text instead. Nobody would have faulted him for it.

The belief that nearly kept Blake home is common enough to be almost invisible: *real support means saying the right thing, and I can do that from a distance just as well.* That belief is comfortable because it's true just often enough to justify itself. A phone call helps. A card helps. But there's a category of grief that doesn't need the right words at all — it needs a body in the room, and the calendar sacrifice a body in the room requires.

Scripture doesn't dress this up as anything more complicated than presence. When Job's three friends heard what had happened to him, "they made an appointment together to come to condole with him and comfort him. And when they saw him from afar, they did not recognize him... and they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his suffering was very great" (Job 2:11-13, RSV-CE). Seven days. No script, no wisdom offered yet, no attempt to fix what couldn't be fixed that fast. Just three men who cleared their calendars and sat on the ground. That's the whole model. It cost them a week each, and the text doesn't record a single word they said in it — because the point wasn't what they said. It was that they came, and stayed.

Most men are better at the coming than the staying. Showing up once is a gesture; it fits neatly into a day already planned around something else. Staying — through the second day, when the casseroles have stopped and the house is quiet and there's genuinely nothing productive left to do — is where the cost shows up. That's also where it actually matters, because that's when the man you're sitting with finds out whether you were there for the story or for him.

Blake's four-hour drive wasn't the sacrifice. The three days after it were.

**Today:** Think of one man in your life who's going through something hard right now. Instead of sending a message that says "let me know if you need anything," block actual time on your calendar — an evening, an afternoon, whatever you can give — and tell him plainly: "I'm not waiting for you to ask. I'm coming Thursday, and I'm not bringing an agenda."

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for the times I've offered words when a man needed my presence instead. Give me the courage to clear my calendar for a brother the way Job's friends did — without waiting to be asked, without rushing to fix what only needs sitting with. Teach me that showing up is its own form of love. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
