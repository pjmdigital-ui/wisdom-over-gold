# Day 10 — Paul's Traveling Companions

Barrett ran the numbers on his ministry's outreach program for the third time that week, alone in his office after everyone else had gone home. He'd started the program himself two years earlier, and somewhere in those two years "started it himself" had quietly turned into "runs it entirely by himself" — the volunteer schedule, the budget, the Sunday planning, the late-night calls with families in crisis. He told people he liked it that way. Fewer moving parts, fewer people to disappoint him.

What he didn't say out loud was that he'd stopped asking for help a long time ago, and somewhere along the way that had started to feel like proof he was doing the work right. If he needed a team to hold this together, maybe it meant he wasn't cut out to lead it. Better to burn out alone than admit the program needed more hands than his.

That's a belief worth naming directly, because men in ministry and men in business carry the same version of it: real capability looks like carrying the load without help, and asking for hands means the load was too big for you to begin with.

St. Paul never operated that way, and he had a claim to self-sufficiency that dwarfs anything Barrett could argue for himself — he'd planted churches across the Roman world, survived shipwrecks and beatings, written a third of the New Testament. Near the end of his life, writing his last letter from a Roman prison, awaiting execution, Paul still named the men around him by name: "Luke alone is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you; for he is very useful in serving me" (2 Timothy 4:11, RSV-CE). Even in his final letter, chained and alone in a cell, Paul was still asking Timothy to send him a companion. He didn't treat that request as a confession of weakness. He treated it as ordinary — the way ministry had always worked for him, from his very first missionary travels with Barnabas onward. Silas, Timothy, Luke, Titus, Priscilla and Aquila, Mark — Paul's letters read like a running list of men and women he did the work alongside, not a solo record with occasional footnotes.

If the apostle who wrote most of the New Testament needed Luke in the room and asked for Mark by name, the man running a single outreach program out of a church office doesn't have a stronger case for going it alone. Barrett's instinct to read his isolation as competence has the story backward. Paul's competence never required isolation. His effectiveness ran directly through the people beside him — he named them because they mattered to the work, not despite it.

The men who actually finish hard, long-term work are rarely the ones who did it solo. They're the ones who kept a Luke close and weren't ashamed to ask for a Mark.

**Today:** Write down the name of one person you've been doing a hard thing alone that you could actually ask to help. Say it plainly: "Needing someone here isn't proof I'm failing — it's how this kind of work has always gotten done." Then send the message asking.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I have mistaken carrying everything alone for strength more times than I can count. Give me Paul's willingness to name the people he needed and ask for them plainly. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
