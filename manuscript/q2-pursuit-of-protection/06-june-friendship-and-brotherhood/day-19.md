# Day 19 — Receiving Correction Without Getting Defensive

"You cut your wife off three times at dinner tonight, and I don't think you noticed you were doing it." Vincent's friend said it low, on the walk back to the cars after the couples' cookout, no audience, no edge in his voice. Vincent felt his chest tighten before the sentence even finished landing.

He had a response loaded before he'd thought about whether it was true — something about being tired, about the conversation moving fast, about how nobody else at the table seemed bothered. He caught himself mid-sentence, still talking, already three excuses deep, and realized he hadn't actually considered for one second whether his friend was right.

That's the reflex worth naming honestly: the belief that *if I explain myself well enough, I don't have to change.* Defensiveness rarely announces itself as defensiveness. It shows up dressed as context, as nuance, as "let me just clarify what actually happened" — a man building a case instead of examining one. The tighter the explanation, the less room there is left to actually hear what was said.

Proverbs describes what correction is supposed to feel like when it's received the way it's meant to be given: "Like a gold ring or an ornament of gold is a wise reprover to a listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12, RSV-CE). Gold, not gravel. The verse assumes correction is valuable — a thing worth wearing, not a wound to be defended against — but it puts the weight on the second half just as much as the first. A wise reprover is only an ornament to a listening ear. The gold doesn't do anything for a man whose ear isn't open. Vincent's friend brought the gold. Whether it became worth anything depended entirely on what Vincent did in the next thirty seconds.

Here's what's actually hard about that verse: it requires believing the correction might be gold before you've verified it's not an insult. Most men do the verifying first — turn the comment over, look for the flaw in the friend's delivery or timing or standing to say it, and only consider the substance if it survives that filter. Flip the order. Assume, for the first sixty seconds, that a friend who cared enough to say something uncomfortable at a cookout instead of just filing it away probably saw something real. You can still disagree after that. Just do the listening first.

Vincent didn't finish his excuse. He stopped mid-sentence and said, "You're right. I do that when I'm stressed. Thank you for telling me instead of letting it go."

**Today:** The next time a friend corrects you, resist your first three sentences of explanation and say instead, out loud: "Let me actually sit with that before I respond." Then tell him: "I'd rather hear this from you than not hear it at all — that's gold, not an attack." Wait a full hour before deciding whether you agree.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, quiet the part of me that starts building a defense before I've even heard the whole sentence. Teach me to receive correction from a friend as a gift instead of a threat. Give me the humility to say "you're right" when it's true. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
