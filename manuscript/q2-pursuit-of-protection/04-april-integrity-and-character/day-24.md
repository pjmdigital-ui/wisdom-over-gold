# Day 24 — Afraid of What Honesty Will Cost

Frank had rehearsed the sentence in his truck for twenty minutes before he ever walked into his boss's office: *I made an error on the Hendricks estimate, and it's going to cost us the margin on the job.* Nobody else had caught it. The client hadn't caught it. His boss was three weeks from finalizing next year's regional assignments, and everyone in the office knew Frank was the leading name for the promotion. He could let the estimate ride, eat the loss quietly out of a different line item, and no one would ever trace it back to a decimal he'd fat-fingered on a Friday afternoon.

He sat in the parking lot instead, engine off, staring at the office door. It wasn't the error that had him frozen. It was picturing his boss's face when he heard it, and picturing that face again three weeks later, reading a promotion list with Frank's name conspicuously absent from it.

The belief was simple, and Frank could have said it in so many words if anyone had asked him directly: *if I tell the truth here, it will cost me something I can't afford to lose.* He wasn't wrong that it might cost him. That was the whole problem — he'd let the possible cost become the whole decision, the only variable that mattered, bigger in his mind than the mistake itself, bigger than the client, bigger than what kind of man walks through that door and what kind doesn't.

Scripture doesn't pretend the fear isn't real. It names it and then names what actually happens to a man who lets it drive: "The fear of man lays a snare, but he who trusts in the LORD is safe" (Proverbs 29:25, RSV-CE). A snare doesn't announce itself as a trap. It looks, right up until the moment it closes, like the safer path — the quieter option, the one that protects what you've built. Fear of man is precisely that kind of trap: it promises safety and delivers a smaller, more compromised version of you, permanently bent around what other people might think or do.

Frank had spent the whole drive doubting his own courage. What he hadn't done was ask the more useful question: what happens to a man who makes every hard call by first calculating what it'll cost him personally? That man doesn't stay trustworthy for long, promotion or not. He becomes someone whose honesty is conditional — reliable exactly up to the point where reliability gets expensive. Nobody promotes that man either, not really; they just haven't found the expensive moment yet.

The trade being offered here isn't between honesty and safety. It's between two different masters. A man who trusts God with the outcome can walk into that office and tell the truth without knowing what it costs him, because his safety was never actually riding on his boss's opinion in the first place.

**Today:** Before you speak the truth you've been avoiding, say to yourself: *I don't control what this costs me, and I trust God with that outcome — this is still the right thing to do.* Then say the actual hard sentence, today, to the person who needs to hear it — not a softened version, not next week's version.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, You know what I've been putting off saying and what I'm afraid it will cost me. Take that fear out of the driver's seat and remind me that You hold what happens next, not the person I'm afraid of disappointing. Give me the nerve to speak plainly today. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
