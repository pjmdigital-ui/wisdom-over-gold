# Day 11 — When the Business Fails

Perry unlocked Reyes Hardware for the last time on a Tuesday morning, not to open it, but to pull the inventory tags off shelves he'd stocked for seventeen years before handing the keys back to the landlord. He'd watched the numbers slide for two years — a national chain opened nine miles up the highway with prices he could never match, and the contractors who used to call him by name started ordering online instead. He'd cut his own pay before he cut anyone else's hours, refinanced the house once, and still watched the math run out anyway.

Standing in the empty aisle where the fasteners used to be, Perry didn't feel like a man who'd made a bad business decision. He felt like a man whose worth had just been repossessed along with the shelving.

That's the belief hiding under a failure like this, and it rarely announces itself so plainly: *what I build is what I'm worth — if the business goes, so do I.* A man can spend seventeen years proving himself competent, generous, and honest, and still walk out believing all of that vanished the moment the doors locked, because he'd let the business become the whole scoreboard.

St. Paul wrote from a position that makes Perry's morning look survivable — chained, awaiting trial, dependent on a church's gift just to eat — and he refused to let his circumstances set his worth. "Not that I complain of want; for I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content. I know how to be abased, and I know how to abound; in any and all circumstances I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and want. I can do all things in him who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:11-13, RSV-CE). Paul isn't describing a talent he was born with. He calls it something learned — a secret acquired the hard way, through actually being abased and actually abounding, not through avoiding both.

Perry's seventeen years weren't erased by the closing. He taught himself the trade, kept every promise he made to a supplier even in the leanest year, and put two kids through school on what that store earned. None of that requires the business to still be open to remain true. What he's being asked to learn now — the part that doesn't come naturally to a man who ties his identity to what he's built — is that his worth was never the store's to hold in the first place.

**Today:** Name one thing you built, earned, or provided that remains true regardless of what happens to your current job, business, or income. Say it out loud: "My worth isn't the scoreboard I built — I can be content abased or abounding, because the One who strengthens me isn't the market."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I have let what I build stand in for who I am, and now I don't know who I am without it. Teach me the contentment Paul learned in want and in plenty. Strengthen me to keep going, not because the business survived, but because You have not let go of me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
