# Day 29 — Starting Over, Again

Twenty-eight days into this month of trying to put God first, and you snapped at your wife over something that didn't deserve it — a dish left in the sink, a schedule mix-up, something so small you can barely justify the tone you used. Afterward, sitting in your truck in the garage because you needed a minute before going back inside, the thought that actually stung wasn't about the argument. It was: *I should be past this by now.* Twenty-eight days of trying, and here you are again, same old pattern, same old you.

That thought is a liar, and it's worth naming exactly what kind of lie it is. It tells you that growth is supposed to be a straight line — that if the month has been real, you should be measurably, permanently different by day twenty-nine, immune by now to the impatience and pride that used to run you. When you're not, it tells you the whole month was fake. You weren't actually changing. You were just performing for a few weeks and now the real you is leaking back through.

Scripture doesn't describe growth that way anywhere, and it definitely doesn't describe mercy that way. Jeremiah, writing in the middle of watching his own city fall apart, in a book that is largely one long lament, still lands here: "The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" (Lamentations 3:22-23). Read where that verse sits — surrounded by grief, not triumph. Jeremiah isn't reporting that he's finally arrived. He's reporting that God's mercy resets every single morning regardless of what kind of man showed up the day before.

That's not a loophole for staying the same. It's the actual mechanism by which anyone ever changes. Nobody grows in a straight line — not the men who seem to have it together, not the saints whose polished stories you read after they were dead and their worst days had been edited out. Growth is a mess of relapse and return, relapse and return, and the return is not evidence that the growth was fake. The return *is* the growth. A man who falls back into an old pattern on day twenty-eight and gets back up on day twenty-nine is not failing at this. He's doing exactly what putting God first actually looks like over a lifetime — not a permanent state you reach and hold, but a direction you keep choosing, one reset morning at a time.

You don't need a better twenty-eight days behind you. You need a willingness to start, again, this morning — which, mercifully, is precisely what's on offer.

**Today:** Go back to whoever you snapped at, wronged, or let down recently and say it plainly: "I was wrong, and I'm sorry." No explanation, no justification — just the two sentences. Then let today actually be new.
