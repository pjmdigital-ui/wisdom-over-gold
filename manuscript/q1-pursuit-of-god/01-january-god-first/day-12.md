# Day 12 — What You Worship, You Become

You'd known Dave since college — good guy, generous with a beer in his hand, the kind of friend who'd drive two hours to help you move. Over the last decade you watched him build a real estate portfolio that made your own finances look like a lemonade stand, and somewhere in there something in him changed. It wasn't sudden. It was the way every conversation eventually turned to square footage or comps or the guy he'd just outbid on a deal. It was the new truck, then the newer truck. It was the way he'd glance at his watch during his daughter's dance recital, and the way he introduced himself at parties now with his net worth practically embedded in his job title.

You had dinner with him last month and realized, driving home, that you couldn't remember the last time he'd asked you a question about your life that wasn't secretly about his. He hadn't become a bad man. He'd become a smaller one — sharper on the metrics that mattered to him, dull to almost everything else. And the uncomfortable part wasn't judging Dave. It was recognizing the shape of it, because you'd felt that same pull in your own chest more than once.

The Psalmist describes idols this way: "They have mouths, but do not speak; eyes, but do not see... Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (Psalm 115:5, 8). That's not a metaphor about ancient statues. It's a law of the human heart. Whatever you fix your attention and hope on, you start to resemble. Worship money and status long enough and you get sharper elbows and a duller soul — not because money is evil, but because it was never built to hold the weight you're asking it to carry.

That's the quiet danger of watching a friend go this direction — it's not really about Dave. It's the mirror. Every man has a Dave-shaped temptation somewhere in his own ambition, the version of himself that would emerge if he let the wrong thing sit on the throne long enough. Seeing it clearly in a friend is a mercy, if you let it be one — a chance to ask what you're actually becoming, not just what you're accumulating.

**On St. Francis of Assisi:** Born into wealth as the son of a prosperous cloth merchant, Francis was expected to inherit both the family business and its status. Instead, he famously stripped off his fine clothes in the town square and returned them to his father, choosing poverty and total dependence on God over the life laid out for him. He didn't become less of a man by giving up what he'd been given first place in his life. He became more himself.

**Today:** Think of one person whose life quietly unsettles you because you envy it. Name, specifically, what they've made an idol of — then ask whether you're chasing the same thing at a smaller scale. Write it down.
