# Day 26 — The Suffering That Isn't Yours to Carry

Elton's younger brother Kevin has called him for money four times in three years — rent, a truck repair, a "short-term" loan to cover payroll for a landscaping business that's failed twice already. Elton always says yes. He tells his wife it's what family does, that someone has to be the one who holds things together, and lately he's started saying it to himself too, usually around midnight when he's redoing the math on what this month's transfer means for their own savings.

Last week Kevin called again. Before Elton even heard the ask, he was already running numbers in his head, already deciding which account to pull from, already rehearsing the sentence he'd say to his wife. He didn't ask himself whether he should say yes. He asked himself how.

Someone has to fix this — that's the sentence Elton's been running on, and it sounds like love. It sounds like sacrifice, the exact thing this whole month has been asking of him. But there's a difference between carrying what's genuinely his to carry and carrying what someone else needs to be allowed to carry himself, and Elton hasn't let Kevin find that difference out even once in three years.

St. Paul writes both halves of this tension in the same short letter, four verses apart. "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2, RSV-CE) — real, and this book has already leaned on that verse once this month, when the weight belongs to both men. But three verses later, in the same breath: "For each man will have to bear his own load" (Galatians 6:5, RSV-CE). Paul isn't contradicting himself. A burden is what crushes a man alone — grief, illness, a blow he didn't choose — and that kind gets shared. A load is what's his to carry as part of walking his own road: his choices, his consequences, his growth. Kevin's landscaping business, run twice into the ground the same way, is a load. Elton's been carrying it as if it were a burden, and every time he does, Kevin gets to skip the part where carrying his own load might actually teach him something.

Overfunctioning wears the costume of sacrifice convincingly enough to fool the man doing it. It feels selfless. Underneath, it's often a way of staying in control — of Kevin's outcome, of the story where Elton is the responsible one and Kevin never has to be.

**Today:** The next time Kevin — or whoever your version of Kevin is — calls with a load that's his, not yours, say it plainly before you say anything else: *I love you, and this one is yours to carry.* Then let the silence after that sentence sit there instead of filling it with a solution.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've been carrying weight that was never mine to carry, and calling it love. Show me the difference between a burden I'm meant to help shoulder and a load someone else needs to carry for his own sake. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
