# Day 1 — The Instinct to Avoid

Every Saturday morning, Dave pulls the mower out of the garage and cuts the yard in the same pattern — back forty first, then the side yard, then the strip along the front walk. He never touches the eight feet of grass along the fence line he shares with the Petersons. That's where, six months ago, his neighbor cornered him about a property-line dispute that got heated fast, both men saying things they shouldn't have. Since then Dave mows everywhere except that strip. He tells himself he'll get to it "once things cool down." The grass there is nearly two feet tall now, gone to seed, visible from the street.

He isn't lying to himself about why. He knows exactly what happened and exactly what needs cutting. What he hasn't looked at is the pattern underneath it: a fence, a phone call he owes his brother, a conversation with his oldest about grades that keeps sliding to "this weekend." Small, separate problems getting the same treatment. He routes around whatever's uncomfortable and calls the rest of the yard handled.

It isn't laziness. Dave works hard — he'll spend three hours edging and blowing off the driveway rather than spend three minutes at that fence line. The avoidance takes effort. It's a skill he's gotten good at without ever deciding to practice it.

Underneath all of it sits one belief, unexamined: if he can just route around the hard thing long enough, it stops being his problem. Not that it gets solved — that it stops mattering, worn down by enough time and enough distance. Comfort will finish the job discomfort was supposed to do.

Scripture says almost the opposite. James writes to men scattered under real pressure, and he doesn't tell them to manage their way around trials. "Count it all joy, my brethren, when you meet various trials, for you know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness. And let steadfastness have its full effect, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing" (James 1:2-4, RSV-CE). Joy is a strange word to set next to trial. James isn't describing a feeling a man manufactures on command — he's describing what happens when someone walks straight into the hard thing instead of around it. The trial is doing something. Route around it, and you route around what it was building in you.

The grass by the fence was never really the problem. It's evidence. Every strip a man leaves uncut — every call he doesn't make, every conversation he keeps rescheduling — is a piece of his life left un-lived. It doesn't vanish. It just grows taller until the whole yard shows it.

**Today:** Name one specific thing you've been mowing around this week — a call, a conversation, a task you keep reclassifying as "not urgent." Say out loud: *Avoiding this doesn't make it smaller. Walking into it is how it finally gets finished.* Then do the smallest possible piece of it before the day ends — make the call, write the first sentence, spend five minutes at the fence line.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, You see exactly what I've been routing around this week, even when I've dressed it up as being busy with other things. Give me the courage to walk toward the hard thing instead of past it, and let me trust that You are working in me through it in a way that avoidance never could. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
