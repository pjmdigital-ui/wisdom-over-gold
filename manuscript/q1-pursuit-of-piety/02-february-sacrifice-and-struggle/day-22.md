# Day 22 — Self-Pity Disguised as Reflection

Marcus lost the Hutchins account on a Tuesday afternoon — six years of steady work, gone to a competitor bidding half his rate. That night he told himself he was going to sit with it, process it like a grown man instead of stuffing it down. Instead he opened his laptop and reread the termination email four times, then dug up the original proposal from six years back and read that too, building a case file for how unfair this was.

By ten he'd texted the same story to three different friends, each version a little more wronged than the last. His wife asked if he wanted to talk about it. He told her he already was, plenty. He went to bed running the client's exact words through his head again, not to understand them — just to feel the sting one more time before he fell asleep.

Two days later, nothing had changed except how many times he'd told the story.

Marcus called what he was doing "processing." It wasn't. Processing a loss moves somewhere — toward acceptance, toward a lesson, toward the next call he needs to make. What Marcus was doing stayed in one place and dug the same hole deeper, and the deeper it got, the more it felt like insight instead of what it actually was: a man rehearsing his own wound because rehearsing it felt like doing something.

St. Paul draws the line Marcus was standing on without knowing it: "godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret, but worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10, RSV-CE). Both kinds of grief feel like grief from the inside — same tight chest, same replay loop. The difference shows up in where they lead. Godly grief moves a man toward something: humility, a changed decision, a harder conversation he's been avoiding. Worldly grief just circles, and Marcus's four rereads and three retellings were circling, not moving.

There's also a version of this that flatters a man. Circling his own wound lets him stay the main character — the wronged party, the one everyone should feel bad for. It's a strange kind of relief, feeling sorry for yourself, because it puts you back at the center of the story instead of putting God there, which is the whole shift this book has been asking of you since January.

Your List of Truths page exists for nights exactly like this — not because rereading a list fixes a lost account, but because a man staring at one loss on repeat needs reminding of everything that's still true and good, not just the one thing that went wrong.

**Today:** Write down what actually happened in one flat sentence — no adjectives, no "unfair," just the fact. Then say out loud: *Feeling this doesn't mean I'm dealing with it. Dealing with it means I move.* Pull out your List of Truths page — or start one if you haven't yet — and read three lines from it before you pick up your phone to tell the story again.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I know how to relive a wound and I still call it healing. Teach me the difference between grief that moves me toward You and grief that just keeps me circling the same hurt. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
