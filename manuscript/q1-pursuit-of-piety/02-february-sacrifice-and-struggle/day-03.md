# Day 3 — Numbing the Ache

The parent-teacher conference ran fourteen minutes. Long enough for Chris to hear that his son is failing two classes, that his teachers think he's "checked out," that this has apparently been building for a month and nobody at home noticed. Chris drove home with the radio off, said almost nothing at dinner, and by eight o'clock was sitting in his truck in the driveway with the engine still ticking, phone in his hand.

He tells himself he's just checking the score. Forty minutes later he's still there — stats, highlights, a group chat argument about a trade that doesn't matter to him at all — engine off, porch light on, wife inside probably wondering where he is. This isn't the first night this month it's gone this way. Some nights it's the phone. Some nights it's two beers that turn into four once the kids are down. Different door, same room: anywhere that isn't the conversation he actually needs to have, with his son and with himself.

What is he actually trying to not feel? Not the failing grades themselves — those are just numbers, fixable with a tutor and a schedule. What he's avoiding is the harder read underneath them: that his son has been quietly struggling for a month and Chris was too distracted, too tired, or too checked out himself to catch it. That's not a problem a screen can solve. It's not even a problem two beers can soften for long. It's just a problem he doesn't want to hold.

Peter writes to Christians who have real reason to want out of what they're facing, and he doesn't tell them the pain is a mistake to be managed around. "Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery ordeal which comes upon you to prove you, as though something strange were happening to you" (1 Peter 4:12, RSV-CE). Not strange. Not a glitch in an otherwise well-run life. The ordeal is treated as normal — expected, even — because it's doing something a comfortable life never could: proving what's actually in a man, under pressure, when nobody's watching him perform.

Every hour Chris spends in the truck is an hour he's not proving anything, one way or the other. He's just postponing the proof. The ache doesn't go anywhere while he scrolls — it's still exactly where he left it, waiting on the other side of the front door, a little heavier for having been carried alone a while longer.

**Today:** Notice the next time you reach for a screen, a drink, or any other reliable exit the moment something hard surfaces. Before you take it, name what you're actually avoiding, and say: *This ache is proving something in me. Numbing it doesn't remove it — it just delays what I have to face.* Then go do the harder thing instead — walk back inside, start the conversation, sit with it for ten minutes without reaching for the exit.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I know the places I go to avoid feeling what's actually in front of me, and I know they don't make the ache smaller. Give me the strength to walk back into the hard conversation instead of away from it, trusting that You're refining something in me I can't see yet. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
