# Day 21 — Forgiveness Is a Sacrifice

His business partner of nine years had cashed out early, lining up the buyer for six months behind his back before he ever mentioned it, and left Steve holding a company worth half of what it should have been sold for — with a check that reflected the old number, not the real one. Legal said there wasn't much of a case. Steve read the settlement letter twice, then put it in a drawer, where it had stayed for two years.

He didn't think about it every day anymore. But it surfaced whenever the guy's name came up at a conference, or a mutual friend mentioned he was doing well, and each time it surfaced Steve noticed the same tight feeling: a debt that was owed and had never been paid. He'd catch himself rehearsing the story to whoever would listen, watching their face for the outrage that told him he'd been right to stay angry. Somebody keeps score, even if it's not him. He'd decided, without ever deciding out loud, that forgiving the man would mean agreeing the debt didn't exist — that what happened was fine.

That's the belief worth naming directly: forgiveness feels like it erases the wrong, so withholding it feels like the last way left to insist the wrong was real. A man protects his sense of justice by refusing to let go, as if his grip is what's keeping the scales honest.

St. Paul tells the Colossians something that doesn't ask a man to pretend the wrong wasn't a wrong: "forbearing one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other; as the Lord has forgiven you, so you also must forgive" (Colossians 3:13, RSV-CE). Paul assumes the complaint is legitimate — he doesn't tell them to stop having grievances. He tells them to forgive anyway, on the same terms Christ forgave them: not because the debt was small, but despite it being real. The measure isn't the size of the wrong. It's the size of the mercy already extended to the man doing the forgiving.

Steve's partner never apologized and probably never will. Forgiveness was never going to come from him settling the account. It was going to cost Steve something — the grip, the tight feeling, the last bit of leverage that came from staying owed.

**Today:** Name the person you're still keeping a private account against, and say the sentence out loud, even if he never hears it: "I forgive you, and I'm not waiting for you to earn it." Then do one thing today that matches it — delete the old email thread, stop rehearsing the story to the next person who asks, or pray for him by name once.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, You have forgiven me far more than I've ever had to forgive anyone else. Give me the strength to release the debt I've been holding, even though it's real and even though it costs me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
