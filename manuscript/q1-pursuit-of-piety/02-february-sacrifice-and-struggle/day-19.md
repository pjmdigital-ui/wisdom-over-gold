# Day 19 — Physical Suffering as Discipline

Mile four of a five-mile run, legs heavy, lungs burning, and Joe's watch buzzed with the easiest permission in the world: a text from his own brain telling him nobody would know if he cut it short at the corner up ahead. Nobody would. He'd still post the same number of runs this month. He slowed to a walk before he'd even decided to, like his body had voted without asking him.

He'd done the same thing three weeks earlier with a Friday fast he'd committed to for Lent prep — made it to two in the afternoon, told himself a headache this bad wasn't holy, it was just unnecessary, and ate. Both times the reasoning sounded almost wise: *my body's signal to stop is the same thing as God's signal to stop.* Discomfort and disobedience felt identical from inside them, so he treated every ache like a stop sign instead of asking whether it was actually one.

That's a comfortable belief to hold because it never asks anything of you. It also isn't true. The body sends plenty of signals that exist purely to get you to quit before you have to, and a man who obeys every one of them by reflex will never do anything that costs him — in training, in fasting, or anywhere else that matters.

St. Paul, a man who ran actual physical races and used the metaphor on purpose, describes what he does with his own body in blunt terms: "but I pommel my body and subdue it, lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified" (1 Corinthians 9:27, RSV-CE). Pommel — not pamper, not negotiate with. Paul isn't describing a wellness routine. He's describing a fight he expects to have with his own body every day of his life, and he doesn't plan on losing it out of politeness toward his own discomfort.

Nobody's asking Joe to chase pain for its own sake. The question underneath the miles and the fasting days is simpler and harder: whether his legs and his stomach get to make his decisions for him, or whether he trains them — the way you'd train anything else you intend to rely on — to keep going past the point where quitting first became available.

**Today:** Pick one thing today — a workout, a fast, a task you've been putting off because it's uncomfortable — and go five minutes past the exact point where your body first asks you to stop. Before you do, say it out loud: "My body doesn't get the final vote." Then finish the five minutes.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, give me the will to subdue my body instead of obeying its every request to quit. Let today's discomfort be an offering to You, not just an inconvenience I avoid. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
