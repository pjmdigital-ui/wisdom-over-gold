# Day 23 — The Pride That Blocks the Door

Your business partner said it plainly in the meeting: "The numbers you gave the investors were optimistic. We both know that." He was right. You'd rounded the projections up, told yourself it was just confidence, just how the game is played, and now the actual quarter had landed a full twelve percent under what you'd promised. Instead of saying so, you defended the number for ten straight minutes — pointed at market conditions, at timing, at anything that wasn't your own decision to inflate the pitch. You watched his face change, watched him stop pushing back not because he agreed, but because he'd realized arguing with you here was pointless.

You knew, driving home, that you owed him a real apology, not the kind that's actually an explanation in disguise. You didn't call. Three days later you still hadn't. The voice running underneath the silence was plain enough, if you'd said it out loud: *Admitting I was wrong costs too much right now.* His respect, maybe. Your own sense of being the guy who's got it handled.

Your wife has heard some version of this from you before, on a smaller scale. The dishes conversation that turns into you defending a tone you know was wrong. The plans you made without asking her that you justify instead of simply owning. It's the same muscle both times, flexed in the office and in the kitchen: the refusal to just say, plainly, I was wrong, because something in you has decided that being right matters more than being close to the people you love.

James 4:6 (RSV-CE) names exactly what's operating underneath both scenes: "God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble." Notice the word — opposes. Not merely withholds blessing from. Actively opposes, like a force pushing back against you. Pride is the one posture Scripture says puts a man directly in God's path, resisting the very help he needs most. And grace — the thing every struggling business, every strained marriage, every tired man actually needs — is reserved, specifically, for the humble. Not the talented. Not the right. The humble.

Pride is effective at blocking the door because it disguises itself as strength. Admitting you were wrong feels like losing ground, so you hold the position instead, even when you know the position is false. What pride actually blocks is bigger than your partner's trust or your wife's warmth tonight — it's grace itself, and you can't receive grace while you're still busy defending a number, a tone, a decision you already know was wrong.

**Today:** Call the person you're currently defending yourself against — your wife, a business partner, a friend — and say the actual sentence: "I was wrong, and I'm sorry." No qualifier, no explanation tacked onto the end.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I know I was wrong and I've spent three days defending myself instead of just admitting it. Give me the humility to say the actual words — I was wrong — without an excuse tacked onto the end. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
