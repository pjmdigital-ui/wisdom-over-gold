# Day 24 — Fear Disguised as Planning

You've built a seventeen-tab spreadsheet modeling your retirement under six different market scenarios. You check it more than you'd admit — some weeks, daily. You've got a three-year runway calculated for the business, a college fund projection for each kid broken out by year, and a line item labeled "worst case" that you've quietly revised downward twice this year alone, just to feel like you're accounting for enough bad news. Your wife has started saying, half-joking, that you love that spreadsheet more than you love sleep. She's not entirely wrong.

You'd call this prudence. Being responsible. Taking care of your family the way a man is supposed to. And plenty of it is exactly that — nobody's arguing you shouldn't plan, shouldn't save, shouldn't think ahead. But somewhere past a certain point, usually invisible from the inside, planning stops being stewardship and becomes something else entirely: an attempt to control a future that was never yours to control in the first place, dressed up in language respectable enough that you never have to call it what it is. Fear, wearing a suit.

You know the tell, if you're honest. Prudence plans and then rests. Fear plans and then plans again, and again, checking the same numbers for reassurance they were never going to be able to give, because no spreadsheet has ever actually guaranteed a single future outcome. It just gives the illusion of a guarantee, which is enough to keep a man opening the tab one more time.

Jesus addresses this directly in Matthew 6:31-34: "Therefore do not be anxious, saying, 'What shall we eat?' or 'What shall we drink?' or 'What shall we wear?' ... your heavenly Father knows that you need them all. But seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things shall be yours as well" (RSV-CE). He doesn't tell you to stop working or stop planning — He tells you where the anxiety underneath the planning needs to go. Not into a seventh scenario model. Into trust that the Father who feeds the birds outside your window, who never once opens a spreadsheet, already knows exactly what your family needs.

The line that should stop you is the last one: "sufficient for the day is its own trouble." You weren't built to carry tomorrow's trouble today, on top of today's. Over-planning tries to do exactly that — pre-load every future worry into the present moment so you can feel, falsely, like you've already handled it. You haven't handled it. You've just moved the anxiety earlier and made it live in your body longer than it needed to.

**Today:** Close the spreadsheet, the app, the account you keep checking — for the rest of today. Instead, write down one sentence naming what you're actually afraid of, and hand that specific fear to God in prayer instead of to another projection.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I keep checking the numbers looking for a guarantee they were never going to give me. Help me close the spreadsheet, name what I'm actually afraid of, and trust You with tomorrow instead of trying to control it today. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
