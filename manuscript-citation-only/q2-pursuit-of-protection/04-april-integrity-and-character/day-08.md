# Day 8 — The King's Good Servant

Weldon's regional director asked him to sign off on a safety inspection checklist a week before it had actually been done. Just a formality, the director called it, paperwork catching up to reality, since the inspection was already scheduled and would obviously pass. Everyone up the chain did it this way. Weldon's signature wouldn't change a single fact on the ground. It would just make the timeline look clean for an audit that was really about appearances, not safety.

He almost signed it. The belief that got his pen halfway to the page was one most men carry without ever examining it: *going along with what the people over me expect is safer than taking a stand nobody even asked me to take.* His director wasn't a tyrant. He was just a boss with a request that cost Weldon nothing visible and would make one meeting easier. Saying no felt bigger than what was actually being asked.

Five hundred years ago, a man faced a version of that same request with far higher stakes. St. Thomas More served as Lord Chancellor of England under King Henry VIII. He was a loyal servant of the crown by every account, a man who believed deeply in the king's right to rule. When Henry demanded an oath naming him head of the Church in England, breaking from Rome, More refused. Not loudly, not as a public campaign. He simply would not sign what he did not believe was true. It cost him his office, his freedom, and eventually his life. On the scaffold, More is remembered for calling himself "the king's good servant, but God's first."

That line holds the whole belief this month is built around, and its answer, in nine words. More didn't refuse Henry because he had a grudge against kings, or because obeying itself was the enemy. He refused because Henry had asked for something that wasn't his to give. A piece of loyalty that belonged to God alone, dressed up as an ordinary act of loyalty to the crown.

Jesus draws that same line when the Pharisees try to trap Him over paying taxes to Caesar. Shown a coin with Caesar's image on it, He tells them to give Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and give God what belongs to God. This isn't a rejection of real authority. Caesar gets what's actually his. It's a line drawn around what Caesar never had a claim to in the first place. A boss, a board, a client, a culture can ask a man for real and rightful things: his effort, his time, his respect. None of them can ask for his conscience. That belongs to God, and no amount of "everyone does it this way" changes who owns it.

Weldon's checklist wasn't Henry's oath. But the shape of the request was the same thing in miniature. Sign the thing that isn't true yet, because saying no seems bigger than what's being asked. It rarely feels that big at the time. That's exactly how it gets signed.

**Today:** Locate the one place this week where what's being asked of you actually belongs to God, not to whoever's asking. State it clearly: "I can serve the people over me honestly, but I don't get to hand them what belongs to God." Then decline the one thing on your list that crosses that line.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've signed off on things that were smaller than they should have felt uncomfortable being, and called it loyalty instead of what it was. Give me More's clarity to give Caesar what's his and keep for You what's Yours alone. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

MATTHEW 22:21
