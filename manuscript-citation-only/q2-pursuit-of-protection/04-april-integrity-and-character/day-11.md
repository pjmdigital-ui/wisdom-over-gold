# Day 11 — Integrity in the Small Print

Miles was three weeks from closing the sale of his landscaping company when he found the gap. The buyer's attorney had sent over the asset purchase agreement. Section 4.3 — the client contracts being transferred — didn't specifically name the Ridgeview HOA account. That account was worth sixty thousand dollars a year, and Miles had promised in three separate phone calls it would come with the sale. The agreement as written was vague enough that Miles could keep servicing Ridgeview himself after closing and argue later it had never technically been included.

The thought arrived fully formed, the kind of sentence a man doesn't have to work to build: *A gap someone else left in the paperwork is fair game — that's just good drafting, not deception.* The gap was the attorney's mistake, not something Miles had set up. All he had to do was sign what was in front of him and say nothing.

Miles sat with the document open for two days before he called the buyer's attorney himself and asked her to add Ridgeview by name to the exhibit. She sounded surprised. Sellers don't usually call to close their own loopholes. The clarified contract meant the buyer would immediately confirm the account was real and performing, which it was. But it also meant Miles couldn't keep it as a side client if the deal ever fell through before closing.

The belief sitting under Miles's two days of hesitation was really a belief about what honesty requires. It said a man is only obligated to correct what he's asked about directly, and anything left in shadow by someone else's mistake is fair to keep. Fine print becomes the place a lot of men practice this habit, letting an unclear clause do the lying they'd never do themselves.

Scripture treats being exact in business as a moral matter, not a technical one. God's instruction to Israel was concrete: keep honest scales, honest weights, honest measures for grain and for liquid, because He is the Lord who brought them out of Egypt, and that history obligates them even in the marketplace. A scale and a weight are small, boring objects. God didn't attach that command to grand betrayals. He attached it to the ordinary tools a man uses every day to measure what he owes someone else. A contract clause is a modern weight on a modern scale. It's either honest or it isn't, no matter who drafted it first.

Miles's business sold for exactly what it was worth once Ridgeview was named — not more, because he'd already promised it verbally, but not less than an honest account of what he was selling.

**Today:** Reread one document you're currently a party to — a contract, an invoice, a listing — specifically looking for a place where an unclear word or omission currently benefits you. Fix it in writing today, even if no one asked you to, and say to yourself: *My integrity doesn't wait to be caught.*

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I know there are places in my business where I've let unclear terms work in my favor instead of speaking up. Give me the honesty to fix what's ambiguous before anyone catches it, not after. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
