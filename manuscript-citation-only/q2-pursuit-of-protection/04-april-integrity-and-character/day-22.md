# Day 22 — Everyone Does This

You run a small HVAC company, and Tuesday your best technician came in from a job wanting to write up a full unit replacement — nine thousand dollars — for a homeowner whose actual problem was a two-hundred-dollar capacitor. "It's basically end of life anyway," he said. "Every shop in this county quotes the replacement first. If we don't, we're leaving money on the table our competitors are already taking." He wasn't wrong about the competitors. You'd sat in enough owner meetups to know the whole trade runs this way — quote high, let the part-swap be the fallback only if the customer pushes back and asks specifically.

You told him to write up the capacitor. He looked at you like you'd cost the company money, which, this month, you technically had.

That night you found yourself rehearsing his line in your head, except now it was addressed to you: everyone in this business does this. Not a rare corner-cutter, not a bad actor — an entire trade that had collectively decided a certain shape of dishonesty was just how the margins worked, repeated so often by so many people that it had stopped sounding like dishonesty and started sounding like industry standard.

That's the real danger of a belief like this — it doesn't ask you to decide something is right. It only asks you to notice that enough other people are doing it, and let that stand in for the question you never actually answered. *If the whole trade does it, it can't really be wrong — or if it is, it's not my wrong to fix alone.* Scripture names this specific move, the outsourcing of your conscience to a crowd's behavior, directly: "You shall not follow a multitude to do evil; nor shall you bear witness in a suit, turning aside after a multitude, so as to pervert justice" (Exodus 23:2, RSV-CE). Moses is instructing judges here, but the warning generalizes past the courtroom — a practice doesn't become honest because a majority of practitioners have adopted it. The multitude can be wrong together as easily as one man can be wrong alone; numbers don't launder a bad act into a good one.

There's a cost to breaking from the multitude, and you felt it Tuesday — the smaller invoice, the technician's look, the knowledge that three competitors down the road will still quote the replacement tomorrow. Standing apart from an industry norm rarely feels like integrity in the moment. It mostly feels like losing.

But a trade's common practice was never the standard you were actually accountable to. One Judge outranks every competitor's invoice.

**Today:** Identify one place in your business or industry where "everyone does this" has replaced an honest answer to whether something is right. Say to your team, out loud, once: "We quote what's true, even when the trade doesn't." Then apply it to the next job.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for letting how an entire industry behaves stand in for whether a thing is actually right. Give me the courage to hold a different standard than the crowd around me, even when it costs real money this month. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
