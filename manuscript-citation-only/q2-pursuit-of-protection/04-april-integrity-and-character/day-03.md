# Day 3 — What Your Kids Are Actually Learning

Nathan's daughter was seven, still young enough to sit up front on the days he picked her up early. The bank closed at five, and he needed to be in and out before that. So when the teller asked if his daughter was under six — a free lollipop, a faster line, some small perk the branch offered for little kids — he said yes without checking with anyone. He didn't even really decide to. She was small for her age. It came out easy.

His daughter didn't say anything in the moment. Two weeks later, standing in line at the movie theater, the woman at the counter asked her age for the ticket price. His daughter answered before Nathan could open his mouth: "I'm six." She wasn't lying to save money. She was repeating the answer she'd learned was correct. The one Dad had given for her. The one that apparently changes depending on which line you're standing in.

Nathan laughed it off in front of the cashier and corrected her age, feeling his face go warm. It wasn't until they were back in the car that it actually landed. He hadn't taught her a rule about lying. He'd taught her something more specific and more useless. He'd taught her that the truth bends when there's a small convenience on the other side, and that Dad decides when the exception applies.

He'd been living for years on a belief he'd never once said out loud. Hearing his own daughter say it back to him in someone else's words was the first time he actually heard it: *small untruths don't really count as lying — they're just how adults get things done.* He hadn't taught her that in a conversation. He'd taught her that in a bank lobby, in four seconds, without meaning to teach her anything at all.

Proverbs draws a straight line most men would rather not look at directly. A father who walks with integrity leaves his sons blessed, simply by having lived that way in front of them. The blessing is the character itself, carried forward into the next generation. Children don't learn integrity from a lecture about honesty. They learn it from which version of the truth Dad reaches for when it's inconvenient. Nathan had spent no time explicitly teaching his daughter to lie. He hadn't needed to. She was simply paying better attention to him than he was to himself.

**Today:** Return to whoever was in the room the last time you shaded the truth for convenience — a child, a spouse, a friend — and correct it out loud in front of them. Tell them straight: "I want to be honest with you even when it costs me something small, because that's who I'm becoming." Let them watch you fix it, not just hear you announce it.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've handed off small dishonesties as if they don't matter, and someone I love has been watching and learning from every one of them. Make me the kind of man whose children inherit the truth instead of the shortcut. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
