# Day 14 — Closing Week Two

I was standing at a self-checkout kiosk on a Tuesday morning when the scanner missed a four-dollar item. It beeped, but the screen showed one less item in the cart than what was actually in the bag. Nobody was watching. The store wouldn't have noticed four dollars in a day of thousands of transactions. I stood there a second longer than the moment deserved. I'd spent the last five days writing about a man who lost a chancellorship, and then his life, rather than sign a document he didn't believe was true. And here I was deciding whether four dollars mattered.

It mattered exactly as much as the crown mattered to St. Thomas More. Not because the stakes were the same size, but because the test was made of the same material. More spent thirty years as a lawyer, a judge, and a courtier, practicing small refusals long before the one that cost him his life — which cases to take, which favors to grant, which flattery to accept. By the time King Henry VIII demanded he sign the Act of Succession, More was simply doing at enormous cost what he had already practiced doing at small cost for three decades.

Four dollars at a kiosk isn't a chancellorship. But ask yourself honestly. If you can't tell the truth when it costs you four dollars and no one is watching, what makes you think you'd tell it when it costs you something that matters and everyone is watching?

That's the real weight of More's line to the crowd at his execution — "I die the king's good servant, but God's first." He was reporting the conclusion of a life spent putting his loyalties in the right order, in rooms far smaller than that one. He wasn't claiming a virtue he'd suddenly discovered on the scaffold. This week you read about a contractor who lost three buildings, a man who wouldn't let silence lie for him, a seller who fixed a contract no one asked him to fix, a homeowner who disclosed a crack that cost him his own closing date, a father who found out his Sunday self and his Monday self weren't as different as he thought. None of those men were facing an axe. All of them were practicing the same thing More practiced.

David prays this without claiming a spotless record. He asks God to stand up for him on the grounds that he has walked with integrity and trusted the Lord without wavering. He's asking to be judged by the direction of an entire life, not by any single misstep pulled out of it. That's available to you starting with whatever's in front of you this week, four dollars or four hundred thousand.

**Today:** Return to the four-dollar version of the test currently in front of you — the small compromise no one would ever catch. Correct it today, plainly, and say to yourself: *I am not building toward integrity someday. I am practicing it right now, in the room no one is watching.*

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, thank You for the example of a man who kept his integrity small before he ever had to keep it large. Let today's small honesty be practice for whatever You ask of me later. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
