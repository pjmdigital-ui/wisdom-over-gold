# Day 6 — Anger as a Warning Light, Not a Verdict

A dashboard light doesn't tell you to pull over and walk away from the car. It tells you to check something. Most men treat anger like it's already handed down a verdict instead, as if feeling it proves the other person is wrong and action is owed right now. Wes had run his business that way for a decade without ever calling it a policy.

The trigger this time was an email from a supplier cutting a payment term Wes's company had relied on for three years. No warning, and a line about "market conditions" that read, to him, like an insult in professional clothing. His hand was already moving toward a reply before he'd finished reading it twice, something sharp, something that would let this supplier know exactly how this landed. He had the cursor in the subject line within ninety seconds of opening the email.

A stray thought stopped him, almost by accident: *what if this feeling is just telling me something, not commanding me to do something?* He'd never separated those two questions before. To Wes, feeling angry and being right had always felt like the same fact. The heat itself was the evidence. He hadn't asked what the light was warning him about, because he'd already treated it as a verdict, case closed, sentence to be carried out right away.

Proverbs 16:32 makes a comparison worth sitting with. It ranks a man who governs his own temper above a mighty warrior who conquers a city. Notice what's being compared to conquest — not suppressing anger, not pretending it isn't there, but governing it, the way a king rules a kingdom he actually controls rather than one merely occupied by force. A man who wins a military campaign has beaten someone else. A man who governs his own temper has won a harder fight, against the part of himself that wants the fastest possible outcome regardless of whether it's the right one.

Wes closed the email instead of answering it. Two hours later, after a walk and a conversation with his ops manager, he wrote back — still direct, still clear that the term change was a problem, but aimed at the actual issue instead of at proving the supplier had insulted him. He never found out what his first draft would have cost him. That's usually how it works. The version he didn't send is the one that would have taught him the lesson the hard way.

**Today:** Next time anger flares — today, if it happens — don't answer whatever caused it right away. Say to yourself instead: *this feeling is information for me to examine, not a verdict I have to act on this minute.* Write down what the anger is actually pointing to before you respond to anyone.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've treated my anger as proof I was owed an immediate reaction, when really it was just a signal asking me to look closer. Teach me to rule my own spirit before I ever try to win an argument. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
