# Day 12 — The Fruit That Takes the Longest to Grow

Bennett had been "working on his temper" for eleven days when he snapped at his daughter for spilling juice on a homework packet he'd already reminded her twice to keep off the counter. He heard his own voice hit the register he'd promised himself he was done with, and the first thought that landed was disgust with the whole project, not remorse. Eleven days of catching himself, praying about it, apologizing when he slipped, and here it was again, same as before. He told his wife that night he didn't think this was something that actually changes in a man, not really, not permanently. Some guys are just wired hot.

That conclusion came from measuring an eleven-day effort against a habit that had been building for decades. Bennett wanted the outburst gone the way he wanted a bad Wi-Fi router replaced — swap it out, problem solved by dinner. When it didn't work that fast, he read the slow timeline as proof the whole thing was hopeless rather than proof it was simply the kind of thing that takes real time.

James doesn't promise a man his temper resolves on a sprint. He points him at a farmer instead. James 5:7-8 urges his readers to stay patient until the Lord returns, the same way a farmer stays patient waiting for his land to yield its harvest, holding steady through the early rains and the late ones because that return is close at hand. A farmer doesn't get discouraged and give up on a field because the seed hasn't broken ground by the second week. He knows the rain comes in seasons, not on demand, and the waiting is the process, not a delay in it. Nobody calls a farmer weak for not having a harvest in July.

Bennett's mistake was grading eleven days of real, unglamorous effort as a failure because it didn't look like a finished harvest yet, not the outburst at the counter. Patience — the very thing he was trying to build — got abandoned the moment it required patience with himself. That's the trap: a man can hold the right goal and still quit early, simply because early growth is invisible and slow growth feels indistinguishable from no growth at all.

Eleven days counts — it's early rain. The late rain — the actual, durable change — comes on a longer clock than any man wants to sign up for, and the only way to reach it is to keep showing up to the field on the days that look like nothing happened.

**Today:** Write down the specific effort you've already made toward controlling your temper, however small or recent. Say it plainly: "This is early rain, not proof of failure — the harvest is still coming if I stay in the field." Show up to the same discipline again today, regardless of yesterday's outcome.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I want this fixed faster than You seem willing to fix it, and I've been ready to quit on real progress because it didn't look finished yet. Give me the farmer's patience to stay in the field through the slow seasons. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
