# Day 9 — The Danger of Being Right

Victor had the numbers. His brother-in-law had floated a home-renovation plan at Sunday dinner that was going to blow the budget by thirty percent, and Victor knew it before the man finished the sentence. He'd run a construction crew for eleven years. He said so, in front of everyone at the table, in the flattest tone he owns — walked through the math, named every place the estimate was wrong, and finished by pointing out that this was exactly the kind of mistake that gets people upside down on a mortgage.

Every word of it was true. His brother-in-law didn't argue a single figure. He also didn't say another word for the rest of the meal, and Victor's wife brought it up in the car before they'd left the driveway.

Victor spent the drive home defending the delivery by pointing at the content. He wasn't wrong about the numbers, so what was the problem. That sentence sounds like an argument. Underneath it is a belief standing in for one: *if what I said is true, how I said it doesn't matter.* Being correct feels like it should settle the whole account. It only settles whether the facts were right. It says nothing about whether a brother-in-law just got humiliated at his own family's table, when the identical true things could have been said in a way that left him standing upright.

Scripture doesn't split those two questions apart the way Victor wants to. James tells his readers to take this to heart: be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger, because a man's anger doesn't produce the kind of goodness God is actually after. That instruction is aimed directly at men like Victor on nights like this one. Being right doesn't produce righteousness. The verse doesn't say anger produces the wrong outcome sometimes. It says a man's anger, however factually loaded, doesn't accomplish what God wants at all. Correctness and self-control run on separate tracks, and Victor mashed them into one because the first one was easier to win.

The harder admission is that the flatness Victor calls being direct is doing double duty. It's real honesty, and it's also a way of making sure everyone at the table knows who was smartest in the room. Both things can be true about the same tone. A man who only checks his facts before he speaks has checked half of what actually needs checking.

**Today:** Before the next conversation where you know you're right, decide out loud — even just to yourself — "Being correct doesn't excuse how I deliver it. I'm responsible for both." Then choose one true thing you were planning to say and say it to the person's dignity, not past it.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've used being right as cover for being harsh more times than I can count, and called it honesty. Teach me to slow down long enough to let my delivery match Your goodness, not just my accuracy. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
