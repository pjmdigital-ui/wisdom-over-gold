# Day 23 — Righteous Anger as an Excuse

Connor ran a small crew, and one of his subcontractors had cut corners on a foundation pour that was going to cost Connor a client he'd spent eight months landing. He found out an hour before the client's final walkthrough, with no time to fix it and no way to hide it. He drove to the job site, found the subcontractor drinking coffee by his truck, and let him have it in front of four other men — voice raised, finger jabbing, words chosen to humiliate rather than correct.

Later that week, retelling it at his men's group, someone gently suggested he'd gone too far. Connor didn't take it well. "Jesus flipped tables in the temple," he said. "He wasn't calm about it. Sometimes righteous anger is exactly what a situation calls for, and I'm not going to apologize for having standards." A few guys nodded. It sounded biblical. It sounded like Connor had scripture on his side.

He didn't. This is one of the easiest excuses a man can reach for, and one of the most damaging once he believes it. *If my anger is for the right reasons, how I express it doesn't need examining* — that's the belief hiding inside Connor's defense. Most men who reach for "righteous anger" as cover are doing something that only looks like what Jesus did in that temple.

Look at what actually happened there. Jesus walked into the temple, drove out the merchants and money-changers doing business inside it, overturned their tables and the stalls of the pigeon-sellers, and told them plainly that God's house was meant to be a place of prayer, not a marketplace they'd turned into something closer to a den of thieves. Three things are true of that anger. It was aimed at a real injustice — merchants exploiting worshippers in God's own house, not a personal slight against Jesus. It was controlled, not a loss of composure — He drove them out with purpose, quoted Scripture to explain exactly why, and stopped. And it was in service of others — restoring a house of prayer for people who'd been shut out of it by greed, not defending His own reputation.

Connor's anger on that job site failed every one of those three tests. The offense was personal — his reputation, his client, his morning ruined. It wasn't controlled — he was still shaking an hour later. And it served no one but his own need to be seen as the man who wouldn't be crossed; the subcontractor learned to avoid Connor, not to fix his work. Calling that righteous only gives a man permission to feel justified about something he already knows crossed a line — it doesn't make it righteous.

There was a real problem on that job site, and Connor was reasonable to be frustrated by it. The test is what it costs the other person, and whether a man is still in command of himself when he delivers it — never whether the frustration was fair.

**Today:** Before you defend your next flash of anger as "justified," ask three questions honestly: Is this actually about an injustice, or about me? Am I still in control right now, or is this controlling me? Who does this serve besides my own pride? Say out loud: "Being right about the problem doesn't make me right about how I responded to it."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've used the idea of righteous anger to excuse reactions that were really about my own pride and my own comfort. Show me the difference between zeal for what's right and rage over what's mine, and give me the honesty to tell them apart before I act. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

MATTHEW 21:12-13
