# Day 29 — Thirty Days of Slow

Theo had started a habit in the first week of the month without telling anyone: a small notebook in his shirt pocket, one line every time he felt real anger flare, no matter how small. May 3rd: cut off on the interstate, laid on the horn. May 9th: snapped at his wife over a scheduling mix-up. May 14th: slammed a cabinet door hard enough to crack the hinge.

Sitting with the notebook on the twenty-ninth, he counted. Nine entries in the first ten days. Three in the last ten. That looked like real progress until he actually reread the recent ones and noticed something he almost missed: the anger itself hadn't gotten smaller. On May 26th he'd dropped a wrench on his foot in the garage and felt the exact same flash of anger he'd have felt a month earlier. The difference was what happened in the next two seconds. He'd caught it, breathed, and put the wrench down instead of throwing it. Same fire. Different response time.

So has the reflex actually changed, or has he just gotten better at hiding what's still there? It's a fair question to ask yourself at the thirty-day mark, and the honest answer is a little of both. The reflex is still the reflex. What's grown is the space between the spark and what he does with it, and that space, small as it sounds, is the entire difference between a man who rules his temper and a man ruled by it.

St. Peter describes exactly this kind of growth, and it's worth noticing that he describes it as a ladder, not a light switch. In 2 Peter 1:5-6, he tells his readers to make every effort to build on their faith with goodness, goodness with knowledge, knowledge with self-control, self-control with endurance, and endurance with a godly life, each quality resting on the one before it. Self-control sits as a rung in that list, not a finish line, built on what came before it, becoming footing for what comes next. Nobody arrives at self-control complete and stays there. It gets built one effort at a time, and it keeps needing rebuilding.

Thirty days is long enough to notice a lifelong reflex clearly for the first time, catch it more often than not, and start proving to himself that the gap between provocation and reaction is something he actually has some say over. It isn't long enough to erase the reflex itself.

**Today:** Look back honestly at your own month, even without a notebook. Name one specific moment where the old reflex fired but the response was slower, smaller, or different than it would have been thirty days ago. Say it out loud — proof, not wishful thinking.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, thank You for the space that's grown this month between what provokes me and what I do about it. It's small, but it's real, and I know You built it, not me. Keep building it. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
