# Day 1 — The Reflex You Call Strength

I was in a Monday pipeline review when Silas, two years into the job and still learning the rhythm of these meetings, walked through a slide that showed three deliverables slipping past their dates. I felt the heat come up my neck before I'd even decided to be angry about anything. In front of nine people, I cut him off mid-sentence: "This is why nothing on your list ever actually happens." Loud enough that the video call went silent on the other end too.

For about four seconds afterward, it felt like leadership. I'd named the problem, drawn a line, made it clear I wasn't going to let sloppy work slide past me. Nobody in that room was going to accuse me of being soft. That was the whole appeal of reacting fast — it reads as command. The man who speaks first is the man running the room, or so I'd have told you if you'd asked me an hour earlier, before I'd had a chance to watch what it cost.

I would not have told you the sentence I was really operating on, because I'd never once said it to myself in those words: *if I react fast and hard, everyone in the room will know I'm strong.* I'd built a whole style of leadership on that reflex without ever examining it — mistaking speed for command, and volume for conviction, and never once checking whether either one was true.

Scripture names exactly what that reflex is, without softening it: "He who is slow to anger has great understanding, but he who has a hasty temper exalts folly" (Proverbs 14:29, RSV-CE). Exalts — not commits, not indulges. Exalts, the way you'd raise something up onto a stage and light it for everyone to see. My four seconds of feeling powerful had put my folly on display for that whole room to see. Silas said almost nothing for the rest of the meeting. Neither did anyone else.

What I'd really been chasing was proof, mostly to myself, that I wasn't the kind of man people walk over — pride wearing the costume of leadership, and it is a costume: underneath it is a man who needed a room full of witnesses to a fight nobody else even knew was happening. Real understanding looks unremarkable next to it. It waits one more beat before it speaks. It asks a question instead of landing a line. It never needs an audience to know it happened.

**Today:** Find the person you spoke to fastest and hardest this week — a call you cut short, a line you didn't need to say the way you said it. Go to them and say plainly, "I reacted instead of leading, and I'm sorry." Then write down the sentence you're replacing it with: *real strength is what I can hold before I speak, not how fast I react.*

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've mistaken speed for strength more times than I can count, and called it leadership when it was really pride needing an audience. Teach me to value understanding over the satisfaction of reacting first. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
