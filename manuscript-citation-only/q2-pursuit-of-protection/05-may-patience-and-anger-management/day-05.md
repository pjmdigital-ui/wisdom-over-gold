# Day 5 — The Cost You Don't See Right Away

Isaac apologized the same night. That part he got right — twenty minutes after he'd raised his voice at his business partner in front of two junior employees over a missed number in a forecast, he pulled the man aside, admitted he'd been out of line, shook his hand, and considered the matter closed. He felt lighter walking to his car. As far as he was concerned, the account was settled.

Three weeks later he noticed his partner had started sending recap emails after every conversation they had — something he'd never done before. Small decisions that used to happen in a five-minute hallway exchange were now routed through longer meetings, with more people in the room. Nobody said why. Isaac just noticed that an ease between them that used to be automatic now had to be managed on purpose.

He would have told you, the night of the apology, that saying sorry and meaning it closes the account. *I owned it, I said the words, we shook hands — we're square.* What he hadn't accounted for was that his partner wasn't just reacting to one bad meeting. He was recalculating what might happen the next time numbers came in wrong, and an apology, however sincere, doesn't retroactively prove that won't happen again. Only time proves that.

Proverbs 15:18 draws the contrast plainly: a man with a short fuse stirs up conflict, while a man slow to anger is the one who actually settles it. Strife, once stirred, doesn't settle the moment the shouting stops. It has to be calmed — a slower, ongoing work, different from the fast work of losing his temper in the first place. Isaac had assumed the apology did that work. It only stopped the stirring. Those are not the same job, and mistaking one for the other is exactly why so many men feel blindsided months later by a distance they thought they'd already resolved.

This is the cost that never shows up on the night of the outburst — not the argument itself, which is loud and obvious and over quickly, but the recalculation that happens afterward inside the other person, invisible, ongoing, and reversible only through consistency he can't rush or apologize his way past.

**Today:** Think of one relationship that's felt slightly more guarded since your last outburst, even if it was months ago and even if you already apologized. Say the true belief out loud: *an apology starts the repair — showing up steady, again and again, is what finishes it.* Then do one specific, trust-building thing for that person today that has nothing to do with apologizing again.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've assumed my apologies closed the door on what my temper opened, when the people I hurt were still counting the cost long after I'd moved on. Give me the patience to rebuild trust slowly instead of expecting words to do a longer work. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
