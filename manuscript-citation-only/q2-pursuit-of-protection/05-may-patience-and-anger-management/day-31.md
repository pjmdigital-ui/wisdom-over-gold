# Day 31 — Ready for Brotherhood

Colin closed his notebook on the last night of May and realized, sitting with it, that he'd done the entire month alone. Not unsupported, his wife had noticed the change and said so more than once, but alone in the sense that no other man had known, day to day, what he was working on. No one had asked him how the week went. No one had caught him mid-flare-up and said his name the way a friend can, cutting through the heat before it lands somewhere it shouldn't. He'd tracked his own temper in private, corrected himself in private, and made real progress in private.

Who actually knew he was working on this? Colin ran through the list of men he'd call if his truck broke down at midnight, and it was short. The list of men who'd feel free to tell him, mid-conversation, that his voice had just changed and he should hear himself, that list was shorter still. Maybe empty.

There's a comfortable belief hiding in that, and it's worth naming before June asks a different question of the same man: *I can build this kind of character on my own, and honestly, that's more honest — nobody has to watch me fail.* It sounds like humility. It works like isolation.

Proverbs describes what's missing in a single picture that doesn't need much explaining. Proverbs 27:17 pictures one piece of iron sharpening another through direct contact, and says a man is shaped the same way by another man. Iron doesn't sharpen itself. It takes contact, friction, another piece of iron pressed against it, to take an edge and hold it. A man alone with a notebook can notice his own pattern. He can even slow it down, the way Colin has this month. But there's a particular kind of correction only another man can offer: the friend who's in the room when the temper flares and says something about it on the spot, the one who asks a direct question three weeks later and won't accept a vague answer, the one whose own struggle with the same thing makes his correction land as brotherhood instead of judgment. No amount of private effort replaces that kind of contact. It isn't even trying to do the same job.

May asked whether Colin could rule his own spirit, in the car, at his desk, at his own kitchen table, alone in the two seconds before he opened his mouth. By any fair measure, he can, more often than he could thirty-one days ago. That's real, and it's his. But a man who's only ever practiced patience by himself has practiced half of it. The other half only shows up in the friction of other men, the ones close enough to notice, honest enough to say something, and steady enough to take the correction back when it's their turn.

That's where this book turns next. The same character May built alone is about to be tested in company.

**Today:** Name one man you trust enough to actually let this close, not just tell him you had a good month, but ask him directly to notice your temper and say something the next time he sees it slip. Call or text him today. Say it plainly: *I've been working on my temper for a month, and I want someone in it with me now, not just watching from outside.*

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, thank You for what You built in me this month alone with You, a slower reflex, a quieter house, a spirit more ruled than it was thirty-one days ago. I know it isn't finished, and I know I wasn't meant to finish it by myself. Bring the men into my life who will sharpen what You've started. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
