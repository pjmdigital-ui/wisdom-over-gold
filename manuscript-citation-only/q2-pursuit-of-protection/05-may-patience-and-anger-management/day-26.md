# Day 26 — Repairing What Anger Broke

Callum had a client call in eleven minutes when his seven-year-old knocked a full glass of orange juice across his open laptop. He came out of his chair loud enough that she burst into tears and his ten-year-old went silent at the table, fork halfway to his mouth. Callum grabbed paper towels, snapped the laptop shut to drain, and threw a "hey, sorry, that was too much" over his shoulder on his way to find a towel for the keyboard.

He made the call from his phone. The laptop survived. By dinner he'd more or less forgotten it happened. He'd apologized, after all, and the day had moved on the way days do.

His daughter hadn't forgotten. That evening, when he raised his voice at the TV to ask someone to hand him the remote, she flinched, a small, fast flinch, the kind a kid doesn't know she's doing. Callum noticed it and told himself he'd already handled this. *I said sorry. That's what you do.*

That's what he'd always believed an apology was: a transaction that closes the account the moment the words are spoken. But a seven-year-old flinching four hours later is evidence the account is still open on her side, whatever it looks like from his.

Jesus asks for more than that when it comes to reconciliation, and He says it in the context of worship itself. In Matthew 5:23-24, He tells His listeners that if they're presenting an offering at the altar and remember a brother has something against them, they're to leave the offering right there, go make things right with that person first, and only then come back and finish what they were doing. Notice what He doesn't say. He doesn't say send a quick word of apology and carry on. He says leave what you're doing and go, an active, deliberate return to the person, not a phrase tossed over your shoulder on the way to something else.

A real repair names what happened, without the built-in excuse of the deadline or the mess or the bad day. It asks the person what it was like on their end. It doesn't move on until the account is actually closed for both of you, not just for the one who did the yelling.

**Today:** Go back to the person you least fully repaired with, today, or from further back than that, and say it plainly: *saying sorry isn't the same as making it right. I want to actually hear what that was like for you.* Then listen without defending yourself.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've mistaken quick words for real repair more times than I can count. Give me the humility to go back, ask what the damage actually was, and stay in the room until it's truly mended. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

MATTHEW 5:23-24
