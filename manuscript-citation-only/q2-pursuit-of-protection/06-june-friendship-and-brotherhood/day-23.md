# Day 23 — Competition Between Men

Duane saw the post before he'd even had coffee: his friend Grady's company had closed a funding round Duane had spent eight months chasing for his own business and never landed. He typed out "congrats man, so happy for you" in twelve seconds, meant maybe half of it, and spent the rest of the morning unable to concentrate on anything, running numbers in his head that had nothing to do with his actual work, Grady's revenue against his, Grady's timeline against his, a scoreboard nobody had asked him to keep.

They'd been friends for six years, real friends, the kind who'd helped each other move and shown up at each other's kids' baptisms. None of that stopped the sour turn in Duane's stomach every time Grady's name came up in a room. He hadn't said anything unkind to Grady, not once. He also hadn't texted him back in several days over something that would have taken ten seconds, and he knew exactly why, even if he wasn't ready to say it out loud.

The belief running the whole thing rarely gets spoken plainly, because saying it out loud would mean admitting something ugly: Grady's success is evidence of my own failure, so his win costs me something. That's the math envy runs on, a fixed pool of worth, where one man's gain has to come out of another man's account. It's false math. It's also completely convincing at six in the morning with someone else's good news lighting up your phone.

St. Paul names the opposite of that math in a passage men usually only hear read at weddings, stripped of how directly it applies to friendship between men. 1 Corinthians 13:4 opens by naming love as patient and kind, and just as clearly, as something that doesn't envy and doesn't puff itself up. Not jealous. Not a general wish to be nice, a specific instruction aimed at the exact thing curdling in Duane's stomach. Love that keeps no scoreboard is a discipline, chosen again every time one of them gets news the other one wanted for himself. It's not the natural state of a friendship between two ambitious men.

Feeling the pull of comparison is close to automatic, wired into a competitive man before he's even aware of it firing. What actually gets tested is what Duane does with it in the next hour. Envy left unexamined turns into distance: fewer texts, thinner conversations, a friendship that slowly starves while both men insist nothing's wrong. Envy named out loud, even just to yourself, loses most of its grip almost immediately, because it can't survive being looked at directly.

Duane called Grady that afternoon instead of finishing the reply he never sent. He didn't lead with congratulations this time. He led with the truth: "I've been avoiding you because I'm jealous of your funding round, and I didn't want you to see it. I'm working on that. I am happy for you, it's just not simple yet."

**Today:** Name the friend whose success has been costing you something you haven't admitted out loud. Call or text him and say the true thing instead of the polite thing: "I've felt envy about your win, and I didn't want to admit it, I'm choosing not to let it run the friendship." Then ask him one real question about how he's doing.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for the scoreboard I keep without meaning to, measuring my worth against a friend's success instead of resting in what You've already given me. Root out the envy before it starves a friendship I actually value. Teach me to love without keeping score. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 CORINTHIANS 13:4
