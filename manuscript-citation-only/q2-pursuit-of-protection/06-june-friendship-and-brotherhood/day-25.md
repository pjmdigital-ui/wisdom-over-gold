# Day 25 — When a Friend Betrays You

The message came from a mutual friend, not from Sterling himself: the business plan Sterling had shared over dinner eight months ago — numbers, sourcing contacts, the whole pitch — had shown up almost line for line in a competitor's launch. The competitor was Sterling's new business partner. Sterling had known about the deal for months before it went public. He'd sat across the table from the man twice since then and said nothing.

I know this one from the inside. Years ago a friend I trusted with a half-formed idea took it somewhere else while I was still building up the nerve to pursue it myself, and the finding-out was worse than the loss: rerunning every conversation we'd ever had, looking for the moment he'd decided I was more useful as a source than as a friend.

Betrayal by a stranger stings and passes. Betrayal by a friend does something else — it makes you interrogate every relationship you have, because if this one was fake, how would you know the others aren't. The belief that forms right there, usually within the week, is simple and corrosive: *If I let anyone that close again, I'm just handing them the knife.*

Scripture doesn't soften this kind of wound or pretend it's smaller than it is. The psalmist, in Psalm 41:9, describes the specific pain of a close friend he trusted — a man who shared meals at his own table — turning against him. Not a rival, not a stranger, the one who ate at his table. Scripture names this specific grief because it's specific: the closer the man, the deeper the wound, and God doesn't ask you to call it minor. He names it exactly as it is.

What Scripture doesn't do is let the psalm end there in permanent withdrawal, and neither can you. The temptation after a wound like this is to solve it by never being that exposed again — fewer friends, less shared, a table nobody eats bread from. Call that what it is: building a moat and mistaking it for wisdom. A man who locks the door on every friendship because one friend used a key against him isn't protecting himself from betrayal; he's guaranteeing isolation, which is its own kind of loss, just slower.

The honest path runs through grief, not around it. Name what happened plainly. Grieve the friendship you thought you had. Let it take the time it takes. But hold that grief apart from the next man who sits across your table — he hasn't earned your guardedness, and treating him as though he owes a debt Sterling ran up is transferred fear, not justice.

**Today:** Write down, plainly, what the betrayal actually cost you — not what you imagine it might cost you next time. Then write the sentence underneath it: *This wound is real, and it does not get to decide who I trust next.* Say it out loud once before you close the notebook.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, You know exactly what it is to be betrayed by someone who shared Your table. Hold this wound with me, and keep it from hardening into a wall against every man You still want to bring into my life. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

PSALM 41:9
