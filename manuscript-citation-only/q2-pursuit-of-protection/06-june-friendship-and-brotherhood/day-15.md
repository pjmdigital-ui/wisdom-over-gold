# Day 15 — Actually Calling Someone

Reid's phone buzzed with a memory notification, a photo from four years ago, him and his old friend Ross on a work trip, laughing outside a diner at midnight. Reid smiled, thought "I really should call him," and kept scrolling. That was Tuesday. By Thursday he'd had the same thought twice more, once while driving past the exit for Ross's old apartment, once lying awake at 11 p.m. wondering how the guy was even doing these days. Each time, the same small resolve. Each time, no call.

This had been going on for months. Reid genuinely cared about Ross. If you'd asked him, he'd have said Ross was one of his closest friends, no hesitation. And yet an entire season had passed with nothing between them but Reid's private, repeated intention to reach out, an intention that felt, somehow, like it was already doing something. Like thinking warmly about a friend and actually calling him were basically the same thing.

That's the exact belief worth naming, because it's so quiet most men never catch themselves in it: meaning to call counts as maintaining the friendship. It doesn't. A friendship that lives entirely in one man's head is being remembered fondly while it slowly starves.

Scripture doesn't leave room for the distinction Reid was drawing. St. Paul, writing to the Thessalonians, doesn't tell them to feel encouraging toward one another or to intend good things for each other from a distance. He tells them what they're already doing and calls them to keep doing it, urging them in 1 Thessalonians 5:11 to keep encouraging each other and building one another up, the way they had already been practicing. Encourage and build up are actions. They're not feelings. They require a voice, a message, a hand actually reaching out to another man, not a warm feeling held silently and left there.

The gap between Reid's Tuesday and an actual phone call has nothing to do with time, the call would take four minutes, or with knowing how to reach Ross, whose number has sat in his phone for a decade. The whole gap is a habit of letting the thought stand in for the deed, and that habit is invisible precisely because the thought feels like it should count for something. It doesn't cost him anything to feel it, and that ease is exactly the problem.

Every friendship in this book so far, Jonathan risking his standing for David, Paul asking Timothy by name for Luke and Mark, St. Ambrose showing up week after week to preach to a restless young man, was built by someone doing the thing, not meaning to do the thing. No covenant in Scripture was ever sealed by a good intention.

**Today:** Stop at the first name that comes to mind when you read this line, you already know who it is. Say to yourself: "Thinking about him isn't calling him." Then call him, today, before you do anything else on your list.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I have let good intentions toward the men You've put in my life stand in for actually reaching them. Move me from thinking about a brother to actually calling him, today and not someday. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 THESSALONIANS 5:11
