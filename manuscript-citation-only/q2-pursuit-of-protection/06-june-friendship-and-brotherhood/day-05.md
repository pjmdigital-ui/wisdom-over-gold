# Day 5 — The Lie That Vulnerability Is Weakness

Our Wednesday morning Bible study opens the same way every week: someone asks how everyone's doing, and everyone answers in one word. "Good." "Busy, but good." "Can't complain." I've given that answer probably two hundred times over the years I've been part of that group, including the stretch last spring when my prayer life had gone completely dry — when I was going through the motions at Mass, saying words I didn't believe I meant, and hadn't told a single man in that room.

Cormac caught up with me in the parking lot afterward one week, the way he sometimes does. He didn't ask how I was doing again. He said, "You've said 'good' for six weeks straight and you don't look like a man who means it." I laughed it off, told him work had been busy, got in my truck. He didn't push. I drove home relieved I'd gotten away with it, and slightly sick about what "getting away with it" actually meant.

What I was really protecting was an image — that I was a man whose faith didn't waver, whose marriage didn't have hard seasons, who had this figured out well enough to be the guy other men in that group looked to. Showing the actual condition of my soul felt like it would cost me that standing. The belief was never stated so plainly in my head, but it ran underneath every "good" I gave: if these men see the cracks, they'll see less of a man.

Scripture reads the situation differently than I did. Proverbs 27:9 draws a contrast between the things that lift a man's mood on the surface — the equivalent of oil and perfume, the stuff that makes him look and feel put-together — and the deeper ache trouble can leave in his soul. The verse doesn't say the surface things are worthless. It says they operate on a different layer entirely than the soul, which can be genuinely torn underneath a well-kept surface, and no amount of polish on the outside touches what's actually wrong on the inside.

That's exactly what six months of "good" had been — oil and perfume over a soul that needed something a one-word answer couldn't provide. The men in that room weren't going to fix my prayer life by hearing about it. But they might have prayed for me by name, which is a different thing than a stranger praying for "requests unspoken," and I'd denied them and myself that for half a year over an image I was the only one actually defending.

**Today:** Tell one man the true answer the next time he asks how you're doing — not the polished one. Text Cormac, or whoever your version of him is, before the day ends. Say it plainly: the truth is I've been struggling with ______, and I didn't want you to think less of me. Speaking it is the new belief in motion: being known costs less than staying hidden.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've spent months polishing the outside while my soul went untended underneath it, afraid of what other men would think if they saw the real state of things. Give me one honest conversation today instead of another practiced answer. Let me trust that being known by a brother is safer than staying hidden from him. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
