# Day 17 — What You Never Learned to Say

A friend asked me last month how I was doing, really, and I opened my mouth and nothing came out except "fine, busy, you know how it is." Not because I was hiding anything. I genuinely didn't know the answer. I'd gone weeks running on autopilot — work, kids, Mass, bed — without once stopping to ask myself what I actually felt underneath all of it. When my friend asked, I went looking for the answer and found an empty room.

That's a more common problem than most men admit. We talk about communication as if the missing piece is always the conversation with someone else — the hard talk with a spouse, the confrontation with a business partner, the phone call to a friend you've been avoiding. But before any of that, there's a conversation almost no man was ever taught to have: the one with himself, naming what's actually going on inside him instead of steamrolling past it toward the next task.

The false belief runs quiet and mostly unnoticed: *I don't need to figure out what I'm feeling — I just need to keep functioning.* Nobody says this sentence out loud. It shows up instead as a man who can tell you his quarterly numbers from memory and cannot tell you, without a long pause, whether he's angry, afraid, or just exhausted. He's fluent in output and illiterate in his own interior.

Proverbs names this directly, and it names the fix too. Proverbs 20:5 compares what's really going on in a man's mind to water sitting deep in a well — hidden, hard to reach — and says a man of understanding is the one who knows how to draw it up and out. Deep water doesn't surface on its own. It has to be drawn — deliberately, with effort, by someone willing to lower a bucket instead of standing at the edge assuming the well is dry because he can't see the bottom. That drawing-out is internal work first. Before you can hand a friend an honest answer, you have to sit still long enough to give yourself one.

This is the order most men get backward. They wait for a crisis to force the external conversation — the marriage counselor's office, the friend finally asking "are you okay" for the third time — and only then, cornered, do they start doing the internal work they should have done alone, on a drive, in a pew, with a journal, weeks earlier. Reverse the order and something changes. A man who has already drawn up his own deep water can walk into a conversation with a friend and say something real in the first sixty seconds, because he isn't discovering what's true while he's speaking — he already knows it. Internal honesty makes external honesty possible in the first place. Skip the first and there's nothing real left to hand a friend when he finally asks.

You don't need a friend in the room to start. You need ten honest minutes and the discipline not to flinch away from what surfaces.

**Today:** Before you talk to anyone else about how you're doing, sit alone for ten minutes with a pen and finish this sentence in writing, more than once if you need to: "What I actually feel right now is ___, and I've been running past it because ___." Then say the new belief out loud: "Naming what's true in me before anyone asks is the water Proverbs says a man of understanding knows how to draw."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for how often I run on autopilot instead of asking You to show me what's really in me. Help me sit still long enough to draw up what's true before I speak it to anyone else. Give me the patience to do the quiet work first. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
