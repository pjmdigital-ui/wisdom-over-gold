# Day 4 — Why Men Stop Making Friends After 30

Weston stood along the fence at his son's baseball practice, half-watching infield drills, when a new father he didn't recognize walked up and introduced himself as Marlon's dad — one of the kids who'd just moved into the district. The man was easy to talk to, made a joke about the coach's fondness for suicide sprints, asked Weston if he wanted to grab a beer sometime after a game. Weston said "yeah, for sure, that'd be great" in the tone men use when they mean *probably not*, and the conversation ended there. He didn't think about it again until he was driving home.

Somewhere on that drive he started counting, out of nowhere, how many friends he'd actually made since turning thirty. He was forty-four. Every man he'd call if something went wrong — his brother-in-law, two guys from his old company softball team, one friend from high school he saw twice a year — he'd known before thirty. Fourteen years, and the roster hadn't added a single name.

It wasn't that he'd become worse at talking to people. He talked to plenty of people, all day, at work and at church and standing at that fence. What he'd stopped doing was the harder, slower thing underneath talking — the follow-up text, the second invitation, the willingness to let a new acquaintance cost him something before it had proven itself worth the cost. Somewhere in his thirties he'd started running the math on new friendships before starting one: new relationships take years to become the kind you can lean on, and I don't have years to spend on someone who might not become that. Easier to just be friendly and move on.

Scripture doesn't run that math. When David finished speaking to Saul, Scripture tells us something in Jonathan fused itself instantly to David — the two of them bound together on the spot, Jonathan cherishing him the way he cherished his own life (1 Samuel 18:1). Bound — not scheduled, not vetted over a probationary period, not weighed against the return on investment. Two men met and something in them fastened together on the spot, and it happened to be a friendship that later cost Jonathan his own father's favor and nearly his life. Jonathan didn't calculate whether David was worth that before he loved him. He just did.

Weston's instinct at that fence hadn't been wrong that a new friendship takes time to prove itself. What was wrong was treating that as a reason not to start one. Every friendship a man has now was once a stranger standing at a fence making an awkward joke about wind sprints.

**Today:** Find the man you brushed off with a "yeah, for sure" you didn't mean — from this week, or further back if nothing's recent. Reach out and follow through on the invitation this time, with an actual day and time attached. As you send it, say the belief you're replacing: friendship isn't an investment I have to justify in advance. It starts the way Jonathan's did — with showing up.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've let years pass without adding one real friend, telling myself I was too busy or too old for it to be worth starting. Forgive the calculation I've been running instead of just showing up for someone new. Knit my heart to a brother the way You knit Jonathan's to David, without me weighing the cost first. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 SAMUEL 18:1
