# Day 1 — Iron Sharpens Iron

I hadn't shown up to Thursday pickup basketball in five weeks. I told myself it was just a busy stretch at work. The truth was different. I'd been sleeping on the couch three nights a week. Standing in a gym, making small talk with men who might ask how things were going, felt like too much that month. It was easier to just not go.

Roman noticed anyway. He'd played on my team every Thursday for six years. Six weeks of silence was enough for him. He showed up at my house on a Tuesday night without calling first. I opened the door already annoyed, already rehearsing the version of "fine" I planned to give him. He skipped the small talk. "You look like a man who hasn't slept right in a month," he said. "And I'm not leaving until you tell me why."

I fought him on it for maybe ten minutes. Then I told him the truth. My wife and I had stopped really talking. I'd been handling it alone because saying it out loud felt like admitting I couldn't handle something I was supposed to handle myself. Roman didn't fix it. He couldn't have. But something in me that had been tight for weeks let go the moment I said it to another man's face instead of just carrying it.

For most of my life I believed something I'd never said out loud: a man who needs other men hasn't gotten strong enough yet. Struggle alone. Solve it alone. Only resurface once it's handled. Asking for help before I'd used up my own strength looked like weakness. I'd built years of self-reliance on an idea I never actually tested.

Scripture answers that idea directly. Proverbs 27:17 says one man sharpens another the same way iron sharpens iron. Iron doesn't sharpen itself. It takes another blade pressed against it, with enough rubbing to wear away what's gone dull. A man alone in a room with his own steel stays exactly as sharp, or as dull, as he already was. Sharpening takes contact. Contact means letting another man close enough to do the wearing-away work you can't do to yourself.

That's the whole case for brotherhood. It's worth sitting with, because most men treat that verse the way I used to treat Roman's Thursday phone calls: a nice sentiment, not something that actually works a certain way. Iron sharpens iron because of the friction, not despite it. Roman's visit that Tuesday wasn't comfortable. It cost him an evening and cost me my pride. That discomfort was the sharpening.

A man who insists on staying unworked calls it independence. Scripture calls it something else: a blade going dull in a drawer, alone, believing itself safe.

**Today:** Name one man in your life who has actually sharpened you, someone who pressed against you enough that you came away changed. Call or text him and tell him so directly. Then say the belief you're replacing, out loud, to him or to yourself: needing other men just means you're finally being honest about how iron works.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, for too long I convinced myself that carrying everything alone was strength. Really it was pride keeping the men You placed around me at arm's length. Thank You for the ones who refused to let me disappear behind that excuse. Give me the courage to be that same kind of friend to another man before he has to ask. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
