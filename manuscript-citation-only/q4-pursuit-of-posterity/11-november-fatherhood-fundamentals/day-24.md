# Day 24 — While He Was Still Far Off

Clement has stopped checking Roan's social media at midnight, not because it stopped hurting but because three months of doing it hadn't changed anything except how little he slept. Roan is twenty-six, living somewhere near Tucson as far as anyone in the family knows, and the last real conversation between them happened eight months ago on the phone, Roan's voice so unlike itself that Clement still isn't sure how much of it was the argument and how much was whatever he'd taken beforehand. Clement has replayed that call enough times to know exactly which sentence ended it — "I can't watch you do this to yourself anymore" — and how fast the line went dead after.

He still texts most Sundays. *Thinking of you today. No reply needed.* Nothing comes back, not even the three dots that mean someone started typing and stopped. His wife has started saying, gently, that maybe it's time to let Roan come to him when he's ready — that reaching out might be starting to look less like love and more like a man who can't accept an answer he doesn't want. Clement isn't sure she's wrong. He's also not sure what else a father does with a son who might be in real danger and won't say so.

Last week he drove past the exit that used to lead to Roan's old apartment, out of habit, before he caught himself. He kept driving. He wanted to stop.

Somewhere underneath the exhaustion, Clement has started operating on a belief that sounds like wisdom: that love which keeps getting refused eventually has to step back and wait for the other person to want it first. It's a reasonable-sounding rule. It is not the rule the father in Jesus's own parable follows.

Luke tells us what that father was doing before the son ever explained himself, apologized, or proved a single thing had changed. In Luke 15:20, the son gets up and heads home, and while he is still a long way off, his father spots him, is moved with compassion, and runs to embrace and kiss him. The father saw him while he was still a long way off — which means he was watching the road. Not occasionally, not until it got embarrassing to keep hoping. He was scanning for his son before the son had done anything to earn it, before any accounting of the money that was gone, before the rehearsed speech got past its first line. The compassion came before the repentance finished.

That's the posture Clement is being asked to hold, and it costs him something different than the posture he's drifting toward. Waiting for Roan to prove himself ready feels safer — it protects Clement from being refused again. Watching for him, the way the father in the parable watches, offers no such protection. It just keeps the road in view. Nothing in the story promises the son comes home on a particular week, or that this Sunday's text gets an answer. Clement doesn't get to set that timeline. He only gets to decide whether he's still watching when it happens.

**Today:** Don't try to fix, fund, or reach Roan any harder than you have been — that isn't the work today. Send this Sunday's text the same as always, expecting nothing back, and then write one sentence somewhere you'll see it tomorrow morning: "I am still watching the road." Not a strategy for bringing him home faster. Just evidence, for yourself, of which father you're choosing to be while you wait.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, You watched for me long before I turned back toward You, and You never once used my distance as a reason to stop looking. Keep my eyes on the road for Roan. Keep my heart from hardening into a man who's quit hoping just because hoping hurts, and give me the strength to keep loving him with no guarantee of when I'll get to see it answered. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
