# Day 13 — The Promise You Make to a Kid

Sylvie tugged Cassian's sleeve for the third time in twenty minutes. "Can you play now?" She was five, holding a plastic tea set, and Cassian had told her "in a minute" all three times, phone still against his ear, a client walking him through change orders on a project already behind schedule. The call ran another forty minutes. By the time he hung up, Sylvie had put the tea set away herself and gone to watch a show instead — not upset exactly, just done asking.

Cassian noticed it later that night, not the call, but the giving up. Somewhere in those forty minutes, "in a minute" had become the real answer, and Sylvie had understood it faster than he had. He hadn't lied to her, not exactly — he'd meant "in a minute" honestly each time he said it. But he'd been treating a promise to a five-year-old as a lower category of promise altogether, one that could stretch and bend without actually breaking anything, in a way a promise to a client or a boss couldn't.

This verse already did work in these pages once, back in April, anchoring a promise made at a wedding altar. It carries just as much weight redirected at a much smaller audience — a five-year-old with a tea set, waiting to find out if "in a minute" means anything at all. In Matthew 5:37, Christ tells His listeners to let their yes simply mean yes and their no simply mean no — anything beyond that plain word comes from a dishonest place. Christ isn't only drawing a line around oaths sworn in court or vows made in front of a priest. He's naming an ordinary problem — words that don't mean what they say, offered because they're easier in the moment than the truth. Cassian's "in a minute," stretched across forty minutes, was exactly that kind of word.

A five-year-old doesn't have a calendar or a contract to hold her father to. She has only whether his word turns out to be reliable, tested a hundred small times a week — the tea set, the bike ride, the promised five more minutes at the park. Every "in a minute" that becomes an hour teaches her something about what Dad's word is actually worth, long before she has the vocabulary to say so.

Cassian found Sylvie on the couch after the call ended and didn't lead with an apology. He turned off the show, sat down on the floor across from her, and asked, "Is the tea still on?" She looked at him for a second like she was deciding whether to believe it, then went and got the cups.

**Today:** The next time you tell one of your kids "in a minute," set an actual timer, out loud, where they can hear it — and when it goes off, stop what you're doing and go. Let them watch your word hold.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've let my youngest learn that my "in a minute" doesn't always mean a minute, and that's a debt I owe her more than anyone. Make my yes a real yes to my children, even when keeping it costs me something I'd rather finish first. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

MATTHEW 5:37
