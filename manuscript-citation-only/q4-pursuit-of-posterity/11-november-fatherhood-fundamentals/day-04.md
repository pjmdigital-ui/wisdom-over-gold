# Day 4 — Words That Don't Discourage

Tate's daughter Hazel came home with a math test — an 84, up from a 71 the month before — and set it on the counter the way a kid sets something down when she's proud of it but not sure yet how it'll land. Tate glanced at it while unloading the dishwasher and said, without really thinking about it, "Good, now let's get you to a 90." He meant it as encouragement, the kind of thing a coach says to a player who's improving. He went back to the dishwasher and didn't notice her take the test off the counter and go straight to her room.

It wasn't until his wife mentioned it that night — Hazel had told her she didn't want to show her dad tests anymore, "he just tells me what's still wrong with it" — that Tate replayed the exchange and heard it the way his twelve-year-old had actually heard it. Not encouragement. Just the next deficiency, arriving before the improvement had a chance to land as good news.

He hadn't meant to discourage her. That was the part that unsettled him. He could point to a dozen moments like that one, each individually reasonable, each one a small withdrawal from an account he hadn't realized he was drawing down — a running total his daughter was keeping that he'd never once checked.

St. Paul wrote to a church in Colossae with a warning aimed directly at fathers, not children: "Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged" (Colossians 3:21, RSV-CE). Paul doesn't warn against cruelty here, or abuse, or the obvious sins a father might catch himself committing. He warns against provoking — the accumulation of small, reasonable-sounding pushes that add up to a kid who quits bringing her father anything good, because good has never once been allowed to just be good in his mouth.

Tate's 90 wasn't wrong as a goal. Said in that moment, attached to nothing but the number still missing, it cost him something he hadn't budgeted for: a daughter's willingness to let him see her trying. Paul's warning is about timing more than standards — a father learning to let a win be a win before he reaches for the next correction, so the correction, when it does come, still has a place to land.

Hazel didn't need her father to stop caring whether she improved. She needed thirty seconds where an 84 was just an 84 — a genuinely better test than the one before it, worth saying so plainly, with nothing attached.

**Today:** Find the last thing your kid showed you — a grade, a drawing, a game, anything — and go say something good about it with no correction, no "but," and no next goal attached. Just let it be good.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've turned good news into the occasion for the next correction more times than I've let it stand on its own. Thank You for warning fathers plainly through Paul's letter, before the discouragement piled up too high to notice. Give me the discipline to let my child's good moments be simply good today, without adding anything to them. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
