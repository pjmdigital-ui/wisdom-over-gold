# Day 4 — Words That Don't Discourage

Tate's daughter Hazel came home with a math test, an 84, up from a 71 the month before. She set it on the counter the way a kid sets something down when she's proud of it but isn't sure yet how it'll land. Tate glanced at it while unloading the dishwasher and said, without really thinking, "Good, now let's get you to a 90." He meant it as encouragement, the kind of thing a coach says to a player who's improving. He went back to the dishwasher and didn't notice her take the test off the counter and go straight to her room.

It wasn't until his wife mentioned it that night that Tate understood what happened. Hazel had told her mom she didn't want to show her dad tests anymore. "He just tells me what's still wrong with it." Tate replayed the exchange and finally heard it the way his twelve-year-old had actually heard it. Not encouragement. Just the next problem to fix, arriving before the good news had a chance to land.

He hadn't meant to discourage her. That was the part that bothered him. He could point to a dozen moments like that one, each one reasonable on its own, each one a small withdrawal from an account he hadn't realized he was drawing down. It was a running total his daughter had been keeping that he'd never once checked.

St. Paul wrote to a church in Colossae with a warning aimed straight at fathers, not children. In Colossians 3:21, he tells fathers not to push their children so hard that they lose heart and give up trying. Paul isn't warning against cruelty here, or abuse, or the obvious sins a father might catch himself committing. He's warning against something smaller and more common: small, reasonable-sounding pushes that pile up until a kid quits bringing her father anything good at all, because good has never been allowed to just be good in his mouth.

Tate's goal of a 90 wasn't wrong. But said in that moment, attached to nothing but the number still missing, it cost him something he hadn't planned for: a daughter's willingness to let him see her trying. Paul's warning is about timing as much as it's about standards. A father has to learn to let a win be a win before he reaches for the next correction, so the correction, when it does come, still has somewhere to land.

Hazel didn't need her father to stop caring whether she improved. She needed thirty seconds where an 84 was just an 84. A genuinely better test than the one before it, worth saying so plainly, with nothing attached.

**Today:** Find the last thing your kid showed you. A grade, a drawing, a game, anything. Go say something good about it with no correction, no "but," and no next goal attached. Just let it be good.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've turned good news into the occasion for the next correction more times than I've let it stand on its own. Thank You for warning fathers plainly through Paul's letter, before the discouragement piled up too high to notice. Give me the discipline to let my child's good moments be simply good today, without adding anything to them. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

COLOSSIANS 3:21
