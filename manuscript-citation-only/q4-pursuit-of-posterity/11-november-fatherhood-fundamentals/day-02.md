# Day 2 — The Blessing You Speak

Archer sat in the car after his son Crosby's baseball game running through the highlight reel out loud, except it wasn't a highlight reel. Two errors in the field. A strikeout looking with runners on. A lazy jog to first on a ball he should have hustled out. Archer walked through each one methodically, the way he'd walk a junior employee through a missed deadline, certain he was doing the responsible thing — a father who lets sloppy effort slide isn't preparing his kid for anything.

Crosby stared out the window the whole ride, giving one-word answers, and Archer chalked it up to a nine-year-old sulking over a loss. It wasn't until bedtime, when Crosby asked him flatly, "Do you like watching me play?" that something in the question stopped him cold. He ran back through every game that season in his head and couldn't find one where he'd said anything to his son besides what needed fixing.

What was Crosby actually hearing, game after game — that his father showed up to correct him, or that his father was for him at all? Archer had never once asked himself that question before Crosby asked it for him, and he didn't have a good answer ready.

The priestly blessing God gave Aaron to speak over Israel doesn't contain a single correction in it. Numbers 6:24-26 records it as pure blessing: the LORD keeping His people, His face shining favor down on them, His peace given over them, three lines deep and nothing else added. God commanded this blessing spoken over His people by name, out loud, as its own act — not attached to a lesson, not earned by good behavior that week, not a reward held back until performance improved. Blessing and correction are both real parts of leading a household, but Numbers 6 shows a father something Archer had never separated out: blessing is a distinct act, spoken deliberately on its own terms, and a child needs to hear it that way — plainly, unattached to anything — before he'll believe it's real.

Archer had been treating every conversation with Crosby as an opportunity to improve him, which meant Crosby had never once heard his father simply delight in who he already was, errors and all. A kid can absorb a lot of correction from a father he trusts is also, separately, for him. He can't tell the difference between a father who's for him and one who's only managing him if the second voice is the only one he ever hears.

**Today:** Find your kid today and say something that isn't correction and isn't tied to anything they did well — just who they are to you. Say it plainly, out loud, like a blessing spoken over them, not a compliment earned.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've spent so much energy correcting my kids that I've let the blessing go unsaid, as if it were optional. Thank You for a blessing You commanded spoken over Your own people freely, not as a reward. Give me the words to bless my children today, plainly and out loud, so they know I'm for them before they ever hear what needs fixing. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

NUMBERS 6:24-26
