# Day 2 — The Blessing You Speak

Archer sat in the car after his son Crosby's baseball game, running through everything Crosby did wrong. Two errors in the field. A strikeout looking with runners on base. A lazy jog to first on a ball he should have hustled out. Archer walked through each mistake the way he'd walk a junior employee through a missed deadline. He was sure he was doing the right thing. He told himself a father who lets sloppy effort slide isn't preparing his kid for anything.

Crosby stared out the window the whole ride home, giving one-word answers. Archer figured he was just a nine-year-old sulking over a loss. Then at bedtime, Crosby asked him flatly, "Do you like watching me play?" Something in that question stopped Archer cold. He ran back through every game that season in his head. He couldn't find one where he'd said anything to his son besides what needed fixing.

What was Crosby actually hearing, game after game? That his father showed up to correct him? Or that his father was for him at all? Archer had never asked himself that question before Crosby asked it for him. He didn't have a good answer ready.

The blessing God gave the priest Aaron to speak over Israel doesn't have a single correction in it. Numbers 6:24-26 records God asking to be spoken over His people, simply and plainly by name: keep them, watch over them, let His face shine kindness on them, and give them peace. God had this blessing spoken over His people out loud, on its own, as its own act. It wasn't attached to a lesson. It wasn't earned by good behavior that week. It wasn't a reward held back until performance improved. Blessing and correction are both real parts of running a household well. But Numbers 6 shows something Archer had never separated out. Blessing is its own act, spoken on purpose. A child needs to hear it plain, with nothing attached, before he'll believe it's real.

Archer had been treating every conversation with Crosby like a chance to improve him. That meant Crosby had never once heard his father simply enjoy who he already was, errors and all. A kid can handle a lot of correction from a father he trusts is also, separately, for him. But he can't tell the difference between a father who's for him and a father who's only managing him, if the second voice is the only one he ever hears.

**Today:** Find your kid today and say something that isn't correction, and isn't tied to anything they did well. Say something about who they are to you. Say it plainly, out loud, like a blessing spoken over them, not a compliment they earned.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've spent so much energy correcting my kids that I've let the blessing go unsaid, like it was optional. Thank You for a blessing You had spoken freely over Your own people, not as a reward. Give me the words to bless my children today, plainly and out loud, so they know I'm for them before they ever hear what needs fixing. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
