# Day 1 — As a Father Pities His Children

Jasper and Nora spent the last thirty days of October fixing things in their marriage that had worn thin. Neither of them had noticed it happening. Somewhere in that work, Jasper started thinking of his home like a project that was almost done. The foundation felt solid. The hard part felt behind him. Then a thunderstorm rolled through at two in the morning, and his three-year-old daughter Ivy started screaming his name like the world was ending.

He got up fast, but it was more out of duty than tenderness. In his head he was already planning the quick version of comfort: check the window, check the nightlight, tell her it's just noise, get her back down, get himself back to sleep before the alarm. He had given her the house, the roof, the room she was crying in. To him, that felt like the job. Sitting with her fear instead of just fixing it, staying until she actually calmed down instead of until she stopped making noise, felt like something he could work on later. Once he wasn't this tired. Once the kids were old enough to really talk to.

He opened her door ready to give the quick version. Ivy was standing up in her crib with her arms already reaching out. She wasn't reaching for a fixed window latch. She was reaching for him. Something about that stopped him cold. He sat down on the floor of her room, pulled her into his lap, and stayed until her breathing slowed on its own. He didn't have a clock running in his head about what came next.

Jasper had never said this sentence out loud, but he had been living by it since Ivy was born: *A good father handles what's necessary and lets the rest wait for an easier season.* That belief was the same one that had almost sent him back to bed with a fixed nightlight and a closed door. Sitting there in the dark, he understood it differently. What Ivy reached for in that crib was him. Not a fixed latch.

Scripture names exactly what a child is building in a moment like that, whether the father realizes it or not. Psalm 103:13 says God's compassion for the people who love Him works the same way a father's compassion works toward his own kids. The psalm doesn't point to some abstract idea of a father. It points to a real one, an ordinary man a child already knows. His face, his arms, his patience become that child's first working idea of what "Father" even means. Ivy wasn't going to remember whether the latch got checked. Somewhere deeper than memory, she was going to remember whether her cry brought someone who stayed.

That's the ground this whole month stands on. A father who thinks his real job is just providing for his kids and staying out of their way has it backward. Presence isn't just a bonus after the basics are handled. Presence is one of the basics. It might be the one a child needs most and can least explain wanting. Every ordinary moment, like a 2 a.m. thunderstorm, quietly teaches a child something about God, long before that child ever sits in a pew and hears the word "Father" used for Him.

**Today:** Name one place this week where you've been giving your kids the quick version of yourself instead of the present one. Go do the slower version today, even for five extra minutes. Don't say anything about it. Just stay.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've told myself the basics were enough and pushed the presence off to a season I keep delaying. Thank You for pitying me the way a father pities his own children, patient with me long before I deserved it. Teach me to stay the way You stay, so my kids learn who You are by learning who I am with them. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
