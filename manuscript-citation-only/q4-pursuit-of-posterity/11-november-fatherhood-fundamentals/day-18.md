# Day 18 — Answering Before He's Heard

Ezra walked in forty minutes past curfew, and Powell was on him before the door finished closing. Phone's dead, coach kept the team late, he tried texting from a friend's — Powell caught maybe half of it, already three sentences into the lecture about respect and responsibility and what happens next time this happens. Ezra stopped trying to explain around the second sentence. He just nodded, said "okay," and went to his room.

Powell felt good about it, actually — handled, addressed, consequence delivered. It wasn't until he ran into another team parent at the grocery store two days later, and heard the real version of that night — a kid on the team had gotten hurt at practice, Ezra had stayed to help get him to his car, and yes, his phone actually had died — that Powell understood what he'd done. He hadn't listened to his son. He'd managed him, the way you manage a problem, and moved on before Ezra ever got to the part that mattered.

*My job as a father is to handle what my kids do, not sit around waiting to hear why they did it* — that was the operating belief, and it had been running Powell's parenting for years without him noticing, because it usually felt like leadership in the moment it was happening.

Scripture is blunt about where that leads: Proverbs 18:13 says that answering a matter before actually hearing it out is both foolish and shameful. Not caution. Not a minor parenting misstep. Foolish and shameful — the same charge Scripture levels at a man who's made himself look the fool in a way he can't take back. Powell hadn't protected his authority by cutting Ezra off fast. He'd damaged something with him, and called it discipline while he did it.

Real listening is the thing correction is supposed to be built on, not the soft alternative to it. A father who answers before he hears isn't managing his household well — he's just getting to the part he was going to say anyway, regardless of what actually happened. Ezra had a true story that night, one that reflected well on him, and his father talked over it. That's the version of this Powell has to live with, and the version he doesn't want to repeat.

Powell found Ezra that weekend and asked him to walk through the whole night again, from the start, no interruptions. He didn't say much. He let his son finish every sentence, including the parts that made Powell look bad for how he'd handled it the first time.

**Today:** The next time one of your kids starts explaining something — a mistake, a fight with a sibling, why they did or didn't do what you asked — let them finish completely before you respond. Count three full seconds of silence after they stop talking before you say anything back.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've mistaken managing my kids' behavior for actually hearing them more times than I want to admit. Teach me to listen all the way to the end before I speak, even when I think I already know what they're going to say. Spare me the folly of answering before I've heard. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
