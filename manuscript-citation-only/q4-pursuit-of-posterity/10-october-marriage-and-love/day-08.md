# Day 8 — Only What Builds Up

Philip burned the toast Saturday morning and said, without thinking, "I swear you set this timer wrong on purpose," loud enough that Vivian heard it from the next room. It wasn't true, and he knew it wasn't true the second it left his mouth. He'd said sharper things than that to her before breakfast plenty of times: a jab about her driving, a crack about how long she took getting ready, the kind of line that gets a laugh from a stranger and a flinch from a wife who hears it every week.

What stopped him that morning was hearing himself say it and realizing he would never, in a thousand years, say that sentence to a coworker who'd handed him the wrong file, or to a stranger at a checkout counter who'd bagged his groceries wrong. With a stranger, he'd shrug it off or say nothing at all. With Vivian, some part of him had decided the normal rules of decent speech didn't apply, because she wasn't going anywhere and would forgive him by lunch anyway.

*She's stuck with me either way, so what I say in the heat of the moment doesn't really cost me anything.* Philip had never said that to himself outright, but it explained a decade of comments he'd never once made to anyone he wasn't married to.

St. Paul's instruction to the church at Ephesus doesn't make an exception for people who already love you. In Ephesians 4:29, he tells believers to let no rotten word leave their mouths, only words that build up whoever needs it and leave them better for hearing it. Paul's standard is simple: does this word build the other person up. He applies it to speech in general, with no lower bar for the person who already knows you love them underneath the sarcasm. If anything, the standard should be higher with Vivian. She hears more words from Philip than anyone else alive, which means his careless comments do more damage over the years than the same line would do landing once on a stranger who forgets it by Tuesday.

Philip had been treating Vivian's closeness to him as a discount on his self-control, when it should have raised the bar instead of lowering it. A stranger gets his best manners because there's no relationship yet to protect. His wife deserved at least that much, and she was the one person he'd actually promised to protect for life.

**Today:** Notice one thing you've said to your wife this week that you'd never say to a stranger or a coworker. Apologize for it specifically today, and replace it with a sentence that fits Paul's standard, something that actually builds her up.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've spoken to my wife in ways I'd never speak to someone I barely know, because I assumed her love could absorb it. Forgive me for treating her closeness to me as permission instead of a reason for greater care. Guard my mouth today, and let what I say to her build her up. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

EPHESIANS 4:29
