# Day 10 — Apologizing First, Again

Terrence had made the reservation three weeks out for their anniversary — a place Bianca had wanted to try for over a year — and then never called to confirm it the way the website told him to. They showed up Friday night to a full dining room and a hostess who had no record of his name. They ate drive-through tacos in the car in the restaurant's own parking lot, in their good clothes, barely speaking.

By the time they got home, the fight wasn't about the reservation anymore. Bianca said this was what always happened — he made the gesture and skipped the follow-through, and she was tired of being the one who noticed. Terrence said if she'd just texted to check like she does with everything else, they wouldn't be sitting in a parking lot eating tacos. Neither of those sentences was entirely fair. Both of them knew it and said them anyway.

Four days later, they still hadn't circled back to any of it. They were polite at breakfast, coordinated on the kids' schedules without incident, and avoided the actual subject with the discipline of two people executing a plan. Terrence caught himself waiting for something specific: an apology from Bianca first, for the nagging comment, before he'd offer his own for the reservation. He'd decided, without quite admitting it to himself, that whoever spoke first was conceding the argument — that being the first to say sorry meant being the one who'd been more wrong.

St. Paul wrote to the Romans with the opposite instinct in mind. In Romans 12:10, he tells believers to love one another with real affection and to compete with each other at showing honor — language that borrows the image of a contest and points it at the exact opposite of winning an argument: who can honor the other person first, who can go lower, who can move first toward someone else's good instead of waiting to be moved toward. Terrence had turned his marriage into the wrong kind of competition, one where going first felt like losing instead of like love.

Pride keeps a ledger. It counts who apologized last time, who's owed one now, who technically started it. Humility doesn't ask who started it. A man who needs to be right more than he needs to be close to his wife will win a great many small arguments and lose something considerably larger in the process.

Terrence apologized first that Sunday — not for the whole fight, just for his own actual part in it, plainly, without a "but you also" attached. Bianca's apology came within the same breath, unforced, the way honor tends to work when somebody finally goes first.

**Today:** Think of one disagreement at home where you've been waiting for her to apologize first. Go to your wife today and apologize for your specific part in it — no conditions, no "but you also," just your own piece of it, said out loud.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've kept score in my own marriage more than I've wanted to admit, waiting to be right before I'll go first. Teach me the kind of humility that moves toward her without needing to win. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

ROMANS 12:10
