# Day 4 — Not Harsh

Edward ran his household the way he ran his crew at the warehouse — corrections issued fast, tone flat, no time wasted softening a point he was sure was right. When Diane left the porch light burning all night for the third time that month, he didn't ask what had distracted her. He said, "This is the third time. Can you just remember one thing?" in the exact voice he used on a forklift operator who'd missed a checklist item. When she loaded the dishwasher a way he didn't like, he'd quietly reload it himself rather than say anything, and somehow that silence came across sharper than words would have.

Diane finally said something after a dinner where he'd corrected the way she was cutting vegetables. "You talk to me like I work for you." Edward wanted to argue, and couldn't, because the comparison landed. He managed people all day by pointing out what was wrong efficiently and moving on. He'd never consciously decided to run his marriage the same way. It had just been the only leadership style he owned, so it went home with him.

*Keeping standards high and pointing out what's wrong is how you run anything well — a crew, a house, a marriage.* Edward had built a career on that principle and never questioned whether it belonged in his kitchen too.

St. Paul draws a much narrower line for husbands specifically, in Colossians 3:19: love your wife, and do not deal harshly with her. The instruction doesn't tell a husband to lower his standards. It tells him that harshness — the flat correction, the efficient jab, the silence that punishes without a word — has no place in how he leads his wife, whatever place it might have on a warehouse floor. Paul assumes a husband will notice what's wrong. He forbids the tone Edward had defaulted to for handling it.

A crew and a marriage aren't run on the same terms, because they aren't the same kind of relationship. A forklift operator needs correction and can clock out at five. A wife needs a husband who has decided, on purpose, that how he says something to her matters as much as whether he's right — because she doesn't get to clock out, and neither does the tone he's been leaving behind in that kitchen every night. Edward wasn't wrong that the porch light mattered, or that the dishwasher had a better way to be loaded. He was wrong about which parts of his management style belonged inside his marriage and which parts needed to stay at work.

**Today:** Recall the last time you corrected your wife the way you'd correct an employee. Apologize for the tone specifically — not just the words — and tell her one standard you'll hold the same way, but say it differently starting today.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've brought a manager's voice home and used it on my wife without ever deciding to. Forgive me for the harshness I've called efficiency. Teach me to hold what matters without losing the gentleness she deserves from me and no one else. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

COLOSSIANS 3:19
