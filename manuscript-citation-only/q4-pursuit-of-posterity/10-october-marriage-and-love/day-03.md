# Day 3 — Love That Endures

Charles noticed it during a stretch when Claire was working nights to finish a certification and he was handling bedtime, dinner, and the dog by himself most evenings. They passed each other in the hallway more than they talked. The easy affection they'd had a year earlier — the inside jokes, the reaching for her hand without thinking about it — had gone quiet under the exhaustion of the schedule. One night, lying awake after she'd fallen asleep mid-sentence describing her day, Charles found himself running an honest inventory and not liking the answer: he didn't feel much of anything toward her lately. Not anger. Not longing. Mostly just tired alongside her.

He didn't say it to anyone, but the thought sat there for weeks: *if I loved her the way I used to, I'd still feel it, even now. The fact that I don't means something's cooling off that I should be worried about.* He'd built his whole sense of the marriage's health on whether the feeling was present, and by that measure, this season was failing.

St. Paul wrote about love to a church that was fighting over spiritual gifts, not to newlyweds — which is part of why his definition has almost nothing to do with feeling. "Love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. Love never ends" (1 Corinthians 13:7-8, RSV-CE). Paul lists love's marks as things it does under pressure — bearing, believing, hoping, enduring — not things it feels while circumstances are easy. Endurance only shows up as a description of love because Paul assumes seasons will come where the feeling thins out and something else has to carry the weight.

That reframes what Charles had actually been measuring. A feeling that fluctuates with sleep deprivation and a packed schedule gauges how tired two people are, nothing more reliable about the state of their love than that. The love Paul describes was still fully at work in Charles's house that whole exhausted stretch: in the dinners he cooked, the bedtime stories he kept reading solo, the fact that he stayed present instead of checking out when checking out would have been easier. He'd been calling that absence of feeling a cooling marriage. It was closer to evidence of love doing exactly what Paul said it does — enduring through a season that didn't have much warmth left over to spend.

Charles didn't need to manufacture a feeling that wasn't there that week. He needed to keep bearing what needed bearing, trusting that the feeling had a way of returning once the season did, and that its temporary absence had never been the real test of whether he loved Claire.

**Today:** Think of one specific way you've kept showing up for your wife this week even without feeling much toward her in the moment. Name it to yourself as love, not as its absence, and tell her one thing you appreciate about how she's carried this season too.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've measured my love for my wife by how much I feel it instead of how faithfully I've carried it. Thank You for showing me love that endures rather than love that only shows up when it's easy. Keep me steady toward her in the tired seasons, trusting You with the feeling I can't manufacture on my own. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
