# Day 15 — A Mutual Gift

Thaddeus and Adeline had been married nine years, and the season they were in — a new baby, her sleep cut into pieces, his hours stretched thin at work — had turned physical intimacy into one more item on a list of things that weren't happening enough. He caught himself keeping a private, resentful tally of how long it had been. She caught herself treating his interest as one more demand on a body that already felt claimed by everyone else in the house all day. Neither of them said any of this out loud. They just felt the distance grow, and each privately blamed the other for it.

Thaddeus's unspoken belief ran something like this: intimacy is owed to a husband who's been faithful and provided well, and its absence is a debt building up against him. Adeline's ran the other direction: her body belonged to her first, and anyone asking anything of it — even her own husband — was asking her to give away something already spent. Both beliefs turned intimacy into a transaction: something earned, something owed, something rationed out based on who'd been good enough that week.

St. Paul's letter to the Corinthians dismantles both sides of that accounting at once. In 1 Corinthians 7:3-5, he teaches that a husband and wife owe this part of marriage to each other equally — neither one holds exclusive claim over their own body anymore, because each has entrusted that authority to the other. He allows a couple to set this part of their marriage aside only briefly and only by mutual agreement, for the sake of devoting themselves to prayer, and then insists they come back together afterward, so that neither one is left exposed to temptation through a weakened resolve. Paul isn't handing either spouse leverage over the other. He describes a mutual surrender — each one giving authority over their own body to the other, freely, as a form of the same total self-gift the whole marriage is built on. Neither owns the right to withhold as punishment. Neither owns the right to demand as tribute.

That reframes what Thaddeus had been keeping score of. His resentment assumed intimacy was something Adeline held and doled out at her discretion, a supply he was owed access to. Paul's language points the other way: the gift runs in both directions, offered freely by two people who've already given each other everything else, not extracted by whoever feels more entitled to it that week.

It reframed something for Adeline too. Her exhaustion was real and deserved patience, not guilt, but treating her husband's desire as an imposition rather than an expression of the same self-gift she'd promised him missed what the vow had actually been about. A marriage built on mutual generosity looks different than one built on ownership from either side, and the difference shows up in every other room of the house too.

They didn't solve months of a hard season in one conversation. But they had one — honest, a little awkward, neither one keeping score anymore — and it started with Thaddeus admitting the tally he'd been keeping, and Adeline naming the exhaustion she'd been carrying alone.

**Today:** If distance has crept into this part of your marriage, don't keep the private ledger going. Talk to your wife honestly about it — not as a complaint or a demand, but as an invitation to figure out, together, how to give this gift to each other freely again.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've turned parts of my marriage into a ledger instead of a gift, keeping track of what I'm owed instead of what I'm called to give. Teach my wife and me to give ourselves to each other freely, the way Your word describes, without resentment on either side. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 CORINTHIANS 7:3-5
