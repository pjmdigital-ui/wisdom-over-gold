# Day 13 — Seasoned With Salt

Colton had a habit at the dinner table he didn't think much about until Celeste called it out. He'd ask how her day went and half-listen while scrolling, tossing back a flat "mm" or a joke at her expense that got a laugh but landed a little sharp underneath it. When she told him about a hard conversation with her sister, his first response was to point out what her sister had gotten wrong. When their son asked an earnest question about school, Colton answered in the shorthand he'd use with a coworker: quick, efficient, already thinking about something else.

None of it was cruel. That was the thing Colton would have said in his own defense if anyone had pushed him on it. He wasn't yelling, wasn't insulting anyone on purpose, just talking the way he talked. He'd told himself somewhere along the way that home was the one place he didn't have to manage his tone, that the filter he used at work all day was tiring, and family was where a man got to set it down. Celeste heard something different underneath that logic. She got the leftover version of him, the one too tired or too checked-out to bother choosing his words, while coworkers and clients got his careful, considered self.

St. Paul gives the Colossians a standard for speech that doesn't have an exception for the people closest to you. In Colossians 4:6 he tells them their words should always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that they know how to answer everyone well. Always, not just in front of people he was trying to impress. Seasoned with salt meant something specific in the ancient world. Salt preserved food and gave it flavor, the opposite of speech that's flat, careless, or left to rot into sarcasm because nobody's paying attention to it anymore. Paul isn't asking for stiff formality at the kitchen table. He's asking for words a man actually chooses, on purpose, for the person receiving them.

Colton had been running his marriage and his fatherhood on autopilot speech and calling it honesty, as if unfiltered were the same as authentic. It's not. A man who thinks carefully about how he answers a client but not about how he answers his wife has his priorities backward, whatever he tells himself about being "just tired." Presence is measured not just by showing up to dinner, but by whether the words that come out of him once he's there are actually chosen for the person across the table, or just whatever came to mind fastest.

He started small: putting the phone face-down before Celeste finished a sentence, and answering his son's questions like they mattered as much as a client's would. It felt stiff at first, almost performed. It stopped feeling that way once he noticed his son starting to talk more, not less, now that someone was actually answering him.

**Today:** Notice the next flat or careless answer you're about to give someone in your own house — a spouse, a kid, a parent. Stop, and give them the same careful, considered response you'd give a client or a boss.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've been giving my family the tired, careless version of my words while saving my best effort for people I'm trying to impress. Season my speech at home the way You'd have me season it anywhere else. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

COLOSSIANS 4:6
