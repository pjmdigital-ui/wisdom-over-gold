# Day 19 — Hope in the Darkest Room

Craig spent the night after his second DUI arrest on a steel bench in a holding cell, one blanket, a stainless toilet in the corner, his phone in a plastic bag with the rest of what had been in his pockets that afternoon. He was thirty-four. His license was gone. His job was, at minimum, in real question, and his wife's last text before they took his phone had said she meant it this time — that this was the one he didn't get to talk his way back from.

Sitting there at two in the morning, Craig didn't reach for hope, because hope had started to feel like something reserved for men whose lives weren't currently a mess. His, at that exact moment, plainly was. Whatever came next, in his mind, would have to arrive before there was anything worth hoping in.

St. Paul had his own long list of cells, and he doesn't write from outside one waiting for it to end before he says this. In 2 Corinthians 4:17 he calls his suffering a light, brief trouble that's producing a weight of glory far beyond anything that could be set against it. He doesn't call the affliction good, and he doesn't minimize it — he calls it light and brief against something it's producing that outlasts it, which is a strange thing to say from inside a cell unless hope was never actually waiting on the door to open first.

**On St. Maximilian Kolbe:** In July 1941, guards at Auschwitz selected ten men to starve to death after a prisoner escaped the camp. Kolbe, a Franciscan priest, was not one of the ten. He stepped forward and asked to take the place of a stranger who had cried out for the wife and children he was about to lose. For two weeks in an underground bunker with no food or water, witnesses said Kolbe led the other men in prayer and hymns rather than despair, and was among the last still conscious. Nothing about his circumstances improved before his hope did. He carried it in with him, into the one room where it would have made the most sense to leave it behind.

Craig's cell was not a starvation bunker, and his night wasn't nearly as severe as Kolbe's two weeks. Both stories point to the same claim: hope, in Paul's letter and in Kolbe's bunker alike, was already present in the room, available before anything about the circumstances changed.

**Today:** Name the room you're actually in tonight, whatever it is, and make one call tomorrow morning you've been avoiding — a sponsor, a counselor, your wife — before you try to solve anything else about the situation.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, tonight doesn't look anything like a place where hope should live, and I've been telling myself it can't, not yet. Teach me that You're already here with me before anything about my circumstances changes. Give me the courage to make the call I've been avoiding. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

2 CORINTHIANS 4:17
