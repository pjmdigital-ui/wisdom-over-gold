# Day 1 — The God of Hope

Peter made the last mortgage payment on a Tuesday afternoon, three years ahead of schedule, after a decade of extra principal payments and a spreadsheet he checked more often than he'd ever admit out loud. He'd pictured this moment for years — signing the payoff confirmation, maybe pouring something to celebrate, finally exhaling all the way down. Instead he closed the laptop, looked around the kitchen his family had eaten ten thousand meals in, and felt almost nothing. Not empty. Just the same low hum of "what's next" that had been running under everything for as long as he could remember.

He told his wife, Renata, that night, half expecting the news to land like something bigger than it did. She was genuinely happy for him, and he was grateful for that. But driving to work the next morning he caught himself already recalculating: if the house is paid off, the real cushion is eighteen months of expenses saved. Once that's built, then he'd actually relax. He'd said some version of that sentence about the promotion two years ago, and about the year the last kid finished daycare, and about a half-dozen other lines he'd crossed convinced peace was waiting exactly one finish line further out.

*Peace is what happens after I've handled enough. I just haven't handled enough yet.* Peter had never said that sentence to anyone, but it was the sentence running his whole adult life, the one that had just turned a debt-free house into base camp instead of a summit.

St. Paul prays a very different picture over the church at Rome near the end of his letter, in Romans 15:13: he asks that the God who is Himself the source of hope would fill them with all the joy and peace that come simply from trusting Him, until they overflow with hope by the Holy Spirit's power. Notice what Paul doesn't pray. He doesn't ask God to first resolve their circumstances and then send peace as the reward. He addresses God by that title — not as the One who eventually supplies the conditions for hope once enough boxes are checked, but as its source, available to fill a man now, in whatever kitchen he's standing in. The joy and peace in that verse belong to present-tense trust, not to some future arrival.

This is the ground this whole month stands on. Most men run some version of Peter's math without ever writing it down: hit this number, clear this debt, get through this season, and peace will finally show up on schedule. It rarely does. Circumstances resolve into new circumstances. The mortgage gets paid and the retirement account becomes the new finish line. Hope treated as a future reward for present striving turns out to be no hope at all — just anxiety with a due date. Real hope has to be rooted somewhere circumstances can't reach it, or it's just optimism, waiting to be disappointed by the next thing that doesn't go as planned.

**Today:** Name one thing you've told yourself will finally bring you peace once you cross it — the number, the season, the finish line. Then do one deliberate act of peace before you get there: say a short prayer of thanks for exactly where you are right now, out loud, with no condition attached to it.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've been treating peace like something I earn by handling enough, and I'm tired of chasing a finish line that keeps moving. Thank You for being the God of hope right now, not the God waiting for my circumstances to catch up. Fill me with real peace in believing, today, exactly as things stand. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

ROMANS 15:13
