# Day 11 — The God of All Comfort

Ulysses heard about Rutger's father two days after it happened — a heart attack, sudden, no warning, at sixty-one. Ulysses's first instinct was to help, so he did what he was good at: he found a grief counselor's number, a checklist for handling an estate, an article on "the five stages" he'd skimmed once years ago. He texted all of it to Rutger within the hour, along with *let me know if you need anything*, and felt like he'd done something real.

He didn't call. He didn't drive over. Sitting across from a man who'd just lost his father felt like standing in a room with no furniture — nothing to do with his hands, nothing useful to say, a silence he didn't know how to fill. Sending resources was concrete. Showing up empty-handed, with nothing to offer but his presence, felt like showing up with nothing at all. Ulysses told himself Rutger probably wanted space anyway. Grief was private. He'd reach out again once there was something more useful to send.

St. Paul opens his second letter to the Corinthians with a different picture of what comfort actually is and where it comes from: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and God of all comfort, who comforts us in all our affliction, so that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction, with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God" (2 Corinthians 1:3-4, RSV-CE). Notice what's missing from that description. No checklist. No five stages. The comfort Paul describes moves from God to a person to another person, and the delivery mechanism is presence, not information — one man who has been comforted simply being with another man who hasn't yet.

Ulysses had it backward. He'd treated his own discomfort — not knowing what to say — as the problem to solve, when the actual assignment had nothing to do with having the right words. Rutger didn't need a checklist. He needed someone willing to sit in the room with no furniture and stay anyway. A text with links attached can look like care from a safe distance while functioning as an exit from the harder thing being asked of him.

He drove over that Friday without a plan. Rutger opened the door, looked surprised, and let him in. They didn't talk about the funeral arrangements or the estate. They sat at the kitchen table for an hour, mostly quiet, Ulysses asking a question here and there and mostly just staying. Nothing got fixed. Rutger later told him that Friday was the first day that week he hadn't felt entirely alone in it.

**Today:** Think of someone in your life carrying real loss right now. Don't send them a link or advice. Go be with them in person, even if you have no idea what to say once you're there.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've hidden behind advice and information when what someone I love actually needed was my presence. Teach me to sit in the hard, wordless room instead of managing it from a safe distance. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
