# Day 17 — Grief at Christmas

I found the ornament by accident, digging through the plastic bin for the string lights, my father's handwriting still on the masking-tape tag he'd stuck to the hook decades ago: *Dad's star — DO NOT LOSE.* He's been gone since March, and somehow I made it through the spring and most of the summer without this particular ambush waiting for me. Christmas is different. Christmas has a box in the garage with his handwriting still in it.

My kids are excited in the uncomplicated way kids are excited in December, counting down, making lists, and some part of me resents how easy that is for them this year and loves them for it in the same breath. My wife keeps checking on me with her eyes across the room, the way you check on someone you're not sure is actually all right. Everyone at the office wished me a merry Christmas like it's the easiest sentence in the world to receive, and I said thank you every time, and every time it cost me something to say it.

Am I even allowed to feel this bad in a month that's supposed to be about hope?

St. Paul never tells the Thessalonians grieving their dead not to grieve. He tells them something more specific: "But we would not have you ignorant, brethren, concerning those who are asleep, that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13, RSV-CE). Read slowly, it stops sounding like permission to skip grief and starts sounding like something else entirely — a description of a different kind of grief, not an absence of one. Paul assumes his readers will grieve. He's naming what makes their grief different from grief with nothing underneath it: hope, sitting right alongside the sadness, not replacing it and not waiting politely for the sadness to finish first.

That's a harder thing to hold than a clean before-and-after, and I don't think it resolves neatly by Christmas morning. Hope doesn't make my father's handwriting on that tag hurt less. It doesn't mean I should try to feel something different at the table this year than what I actually feel. It means the ache and the hope stay in the room together, both true on the same day, sometimes in the same hour, neither one waiting for the other to leave first. I don't have to get to the other side of the grief before I'm allowed anywhere near the hope. They were already sharing the table, the first Christmas my father wasn't at it.

**Today:** Don't perform being fine today, for your kids, your spouse, or the relatives who ask how you're doing. Tell one person the truth about this Christmas, without adding "but I'm okay" at the end.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, this first Christmas without my father sits heavier than I let on to the people around me, and I don't want to keep pretending otherwise for the sake of everyone else's cheer. Let me hold my grief and the hope You've given me at the same time, without rushing past either one. Be near to my family this year the way You are near to me in this. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
