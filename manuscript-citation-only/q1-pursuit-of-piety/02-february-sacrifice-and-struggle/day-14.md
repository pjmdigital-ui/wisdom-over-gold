# Day 14 — Turning Struggle Into Offering

Sunday evening Mass, and Nate knelt down in the pew with a week he was glad to see the back of. The shop had lost a contract they'd been counting on for the fall. He and his daughter had gone another round about her phone that ended with her slamming a door. His back, thrown out doing something stupid on a Tuesday, still wasn't right. None of it was catastrophic. All of it had worn him down by Friday to the point where his wife had asked, gently, if he was okay, and he'd answered "fine" in the tone that means the opposite.

Growing up, he'd heard the phrase "offer it up" from his grandmother constantly, usually about something small, a headache, a boring chore, and he'd always taken it as a throwaway line, the Catholic version of "walk it off." Kneeling there, he realized he'd never actually done it, not once. He gutted through hard weeks. He never offered them.

Gutting a week out means gripping the hardship tighter and pushing through it on willpower until it's over. Offering it means handing it to God before it's over, on purpose, by name, and trusting Him to use it, the way St. Paul describes to the Romans. In view of everything God has already done for them in His mercy, he urges them to offer their bodies, their whole, ordinary, everyday selves, back to Him as a living sacrifice, set apart and pleasing to Him, which is the truest way they can worship (Romans 12:1).

Paul is talking about a living sacrifice that's still living: it goes to work Monday, still has the bad back, still has the daughter who slammed the door. This is bigger than grand religious acts. Presenting it means the ordinary week itself, cost and all, becomes the thing laid on the altar rather than something separate from it. That's a full week's reframe available to any man kneeling in a pew on a Sunday night with nothing dramatic to report, just a normal amount of hard.

Nate had spent the whole week, without naming it this way, learning the difference between suffering he survives alone and suffering he hands over. This was the same lesson in its smallest, most Tuesday-and-Friday form. The shop, the door, the back, none of it needed to go to waste. It needed to be offered, specifically, on purpose, before Mass ended and Monday started again.

He thought about the harder stretches of the past couple weeks: the crisis he couldn't explain, the burden he'd been carrying alone, the love that cost him sleep he didn't get back. None of it had come with a tidy resolution, and the hardship itself hadn't gotten any smaller. What had changed was what he did with it, once he stopped treating it as something to merely survive.

**Today:** Before you go to bed tonight, name the hardest thing from this past week out loud and say: *I'm offering this week to You as my spiritual worship, not just gutting it out.* Then let it go, don't replay it again tomorrow.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, this week wore me down in small ways I usually just push through without bringing to You. Take this ordinary hard week and use it for something, the way only You can. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

ROMANS 12:1
