# Day 4 — The Lie That Good Men Don't Suffer

The layoff came on a Thursday, no warning, twelve years of loyalty ended in a fifteen-minute video call. Marcus took it hard, but what surprised him more than the loss itself was the timing. This past year had been the most disciplined stretch of his spiritual life — Mass most weekdays, not just Sundays, tithing off the gross instead of the net for the first time, up at 5:30 to pray before the house woke up. He'd been doing the work. He'd been, by any measure he could point to, trying.

By the second week of unemployment he'd stopped setting the 5:30 alarm. Daily Mass became Sunday Mass, then Sunday Mass with a headache he didn't quite have. He couldn't have told you why, exactly — nothing had changed in what he believed. What had changed was a low, persistent question he never said out loud to his wife or his pastor: if he'd actually been getting this right, wouldn't things have gone differently? He started running the tape backward, looking for the sin that must have caused this, the corner he must have cut. Good men who do the work don't end up folding laundry at eleven on a Tuesday morning with no job to go to. Something about that math didn't add up, and rather than question the math, Marcus started questioning himself.

Christ speaks to this directly, to men who'd staked everything on following Him and were about to watch Him die. He doesn't promise them an easier road for their faithfulness. He tells them He's said all this so that they can have real peace in Him, because out in the world they will run into real trouble — and then, in the same breath, He tells them to take heart, since He has already conquered the world that trouble comes from (John 16:33). Read that closely: trouble there is simply assumed, spoken to the most faithful men in the room, the night before the worst of it, never framed as a sign something had gone wrong in a disciple's life. Peace was promised alongside hardship, underneath it, in spite of it — not as a reward for hardship's absence.

The unspoken belief Marcus had been operating on — that real faithfulness should buy him a smoother road — is a bargain he made up, one scripture never authorized, and then blamed himself for God not honoring. The men in the Gospels who suffered most were, almost without exception, the ones who'd given the most. Faithfulness is what carries a man through the storm still standing, a different and better thing than a toll that gets him out of it.

**Today:** If you're carrying a hardship right now and privately wondering what you did to deserve it, write down the honest version of the belief you've been running on. Underneath it, write: *Tribulation is proof I'm in the world Christ already told me to expect, not proof I'm failing God.* Take that thought back into prayer tonight instead of the self-diagnosis you've been running instead.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've been weighing my hardship against my faithfulness and coming up short, as though the two should have canceled out. Remind me that tribulation was never a verdict on how I'm doing with You, and that Your peace was promised in the middle of it, not instead of it. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

JOHN 16:33
