# Day 3 — Numbing the Ache

The parent-teacher conference ran fourteen minutes. That was long enough for Chris to hear that his son is failing two classes. His teachers think the boy has "checked out." This has apparently been building for a month, and nobody at home noticed. Chris drove home with the radio off. He said almost nothing at dinner. By eight o'clock he was sitting in his truck in the driveway, engine still ticking, phone in his hand.

He tells himself he's just checking the score. Forty minutes later he's still there, reading stats, watching highlights, scrolling a group chat argument about a trade that doesn't even matter to him. The engine is off, the porch light is on, and his wife is inside probably wondering where he is. This isn't the first night this month that's happened. Some nights it's the phone. Some nights it's two beers that turn into four once the kids are down. It's a different door, but it leads to the same room: anywhere that isn't the conversation he actually needs to have, with his son and with himself.

What is he actually trying to not feel? It isn't the failing grades themselves. Those are just numbers, fixable with a tutor and a schedule. What he's avoiding runs deeper than that. His son has been struggling for a month, and nobody noticed. Chris was too distracted, too tired, or too checked out himself to catch it. That's a problem he doesn't want to hold, and no screen or two beers can actually solve it.

St. Peter writes to Christians who have real reason to want out of what they're facing. He doesn't tell them the pain is a mistake to be managed around. He tells them plainly not to be shocked by the trial burning through their lives right now, as if it were some strange exception to how following God was supposed to go (1 Peter 4:12). It isn't strange. It isn't a glitch in an otherwise well-run life. Scripture treats the ordeal as normal, even expected, because it's doing something a comfortable life never could. It's proving what's in a man under pressure, when nobody's watching him perform.

Every hour Chris spends in the truck is an hour he's not proving anything, one way or the other. He's just postponing the proof. The ache doesn't go anywhere while he scrolls. It's still exactly where he left it, waiting on the other side of the front door, a little heavier for having been carried alone a while longer.

**Today:** Notice the next time you reach for a screen, a drink, or any other reliable exit the moment something hard surfaces. Before you take it, name what you're avoiding, and say: *This ache is proving something in me. Numbing it doesn't remove it — it just delays what I have to face.* Then go do the harder thing instead. Walk back inside, start the conversation, or sit with it for ten minutes without reaching for the exit.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I know the places I go to avoid feeling what's right in front of me, and I know they don't make the ache smaller. Give me the strength to walk back into the hard conversation instead of away from it, trusting that You're refining something in me I can't see yet. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 PETER 4:12
