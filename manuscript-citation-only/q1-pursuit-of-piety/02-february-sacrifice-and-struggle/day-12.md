# Day 12 — Carrying What You Can't Put Down

Frank's father stopped recognizing him in March and started again in April, like it came and went with the weather, though the doctors had told him Alzheimer's didn't actually work that way. Frank drove to the memory care facility three evenings a week after work, sat with a man who sometimes called him by his own brother's name, and got home to his own family an hour later than he'd promised, again.

There was no finish line. No surgery date, no treatment that would end this, no month he could circle on the calendar and tell his wife "it'll be easier after that." Six years in, he was still doing the math on how much longer this could go — his father was seventy-one and healthy otherwise, and Frank had done enough reading to know that could mean a long road with the mind gone well before the body followed.

He'd started telling himself something he never would have said to another man's face: *I'm not strong enough to carry this indefinitely. Something has to end for me to keep going.* It felt honest. It also assumed he was supposed to be doing this alone, indefinitely, with no help — which was the real lie underneath the exhaustion, not the burden itself.

St. Paul writes to the Galatians, "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2, RSV-CE). One another's — not "figure out how to bear it alone until it breaks you." A few verses later Paul says something that sounds almost like a contradiction: each man will have to bear his own load. Read together, the two verses draw a real line, not a contradiction. Some things are a man's alone to carry — his own choices, his own work, his own soul before God. But a father's decline, a wife's illness, a burden this size — those are the ones Scripture is explicit are meant to be shared, split across a family, a parish, a brother who shows up on the Tuesday a man can't. Frank had been treating a bear-one-another's-burdens situation like a bear-your-own-load one, and it was crushing him for no reason except pride and habit.

**On St. Padre Pio:** For over fifty years Padre Pio bore the visible wounds of Christ's Passion in his own hands and feet, in pain that never fully left him, while spending up to sixteen hours a day hearing confessions — taking on, in a real sense, the moral weight other men had been holding alone for years. He didn't manage his suffering privately and hide it. He let people bring him theirs.

**Today:** Call one person — a sibling, a friend, a priest — and ask them to take one specific piece of this burden off you this week, even something small like one evening's visit. Say to yourself first: *This isn't only mine to carry. God built it to be shared.*

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've been trying to carry this alone because asking for help feels like failing, and I know that's pride talking, not strength. Send me someone to share this load with, and soften my heart to actually let them. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
