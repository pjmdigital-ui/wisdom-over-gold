# Day 1 — The Instinct to Avoid

Every Saturday morning, Dave pulls the mower out of the garage. He cuts the yard in the same pattern each time — back forty first, then the side yard, then the strip along the front walk. He never touches the eight feet of grass along the fence line he shares with the Petersons. Six months ago, his neighbor cornered him right there about a property line. The talk got heated fast, and both men said things they shouldn't have. Since then Dave mows everywhere except that strip. He tells himself he'll get to it "once things cool down." The grass there is almost two feet tall now. It's gone to seed, and you can see it from the street.

Dave knows exactly what happened and exactly what needs cutting. He isn't hiding that from himself. What he hasn't looked at is the pattern underneath it. There's a fence. There's a phone call he owes his brother. There's a talk with his oldest about grades that keeps sliding to "this weekend." These are small, separate problems, but Dave treats them all the same way. He routes around whatever feels uncomfortable and calls the rest of the yard handled.

Dave works hard. He's not lazy by any stretch. He'll spend three hours edging and blowing off the driveway rather than spend three minutes at that fence line. Avoiding things actually takes effort. It's a skill he's gotten good at without ever deciding to practice it.

Underneath it all sits one belief he's never questioned: if he can just route around the hard thing long enough, it stops being his problem. The problem doesn't actually get solved. It just stops feeling urgent, worn down by enough time and distance. Dave is counting on comfort to finish a job that facing the problem was supposed to do.

Scripture says almost the opposite. St. James writes to men living under real pressure. He doesn't tell them to manage their way around trials. He tells them to treat every kind of trial as a reason for joy, because testing builds real perseverance in a man. And when that perseverance finishes its work, a man comes out the other side whole, missing nothing he needs (James 1:2-4). Joy is a strange word to put next to trial. James isn't describing a feeling a man forces himself to have. He's describing what happens when someone walks straight into the hard thing instead of around it. The trial is doing something. Route around it, and you also route around what it was building in you.

The grass by the fence stands for something bigger than the grass. Every strip a man leaves uncut — every call he skips, every conversation he keeps rescheduling — is a piece of his life left unlived. It doesn't disappear. It just keeps growing until the whole yard shows it.

**Today:** Name one specific thing you've been mowing around this week — a call, a conversation, a task you keep calling "not urgent." Say out loud: *Avoiding this doesn't make it smaller. Walking into it is how it finally gets finished.* Then do the smallest possible piece of it before the day ends. Make the call, write the first sentence, or spend five minutes at the fence line.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, You see exactly what I've been routing around this week, even when I've dressed it up as being busy with other things. Give me the courage to walk toward the hard thing instead of past it, and let me trust that You are working in me through it in a way that avoidance never could. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

JAMES 1:2-4
