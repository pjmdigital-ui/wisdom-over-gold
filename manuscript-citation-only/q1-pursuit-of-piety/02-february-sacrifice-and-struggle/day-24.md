# Day 24 — When You Want to Quit

Gareth said yes to leading the Tuesday morning men's group back in September — six guys, six a.m., a full year, his own idea. Eleven months of showing up before the sun came up seemed impossible in September and even more impossible now, in week forty. Two guys stopped coming in December. The other four still show up, but Gareth's the one who has to have something ready every single week, and last night he sat in his truck outside his house and drafted a text to the group: "Hey guys — think we should take a break till spring, reassess after the holidays." He didn't send it. He read it four times and left it sitting there.

He told himself it wasn't quitting. A break wasn't quitting. Reassessing wasn't quitting. He'd already given eleven months when plenty of guys wouldn't have given eleven days — didn't that count for something? The math felt airtight at eleven p.m. in a cold truck.

That's the shape burnout usually takes with a real commitment: not a clean break but a slow renegotiation of the terms nobody else agreed to. Gareth didn't want to quit outright — quitting outright would have meant admitting it to four other men's faces. He wanted to soften the finish line until it moved somewhere he could actually reach, without ever calling it what it was.

St. Paul wrote to the Galatians from inside exactly this kind of fatigue — a young church worn down by real, ongoing effort — and didn't offer them an exit ramp: he tells them not to give up doing good, because if they don't lose heart, the harvest will come in its own season (Galatians 6:9). Notice what he doesn't say. He doesn't say the weariness isn't real, or that Gareth should feel differently than he does. He says don't grow weary in the doing — keep doing it while weary, because the harvest is on a timeline Gareth doesn't control and can't see from week forty.

A commitment made to four other men at six a.m. isn't Gareth's alone to renegotiate at eleven p.m. by himself. If the terms genuinely need to change, that's a conversation with the group, out loud, not a private downgrade he talks himself into and calls a break.

**Today:** Delete the draft text. Say out loud: *I said a year, and I'm finishing what I said, even tired.* If the schedule genuinely needs to change, raise it with the group directly this week — not as an unspoken way out, but as an honest ask.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I'm tired of a commitment I made freely, and part of me is looking for an unspoken way out that doesn't look like quitting. Give me the strength to finish what I said I'd do, and the honesty to ask directly if something truly needs to change. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

GALATIANS 6:9
