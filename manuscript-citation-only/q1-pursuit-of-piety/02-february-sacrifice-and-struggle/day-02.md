# Day 2 — The Cost of Comfort

Rich owns a small commercial print shop. He has twelve employees and still owes money on the building. One account, Whitfield Logistics, brings in almost a third of his revenue. But Whitfield pays on ninety-day terms and demands rush jobs with no notice. Once you count the overtime it costs to keep up, the margin is close to zero. His operations manager has told him twice this year: renegotiate the terms or cut the account loose. Whitfield's chaos is crowding out three smaller clients who'd actually be profitable to grow. Rich has run the numbers himself. He knows his manager is right.

He still hasn't made the call. Every quarter he tells himself the same line: *Once things settle down, I'll deal with it.* Things never settle down, because servicing Whitfield is the reason they don't. The shop hasn't grown in two years. Rich tells his wife he's "in a stable season." That was the lie he told himself first, and now she's only hearing the finished version.

He's rehearsed the conversation with Whitfield a dozen times in his head, in the shower, in the truck. He knows exactly what he'd say. What stops him is that staying the same size feels safer than growing, even though the same size is slowly losing ground. As long as nothing changes, nothing can go visibly wrong on his watch. Most days, that feels like enough.

Hebrews wasn't written for a boardroom, but the wisdom still fits. No discipline feels good while a man is in the middle of it. In the moment it just hurts. But for those who let it do its work on them, it later produces a settled, right way of living, something that looks like peace once you're standing on the other side of it (Hebrews 12:11). Discipline here isn't punishment. It's the hard correction that reshapes something so it can grow, more like a pruning cut than a penalty. The decision sitting on Rich's desk is the thing the business actually needs to become what it could be. It's a pruning cut, not a threat to what's already there.

Comfort has a cost most men never put on a balance sheet, because it shows up as nothing happening rather than as a loss. No new hires. No new equipment. No client roster that looks different in year three than it did in year one. A company that refuses correction stays the exact same shape forever. In a market that keeps moving, that's its own kind of decline. It's just a slower one, and slower makes it easier to live with.

**Today:** Write down the decision you've been calling "stable" that's really just been avoided. Say it plainly: *What I've been calling safety is the failure I can't see yet.* Then set an actual date this week, on the calendar, not "soon," to have the conversation, make the call, or take the first concrete step toward it.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, You know the decision I've been calling patience when it's really been fear of what might change. Give me the courage to accept the discipline You're offering instead of the comfort I keep choosing, and trust that what feels like loss now is how You're growing something better. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
