# Day 2 — Faking Certainty

Monday mornings I run a fifteen-minute huddle with my crew before the trucks roll out — safety reminders, the day's jobs, whatever needs saying. Three weeks ago one of my techs, Marcus, stood up in the middle of it and told the room his brother had just been diagnosed with the same cancer that took their dad four years ago. The room went quiet, and six guys looked at me, because I'm the one who prays before we eat at the company picnic, the one with the fish sticker on his truck. I'm "the Christian boss."

So I did what I always do. I said something about how God is faithful, how we'd keep his brother in prayer, how good could come even out of this. I meant every word as I was building the sentence — the trouble is I've been asking a version of that same question about my own father for two years, ever since his stroke, and I don't know if I believe what I just told Marcus or if I've just gotten good at saying it.

Here's the question I don't let myself sit with: what happens if these men ever find out I'm not sure either? I've built my whole role around being the steady one, the guy with the answer, and somewhere along the way "steady" turned into "certain," which isn't the same thing. I perform the second one because I think the first one requires it.

Scripture doesn't ask me to be certain on someone else's behalf — it asks me to bring mercy to their doubt the way I'd want mercy brought to mine. St. Jude's short letter, mostly a warning, ends with a line easy to skip past — in Jude 1:22, he tells his readers to have mercy on those who are still wrestling with doubt, to stay engaged with them rather than write them off. Notice the posture. Not correct them. Not out-argue them until they stop asking hard questions. Show mercy — walk alongside, reason with, stay in the room. That's what my guys needed from me in that huddle, and it's what I've refused to ask for myself.

The performance costs more than it looks like from the outside. Every time I hand a man certainty I don't have, I widen the gap between what he sees and what's true, and I make it harder for him to ever bring me his own doubt, because I've shown him a boss who doesn't carry any. Marcus doesn't need a leader with no questions. He needs one honest enough to say some days are harder to believe than others, and still show up.

**Today:** Before your next conversation where you'd normally perform certainty, tell one person — your spouse, a friend, your crew — one true thing: *I don't have to have this all figured out to still be faithful.* Say it in those words, or close to them, out loud, to someone who can hear it.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I have been handing people a certainty I don't carry myself, and it's made it harder for me to be honest with anyone, including You. Give me the courage to let the people I lead see a faith that's real instead of one that's just steady-looking. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
