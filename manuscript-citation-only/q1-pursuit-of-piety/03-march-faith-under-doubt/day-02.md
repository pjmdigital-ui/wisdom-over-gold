# Day 2 — Faking Certainty

Monday mornings I run a fifteen-minute huddle with my crew before the trucks roll out. Safety reminders, the day's jobs, whatever needs saying. Three weeks ago one of my techs, Marcus, stood up in the middle of it. He told the room his brother had just been diagnosed with the same cancer that took their dad four years ago. The room went quiet. Six guys looked at me. I'm the one who prays before we eat at the company picnic. I'm the one with the fish sticker on his truck. I'm "the Christian boss."

So I did what I always do. I said something about how God is faithful. I said we'd keep his brother in prayer. I said good could come even out of this. I meant every word as I said it. But here's the truth: I've been asking that same question about my own father for two years, ever since his stroke. I don't know if I believe what I just told Marcus, or if I've just gotten good at saying it.

Here's the question I don't let myself sit with: what happens if these men ever find out I'm not sure either? I've built my whole role around being the steady one, the guy with the answer. Somewhere along the way "steady" turned into "certain." Those aren't the same thing. I perform certainty because I think steady requires it.

Scripture doesn't ask me to be certain on someone else's behalf. It asks me to bring mercy to their doubt, the way I'd want mercy brought to mine. St. Jude's short letter is mostly a warning, but it ends with a line that's easy to skip past. In Jude 1:22, he tells his readers to have mercy on those who are still wrestling with doubt. Stay engaged with them instead of writing them off. Notice what that doesn't say. It doesn't say correct them. It doesn't say argue with them until they stop asking hard questions. It says show mercy. Walk alongside them. Stay in the room. That's what my guys needed from me in that huddle. It's what I've refused to ask for myself.

This kind of performing costs more than it looks like. Every time I hand a man certainty I don't have, I widen the gap between what he sees and what's true. I make it harder for him to ever bring me his own doubt, because I've shown him a boss who doesn't carry any. Marcus doesn't need a leader with no questions. He needs one honest enough to admit some days are harder to believe than others, and still show up anyway.

**Today:** Before your next conversation where you'd normally perform certainty, tell one person — your spouse, a friend, your crew — one true thing: *I don't have to have this all figured out to still be faithful.* Say it in those words, or close to them, out loud, to someone who can hear it.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I have been handing people a certainty I don't carry myself, and it's made it harder for me to be honest with anyone, including You. Give me the courage to let the people I lead see a faith that's real instead of one that's just steady-looking. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
