# Day 27 — Thankful in the Waiting

Nate drove to the hospital not knowing what to say and left having said the wrong thing. His friend Josh and his wife had lost their third pregnancy in two years, this one at fourteen weeks. Nate sat with him in the waiting room chairs that never seem built for actual sitting, ran out of things to say after about four minutes, and reached for the only thing he had left: "I know it doesn't feel like it, man, but God works all things together for good." Josh nodded, the way you nod at something you've decided not to argue with, and looked back down at his hands. Something in his face had closed. Nate felt it happen and didn't know how to undo it.

He'd meant it as comfort. It landed as a period at the end of a sentence Josh wasn't finished with. Nate had used a true thing as a way to stop sitting in an untrue-feeling moment — to make the silence make sense faster than silence was willing to.

That's the belief worth naming plainly, because it's common enough to be almost invisible: *if I say the verse, the pain should resolve, or at least go quiet.* Men reach for Romans 8:28 like a tool for ending a hard conversation instead of a truth for enduring one. And when the good it promises doesn't show up on any timeline you can see — when the third pregnancy doesn't become a fourth that holds — the verse starts to feel like it lied, or worse, like it was never really true, just something people say.

Read what the verse actually says, not what it gets flattened into: "We know that in everything God works for good with those who love him, who are called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28, RSV-CE). It doesn't say you'll see the good. It doesn't say the good arrives on a schedule that makes the loss make sense, or that the loss stops being a loss once the good shows up somewhere down the line. It says God is working — present tense, ongoing, often invisible — inside a situation that is genuinely, unambiguously bad. The verse was never a claim that pain isn't pain. Job never got an explanation for his suffering and was never told it didn't count as suffering; he was met by God in the middle of it. This verse works the same way — a promise that God hasn't left the room while the loss is still fresh and still real, offered without pretending to tie a bow around it.

Nate's mistake wasn't believing Romans 8:28. It was using it to end a conversation instead of staying in one. Josh didn't need the verse quoted at him. He needed someone willing to sit in the waiting room without rushing him toward a resolution neither of them could see yet.

**Today:** Think of someone you've comforted with a version of "it'll all work out" when what they needed was your presence, not your explanation. Call or text them this: *I don't have an answer for why this happened, but I'm not going anywhere, and I believe God isn't either.* Then follow through — show up again this week, no verse required.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've used Your promises to end hard conversations instead of staying inside them with the people I love. Teach me to trust that You are working for good even when I can't see it or explain it to anyone else. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
