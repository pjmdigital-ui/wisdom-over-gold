# Day 6 — The Faith of Your Father Isn't Automatically Yours

My son asked me last Sunday, on the drive home from Mass, why we believe bread and wine actually become the body and blood of Christ instead of just standing for it. He's eleven. It's a fair question, exactly the kind a kid who's paying attention should ask. About four seconds into my answer, I realized I didn't have one. I said something about "that's just what the Church teaches" and changed the radio station before he could ask a follow-up.

I've gone to Mass nearly every Sunday of my life. I went through CCD, made my confirmation, got married in the Church, had my kids baptized there. By every visible measure I'm a practicing Catholic. Until that car ride I would have told you my faith was solid. That four-second silence taught me something different. Most of what I call my faith is really my father's faith. I've worn it like a jacket I never took off long enough to check if it fit. Inherited, assumed, never actually examined on my own terms. I know the words. I've never done the work of finding out for myself why they're true.

That gap between inherited and owned doesn't announce itself. It hides behind decades of regular Mass attendance and looks, from the outside, exactly like real faith. Then an eleven-year-old asks a direct question, and there's nothing underneath the habit to answer with.

St. Paul names the alternative when he writes to a young man named Timothy about faith passed down through his family. In 2 Timothy 1:5, Paul recalls the sincere faith that lived first in Timothy's grandmother Lois, then his mother Eunice. He tells Timothy he's convinced that same faith now lives in him too. Notice what Paul doesn't say. He doesn't credit Timothy's faith to Lois and Eunice as though it simply belongs to them, handed to him unchanged. He says it now dwells in Timothy — present tense, his own, having taken root in him specifically, even though it started somewhere else. Inherited and owned aren't opposites. But one has to become the other, and that only happens through some kind of deliberate work.

I can't answer for my father's faith. I can only find out whether mine actually holds up when I go looking for the reasons behind it instead of just the habit of it.

**Today:** Pick one thing you've believed your whole life without ever asking why the Church teaches it — the Eucharist, confession, whatever your son's version of the question is. Look up the actual answer this week: a priest, a solid book, the Catechism itself. Then tell someone, out loud: *I looked into this faith instead of just inheriting it, and now it's truly mine.*

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I have been living off a faith I inherited without ever making it fully my own, and my son's question showed me exactly where the gap is. Give me the discipline to go find the real answers instead of coasting on habit. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

2 TIMOTHY 1:5
