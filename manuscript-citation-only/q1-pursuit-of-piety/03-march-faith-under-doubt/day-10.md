# Day 10 — Doubt That Leads Somewhere

I used to be the guy in my men's group who poked holes. Somebody would share something that had moved them — a homily, a passage, a moment of answered prayer — and I'd have a question ready before they finished talking. Not a real question. A gotcha. I got good at finding the soft spot in whatever someone just said and pressing on it until they backed off.

One night after group, driving home, I caught myself replaying the exchange with something close to pride. I'd made a guy who'd just shared something vulnerable stumble over his words in front of six other men, and some part of me felt sharper for it — like I'd won something. It took me a few miles to admit what that feeling really was. I hadn't asked a single one of those questions because I wanted an answer. I asked them because being unconvinced had become the easiest way to look like the smartest man in the room.

That's a different thing than doubt. Doubt goes looking. What I'd been doing was closer to a posture — arms crossed, nothing's getting past me — dressed up as intellectual honesty so I didn't have to call it what it was: pride wearing a lab coat.

Nathanael, when Philip told him they'd found the Messiah in Jesus of Nazareth, didn't hide his skepticism — in John 1:46, he asks whether anything good could possibly come out of a town like Nazareth. That's a real objection — Nazareth had no reputation worth mentioning. But look at what Philip says back, and what Nathanael does next. Philip doesn't argue him out of it. He says, "Come and see." And Nathanael goes. He puts his skepticism to the test instead of just repeating it at dinner parties for the rest of his life. That's the difference between doubt that leads somewhere and cynicism that just leads back to itself, applauding.

Real inquiry risks being wrong. It goes and looks. Cynicism never risks anything — it just collects reasons to stay unconvinced and calls the collection wisdom. I'd built a version of faith-adjacent identity that let me sit at every men's group, ask every hard question, and never once go looking for an answer, because looking might mean I'd have to change something.

**Today:** Think of the last real objection you raised about the faith — not to sound sharp, but one you truly meant. Instead of sitting on it, go find out: read something substantive on it, or ask a priest or a man you trust who's studied it. Then write and say out loud: *I'll go and see instead of just staying unconvinced.* Make one specific move today — a call, a search, a question asked in person — toward finding out.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've used sharp questions to look wise instead of using them to find You, and I'm asking You to show me the difference in my own heart. Give me the humility to go and see instead of just staying comfortably unconvinced. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

JOHN 1:46
