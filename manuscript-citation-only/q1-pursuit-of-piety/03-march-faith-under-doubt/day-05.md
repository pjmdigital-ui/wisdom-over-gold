# Day 5 — When God Feels Silent

Gary has prayed for his son Michael every night for four years — since the night Michael, twenty-two then, packed a duffel bag and walked out after an argument neither of them has ever fully apologized for. Four years of the same short prayer at the kitchen table after his wife goes up to bed: bring him home, God, or at least let me know he's safe. Four years of half-answered texts, missed Christmases, a birthday card returned unopened once.

Nothing has changed. Gary doesn't know if that's a fact about God or a fact about Michael, and after this long he's stopped being able to tell the difference. What he does know is that the praying itself has started to feel less like reaching out and more like talking to a closed door — not angry exactly, just tired, the specific tiredness of asking for the same thing so many times that the words stop feeling like they're going anywhere.

He hasn't said this to anyone, including God directly. He keeps praying because stopping feels like giving up on Michael, but somewhere in the last year the prayer stopped being honest. It became a habit performed instead of a plea actually meant, because Gary has decided, without ever quite deciding it, that telling God the silence is hard would sound like accusing Him of something.

Scripture has no such hesitation. The Psalms are full of exactly this complaint, unfiltered. In Psalm 13:1, David asks God outright how long he's been forgotten, how long God's face is going to stay turned away from him. David wrote that as prayer, not as doubt he was ashamed of — the questions are addressed straight to God, by name, with no polite softening. He doesn't pretend the silence doesn't hurt. He tells God it hurts, and keeps talking to Him anyway.

That's the piece Gary's been missing. He assumed real faith meant the prayer had to stay composed, grateful, patient-sounding, even four years in. David shows a different shape of faithfulness — one that can demand *how long* and still be trusted as Scripture, still be counted as prayer. Gary is allowed to tell God exactly what four years of waiting costs him, in those words, instead of managing his own prayer down into something smaller and safer than what he actually feels.

**Today:** Pray your unanswered prayer again tonight, but say the honest version out loud instead of the managed one — including the part where you tell God how long it's felt. Add this afterward: *I can tell God the waiting is hard. That honesty is still faith, not a failure of it.*

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I have been praying the same prayer for a long time without any sign You've heard it, and I have been afraid to tell You how much the silence costs me. Hear the honest version tonight, the way You heard David's, and stay near me in the waiting. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

PSALM 13:1
