# Day 19 — The King's Letter

Malcolm helped his son load the last box into the car three weeks before move-in. Both of them were sweating through their shirts in the driveway. Walking back inside, he realized he had nothing prepared to say to him. He'd coached him, disciplined him, worked alongside him on a hundred Saturday projects. He'd driven him to practices and appointments for eighteen years. But he had never once sat him down and said, in so many words, what he actually believed mattered most. How to treat people who couldn't do anything for him. What to do when nobody was watching. What a man owes the people who depend on him. Malcolm had always assumed his son was picking that up just by living in the same house as him.

That assumption is where the belief hid: *watching me will teach him everything he needs. I don't need to actually say it.* It's a comfortable belief, because it asks nothing of a father beyond doing what he was already doing. But it also leaves the most important things unsaid, maybe forever, on the theory that they're somehow being picked up anyway.

Scripture doesn't let a father off with example alone. In Titus 2:6-7, St. Paul tells Titus to urge younger men toward self-control. He tells him to be a model of good deeds himself in every way, showing honesty and seriousness in how he lives and in how he teaches. The example and the teaching sit side by side in that instruction. Both are required. Neither one replaces the other. A young man needs to see the thing lived, and he needs to hear it named. Without one or the other, half the lesson goes missing.

**On St. Louis IX of France:** before Louis left on crusade, not knowing if he would return, he wrote his son Philip a set of instructions. Not a strategy for ruling. Instead, how to love God, deal fairly with people, care for the poor, and keep his own soul intact under the weight of power. Louis didn't trust that a crown alone would teach his son how to wear it well. He wrote it down, plainly, because he understood that some things a son needs said to him directly, not left for him to guess.

Malcolm didn't have a crusade or a crown. Just a driveway and three weeks before a dorm room. That night he sat down and wrote his son a letter. Not a card, an actual letter, naming the handful of convictions he wanted said out loud at least once before his son moved somewhere without him. He gave it to him the morning they drove up. He told him to read it later, alone, not in the car. He didn't know if it would land the way he meant it to. He knew it mattered more that he'd said it than that he'd said it perfectly.

**Today:** Write your son or daughter a short, specific letter naming one or two convictions you want them to carry, things you've assumed they already know. Give it to them today, in writing, instead of trusting your example to say it for you.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for assuming my children will absorb what matters most without my ever saying it plainly. Give me the words today to put into writing what I've only shown, and the courage to hand it over. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

TITUS 2:6-7
