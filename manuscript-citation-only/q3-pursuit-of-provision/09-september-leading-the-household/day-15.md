# Day 15 — A Handful of Quietness

Lawrence had said yes to overtime for six straight weekends before he did the math on what it actually cost him. The extra shifts paid well. Well enough to make the guilt easy to put off. But by the time he added up the missed Saturday pancakes, the two dance recitals he caught only the last five minutes of, and the string of nights he came home after his kids were already asleep, the math stopped looking like a win. It looked like a man trading his family's evenings for a number on a pay stub. One weekend at a time, never all at once, so it never quite felt like a real decision.

He told himself the same thing every time a supervisor asked: I'll catch up on rest and family time once this stretch is over. There was always another stretch waiting behind it. A promotion opened up that needed more travel. Lawrence said yes to that too. He told himself a bigger title now meant more time with his family later, once he reached a level where the hours finally settled down. He had never actually met a man at that level whose hours settled down.

The belief running the whole six weeks was simple enough to fit on an index card: more work now buys my family more of me later. Lawrence had never once tested it against the evidence sitting across the dinner table from him most nights.

Solomon tested nearly every possible answer to what makes a life worth living. He ran the same math and landed somewhere unexpected. In Ecclesiastes 4:6, he weighs one hand held in peace against two hands grasping at endless work. He says the single hand wins. He calls the alternative nothing but chasing after wind. He isn't praising laziness in that verse. The man he compares the quiet handful to isn't resting. He's grasping with both hands, working himself past any reasonable return, chasing wind that was never going to stay caught. Solomon's math says the smaller amount, held in peace, outweighs the larger amount grasped after.

Lawrence's math had assumed his family's presence was a debt. He thought he could leave it unpaid for a while and settle it later, with interest, once the stretch ended. Presence doesn't work like a line of credit. Every missed Saturday was a cost paid in full the day it happened, by his kids, whether or not Lawrence noticed the bill arrive.

He turned down the next overtime slot and put in for a Saturday off instead. No trip. No plan. Just a handful of ordinary hours at home with nothing else pulling at him. Weeks later, he asked his son about that day. His son didn't remember it as a big one. He remembered it as a normal Saturday. And that was exactly the kind of day Lawrence had been trading away for six weekends straight.

**Today:** Decline the next optional overtime shift or travel opportunity you're tempted to say yes to automatically, and protect the evening it would have cost you. Tell yourself: "A handful of quietness with my family is worth more than two hands full of everything this extra shift promises."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for treating my family's evenings like a debt I could repay whenever it was convenient for me. Teach me the math Solomon learned the hard way — that a small handful held in peace outweighs everything I could grasp for with both hands. Give me the courage to say no to hours my family will never get back. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
