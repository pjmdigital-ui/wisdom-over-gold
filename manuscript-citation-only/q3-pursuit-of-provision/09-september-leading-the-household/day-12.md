# Day 12 — Living What He Says

Jesse had spent the whole spring season telling his son the same line. After every rough call, every strikeout, every error at second base: "Control what you can control. Don't let your temper decide how you play." His son had started saying it back to him after a bad inning, without being asked. That felt like a decent kind of pride for a father to carry. Then, in the sixth inning of the last regular-season game, the umpire called his son out at home on a play replay would have overturned in half a second. Jesse was on his feet yelling before he'd decided to stand up. Words he wouldn't have wanted his son repeating carried loud enough for both dugouts to hear. His son, still standing in the on-deck circle, turned and looked at him. It was the exact look Jesse had spent all season coaching him not to give an umpire.

Jesse had never said the belief in so many words, but it had been running underneath his whole season of coaching: instruction was the job. If he said the right thing to his son enough times, it would take root regardless of what Jesse himself did under pressure.

St. Paul, writing to young Timothy, doesn't separate the two that easily. In 1 Timothy 4:12, he tells Timothy not to let anyone look down on him for his age. Instead, he tells him to become a model for other believers — in what he says, how he lives, in love, in faith, in purity. Paul doesn't just tell Timothy to teach well. He tells him to become an example, pairing speech and conduct together, as though the two always travel as one. A father's version of that verse runs the same direction. His son needed an example of staying calm far more than he needed one more lecture about it.

A twelve-year-old remembers almost nothing of what his father tells him to do over a season. He remembers what his father actually does in the sixth inning of a close game, in full view of both dugouts. Jesse's lecture about controlling his temper had been true as a principle. It became useless the moment his own example went against it, in front of the one person he'd been telling it to all year.

He found his son walking to the car after the game. He didn't excuse it or explain it away. "I told you all year to control your temper, and I didn't tonight. I was wrong, and I'm sorry." Something in his son's shoulders eased at that. Not because the outburst got erased. It eased because his father had shown him what a man does after failing to live up to his own word. That turned out to matter more than the original lecture ever had.

**Today:** Think of one thing you've been telling your kids to do that you haven't been doing yourself. Name it honestly. Apologize if it's needed. Change the actual behavior today, not just the wording of the lecture. Say to yourself: "My kids learn from what I do more than what I say, so today my example leads."

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for the times my example has undercut everything I've told my kids to become. Give me the humility to admit it plainly when I fail to live up to my own words. Let my conduct teach what my lectures alone never could. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 TIMOTHY 4:12
