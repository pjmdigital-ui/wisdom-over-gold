# Day 9 — Leading Without Controlling

Cody stood at the open dishwasher on Saturday morning and started pulling plates back out. His ten-year-old had loaded it twenty minutes earlier — plates crammed into the wrong rack, bowls stacked instead of angled — and Cody was redoing the whole thing before the cycle even started. Down the hall, his wife had already written a packing list for next weekend's camping trip and taped it to the fridge. Cody grabbed a highlighter, added his own items, reordered hers by priority, and texted her "just a few tweaks" before she'd even seen it hanging there.

None of it looked like a fight. He never raised his voice, never told anyone out loud they'd done something wrong. He just fixed things behind them, one dish and one list at a time, and told himself he was simply the kind of man who cared about getting things right.

The belief running under both hands was the same one: if I don't check it myself, it won't be right. Somewhere along the way Cody had started treating leadership like quality control — as though his job was to catch every mistake before it happened, in the dishwasher and in the family calendar alike, instead of building people capable of making good calls without him standing over their shoulder.

St. Paul writing to the Philippians didn't tell husbands and fathers to run tighter oversight of their households. He told them where to aim their attention instead: "Do nothing from selfishness or conceit, but in humility count others better than yourselves. Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others" (Philippians 2:3-4, RSV-CE). Cody's redone dishwasher wasn't malicious, but it carried a message anyway — that his standard mattered more than his son's competence, that his version of the list mattered more than his wife's judgment already at work on the fridge. Counting others better than himself would have meant trusting that his wife had written a workable list and trusting a ten-year-old to learn how a dishwasher actually runs, sloppily, wrong a few times, on his own.

A house where the man corrects everything eventually raises people who stop bothering to try, or who stop telling him what they're doing altogether, since the effort is going to get overwritten either way. Control feels like leadership because it's exhausting, and exhaustion feels like proof of dedication. But leadership that never lets go is control wearing a nicer word. A wife capable of packing for a weekend doesn't need a highlighter. A son learning to run a dishwasher needs the chance to run it badly a few more times far more than he needs it run right by someone else's hands.

Cody left the highlighted list on the fridge without adding another mark to it that weekend. His wife noticed before he said anything. His son loaded the dishwasher wrong again Sunday night, and Cody let the cycle run.

**Today:** Pick one thing you've been redoing behind someone in your house — a chore, a plan, a decision — and let it stand exactly as they did it, without comment or correction. Say it plainly to yourself: "Leading this house means trusting the people God gave me to lead, not managing every decision myself."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I've mistaken control for care more times than I'd like to admit. Teach me to trust my wife's judgment and let my kids learn by doing, even when they get it wrong. Grow in me the humility Your word asks for — the kind that counts others' good above my own preference for how things get done. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
