# Day 27 — A Home Marked by Joy

Norman ran a tight house. Homework before screens. Chores before homework. No exceptions for anyone, including himself. If you asked, he'd have told you he was doing his kids a favor. The real world doesn't grade on a curve, and somebody in this house has to hold the line.

Friday night the family had a standing game night. It was his wife's idea originally, one Norman had grudgingly agreed to and privately valued more than he let on. This particular Friday, he walked in forty minutes late from a bad week at work. He sat down mid-round. Within two minutes, he'd corrected his youngest for playing a card out of turn. Not unkindly, just flatly, the way he corrected everything. The room went quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet around a man who's about to be right about something small. Two rounds later, the game folded early. Nobody said why.

Norman noticed it in the car later, dropping his oldest at a friend's house. His kids had started reading the door before they read him. They checked his face before deciding how loud they were allowed to be in their own living room. He'd built a house that ran on time and stayed on schedule. He hadn't built one anyone particularly wanted to be in once he walked through the door.

The belief had never been questioned, because it looked so much like responsibility: *my job is to keep this house in order, and joy is what happens on its own once order is in place, if it happens at all.* Order had become the whole assignment. Joy had slipped into an optional bonus round, not really part of the job at all.

Nehemiah tells the exiles who'd just heard the Law read aloud, and wept over how far short they'd fallen, not to spend the day in grief. Instead, in Nehemiah 8:10, he tells them to go celebrate. Eat rich food. Drink something sweet. Send portions to anyone with nothing prepared. The day is holy, and grief has no place in it. The strength they'd need going forward would come from delighting in the Lord, not from dwelling on what they'd gotten wrong. The people had every reason to feel heavy that day — real failure, real guilt. Nehemiah tells them to feast anyway. Joy was the strength they'd need to keep going after getting it wrong, not a reward for having gotten it right in the first place.

Norman had mixed up a house that felt correct with a house that felt strong. They aren't automatically the same thing. A home run entirely on correction produces kids who've learned to manage their father. It doesn't produce a household anyone draws real strength from.

**Today:** Do something at home tonight with no correcting purpose at all — play the game to lose, laugh at the joke that isn't that funny, let a small rule slide — and tell your family afterward, out loud, that you want this house to feel like somewhere they want to be, not just somewhere they behave.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for running my home like a project to manage instead of a gift to enjoy. Teach me to lead with joy that actually strengthens my family, not just order that keeps them quiet. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

NEHEMIAH 8:10
