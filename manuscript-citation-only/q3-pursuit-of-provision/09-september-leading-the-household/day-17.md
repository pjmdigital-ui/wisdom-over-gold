# Day 17 — A Soft Answer

"How was school?" "Fine." Tobin had asked his son some version of that question every day for a week and gotten some version of that answer every time, and on the eighth day he caught himself about to ask it again on autopilot, already half-listening for the word "fine" so he could move the conversation along to what was for dinner. He realized, standing at the counter, that he did the same thing to his wife most nights — traded the day's logistics, the pickup schedule, whose turn it was to call the insurance company — and called it talking. Nobody in the house was lying to anyone. Nobody was raising their voice either. The house ran smooth and pleasant and almost entirely on the surface.

Tobin had built that smoothness on purpose, without ever deciding to. Years back, a few real conversations at that same counter had gone sideways — his wife upset, his son shutting down, an evening ruined over something that felt too big to fix quickly. Since then he'd steered toward the easy questions and away from the ones that might actually cost something to ask. The belief running underneath it rarely got stated so plainly, but it amounted to this: *as long as everyone's calm and the house sounds friendly, we're doing all right.* He had confused an absence of friction with the presence of peace.

Proverbs draws a sharper line than that. Proverbs 15:1 teaches that a gentle response defuses anger, while a sharp word only stirs up more of it. The verse assumes there's already something with an edge to it on the table — a real grievance, a real question, something worth an actual answer. A soft answer is a real response, not a dodge, gentle in its delivery but honest in its content, aimed straight at whatever's actually there. What Tobin had been offering instead was empty — pleasant words with nothing underneath them, standing in for an answer instead of being one.

His son had learned the pattern from him before Tobin ever noticed he was teaching it. "Fine" was less a lie than an inheritance, one generation's evasion becoming the next one's habit. Leading this house well meant more than keeping the volume down. It meant staying gentle while still saying something true, instead of using gentleness as cover for saying nothing at all.

That night Tobin asked again, but he didn't move on when the word "fine" came back. He said, "I believe you, but tell me one actual thing that happened today — good or bad, doesn't matter which." His son paused long enough that Tobin almost filled the silence for him. He didn't. What came out wasn't dramatic — a friend's parents were splitting up, and it had rattled him more than he expected. It was a real thing, said out loud, in a kitchen that had gone a long time without hearing one.

**Today:** Pick one person in your house and ask a question you actually want the honest answer to. Wait through the silence instead of filling it, and let the answer be as real, and as unhurried, as it needs to be.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for mistaking a quiet house for a peaceful one. Give me the gentleness to ask real questions today, and the patience to sit with real answers instead of steering us both back to easier ground. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
