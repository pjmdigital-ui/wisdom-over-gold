# Day 17 — A Soft Answer

"How was school?" "Fine." Tobin had asked his son some version of that question every day for a week. He'd gotten some version of that answer every time. On the eighth day, he caught himself about to ask it again on autopilot, already half-listening for the word "fine" so he could move on to what was for dinner. Standing at the counter, he realized he did the same thing with his wife most nights. They traded the day's logistics — the pickup schedule, whose turn it was to call the insurance company — and called it talking. Nobody in the house was lying to anyone. Nobody was raising their voice either. The house ran smooth and pleasant, almost entirely on the surface.

Tobin had built that smoothness on purpose, without ever really deciding to. Years back, a few real conversations at that same counter had gone sideways. His wife got upset. His son shut down. An evening got ruined over something that felt too big to fix quickly. Since then, he'd steered toward the easy questions and away from the ones that might actually cost something to ask. He rarely put the belief into words, but it amounted to this: *as long as everyone's calm and the house sounds friendly, we're doing all right.* He had mixed up a lack of friction with real peace.

Proverbs draws a sharper line than that. Proverbs 15:1 teaches that a gentle answer calms anger, while a harsh word only stirs up more of it. The verse assumes there's already something with an edge to it on the table — a real complaint, a real question, something worth an actual answer. A soft answer is a real answer, not a dodge. It's gentle in tone but honest in content, aimed straight at whatever's actually there. What Tobin had been offering instead was empty. Pleasant words with nothing underneath them, standing in for an answer instead of being one.

His son had learned the pattern from him before Tobin ever noticed he was teaching it. "Fine" was less a lie than an inheritance — one generation's dodge becoming the next one's habit. Leading this house well meant more than keeping the volume down. It meant staying gentle while still saying something true, instead of using gentleness as cover for saying nothing at all.

That night Tobin asked again. But this time he didn't move on when the word "fine" came back. He said, "I believe you, but tell me one actual thing that happened today — good or bad, doesn't matter which." His son paused long enough that Tobin almost filled the silence for him. He didn't. What came out wasn't dramatic. A friend's parents were splitting up, and it had shaken him more than he expected. It was a real thing, said out loud, in a kitchen that had gone a long time without hearing one.

**Today:** Pick one person in your house and ask a question you actually want the honest answer to. Wait through the silence instead of filling it, and let the answer be as real, and as unhurried, as it needs to be.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for mistaking a quiet house for a peaceful one. Give me the gentleness to ask real questions today, and the patience to sit with real answers instead of steering us both back to easier ground. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
