# Day 25 — What Outlives the Instruction

Emory had a rule about phones at the dinner table. He enforced it like it mattered, because he'd decided it did. Phones face-down in the basket by the door, no exceptions, from the first bite to the last. He'd taken his oldest daughter's phone away for a week, the one time she snuck a look at it under the table. Every few months he gave some version of the same speech, about presence, about a family actually looking at each other for twenty minutes a day.

One Thursday, mid-meal, his own phone buzzed with a work email he'd half expected. He slid it out below the table's edge, thumb moving fast, eyes flicking up between words. He didn't notice anyone watching until he looked up. His nine-year-old was doing the exact same thing. Phone under the table edge, eyes flicking the same way his had, a move so exactly his own it stopped him mid-bite.

She'd learned the loophole, not the rule. She'd learned it from watching him work it, not from anything he'd ever said to her directly. He tried to imagine explaining the rule to her later that night. He couldn't get past how ridiculous it would sound, coming from the man she'd just watched break it under the same table.

Emory had operated for years on the assumption that if he said the right thing to his kids often enough, clearly enough, that would be what actually shaped them. The instruction was the real inheritance, in his mind, and his example was just background noise under it. He'd never spelled it out that plainly, but it was the math behind every lecture he'd given: say it well enough, often enough, and the saying does the work. The dinner table proved him wrong in about four seconds, and it did it without a single word.

The psalmist, praying as an old man, asks God for something specific. He doesn't just want to have said something true once. He wants to still be saying it, still declaring it, long enough for the next generation to actually receive it. In Psalm 71:18, he prays that even in old age, with gray hair, God won't leave him. He asks for exactly this reason: so he can keep declaring God's power to every generation still to come. What survives in a child is whatever a father kept doing long after the words that first taught it were forgotten.

Four seconds of a Thursday had taught Emory's daughter more than any lecture about presence ever had. She'd probably seen it a dozen times he never noticed, until it just became how the family ate.

**Today:** Name one specific thing you've been teaching your kids with words that you're actually undoing with your actions, and fix the action first — today, in front of them, without announcing that's what you're doing.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, show me the gap between what I say to my children and what I actually let them watch me do. Give me the years and the faithfulness to make my example worth inheriting, not just my instructions. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

PSALM 71:18
