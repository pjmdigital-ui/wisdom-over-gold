# Day 18 — Apologizing First

Carter told his daughter to put her phone away at the dinner table. It was the third time that week. She rolled her eyes hard enough that he snapped. He told her she could go eat in her room if the table was such an inconvenience. She left. He sat there a minute longer than he should have. He knew his voice had carried more edge than the phone comment earned on its own. But he also knew she'd started it — the eye roll, the tone, the whole attitude of a fifteen-year-old who thought the rule itself was beneath her. By his math, she owed the first apology. He was willing to accept it whenever she got around to offering it.

Two days went by like that. Polite, distant, both of them technically fine, neither one bringing it up. Carter noticed the rest of the house had gone quieter than usual. His wife was stepping carefully around both of them. His younger son had picked up on tension he couldn't name. Carter told himself he was holding a reasonable line. He'd apologize for his tone the moment she apologized for hers. Waiting felt like fairness. But underneath the fairness, it also felt like keeping score.

The belief running the whole standoff was simple enough to name: *whoever was wrong first should be the one who apologizes first.* It sounds like justice. It works like a standoff, because both people can always find a reason the other one went first. The house sits in a low-grade cold front until somebody decides the scorekeeping isn't worth it anymore.

Scripture doesn't order confession by who was more at fault. James 5:16 tells believers to admit their sins to each other and pray for one another so they can be healed. It adds that a righteous man's prayer carries real power. The instruction runs both directions at once. Nobody waits to confess until the other person goes first. Healing in that verse depends on someone going first. It doesn't say that someone has to be whoever started it.

Carter found his daughter in her room that evening and said the plain thing: his tone had been out of line, whatever hers had been. He didn't reopen the phone rule or ask for her side first. She was quiet for a second, then said she was sorry too, in a smaller voice than the eye roll had suggested she had. Leading a house from the front sometimes looks exactly like this. Being the one who moves first. Not because he was more wrong, but because somebody has to break the standoff. The man leading the house is the one who can carry that weight without it costing him much at all.

**Today:** Go to the person you're in a standoff with — spouse, child, anyone under your roof — and apologize for your actual part, without waiting for theirs first and without attaching a but. Say it plainly and let it stand on its own.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for treating apology like something owed to me before I'll offer it. Give me the strength to go first today, even when I'm convinced I have the better argument. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
