# Day 13 — Hating Correction

Declan had built the third-quarter forecast himself. He walked the regional VP through it slide by slide and was two questions from wrapping the call clean when the VP stopped him. The growth assumption in the northeast territory didn't match the actual pipeline data from the CRM, and hadn't for two reporting cycles. It was a real error, not a nitpick. It was the kind that would have set three other people's targets on a number that didn't exist.

Declan felt his neck go hot before he'd even finished processing what the VP said. He didn't own the mistake out loud. He explained it instead. The CRM sync issue. The rep who'd been slow entering data. The tight turnaround he'd had to build the deck under. Every sentence was true. Not one of them was the answer the moment actually called for, which was simply: you're right, I missed it, let me fix it.

He hung up the call and sat with the specific weight of what had just happened in his chest. It wasn't quite embarrassment. It was something closer to injury, as if being wrong in front of his own boss had cost him more than an afternoon of rework. The belief underneath that reaction rarely gets said in plain words, but it was running the whole call: *if I admit the mistake instead of explaining it, they'll stop seeing me as someone who has this handled.*

Scripture doesn't soften what that belief actually is. Proverbs 12:1 says plainly that whoever welcomes correction loves knowledge, while whoever can't stand being corrected is acting like a fool. It's a hard word to sit under, on purpose. The man who can't stand being corrected is actively refusing the one thing that would have made him better at his job. Knowledge arrives, most often, through somebody else catching what a man's own untested confidence in his forecast missed.

The VP's phone call was the one moment in that entire quarter when Declan had the chance to actually get smarter about his own numbers. It wasn't an attack on his standing. He spent it defending a version of himself that didn't need defending, because being wrong about a data point was never going to cost him what he thought it would.

**Today:** Go back to the last correction you explained away instead of simply accepting. Say the sentence you skipped the first time, out loud, to the person who gave it: "You were right, I missed it, thank you for catching it." Let that be the whole response — no explanation attached.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I have loved being seen as right more than I've loved actually being corrected. Break the pride that turns every mistake pointed out into an attack I have to defend against. Teach me to love discipline the way the wise do. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
