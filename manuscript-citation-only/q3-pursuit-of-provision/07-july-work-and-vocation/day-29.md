# Day 29 — The Tentmaker

Patrick built his handyman business from nothing after a layoff two years ago. No assistant, no office, just a truck, a website he made himself, and Sunday nights spent writing estimates until his eyes gave out. It's steady work now, but never easy money, and his father-in-law noticed. Over dinner he offered Patrick a spot at the family furniture business: decent title, real benefits, hours nobody would ever check closely. "Just to give you some breathing room," he said. He meant it kindly.

Patrick sat with the offer for three days. Health insurance without the monthly scramble. A paycheck that didn't depend on whether the phone rang. Nobody would have blamed him for taking it, and some part of him wanted to, because it would have felt like being taken care of instead of always doing the taking care of. The belief underneath the temptation was simple: *if an easier check is offered, taking it is just being smart, whether or not the work behind it is real.*

St. Paul turned down easier checks his whole ministry, and he had a stronger claim to one than Patrick does. As an apostle, he had every right to be supported by the churches he served. He worked with his hands instead. He made tents, the same trade as the couple he lodged with in Corinth (Acts 18:3), so that nobody could say he was living off people poorer than himself. To the church in Thessalonica, watching some believers coast on the generosity of others while calling it faith, Paul didn't soften the point. In 2 Thessalonians 3:10-12, he lays down a rule that anyone unwilling to work shouldn't expect to eat. He calls out believers who'd become idle busybodies instead of doing honest work, and instructs them plainly, in Christ's name, to settle down and earn their own keep. That's not a verse about laziness in general. Paul is pointing straight at his own example a few verses earlier. He worked night and day, he says, precisely so he wouldn't be a burden to anyone. He earned the right to say this because he'd lived it first.

Patrick isn't being asked to refuse every kind offer for the rest of his life out of stubborn pride. He's being asked to notice what he's actually choosing when comfort is on the table dressed up as a job. Paul's tentmaking was itself an act of provision and integrity, not a fallback while he waited for real ministry to start. It proved that his faith cost him something and that the people around him could trust his motives, because his living didn't depend on their pity or their patronage.

The furniture business offer isn't dishonest. But taking it for the reasons Patrick was considering, comfort disguised as smart planning, would trade something real for something easy. Paul's whole example argues that the trade isn't worth it.

**Today:** Turn down (or renegotiate honestly) whatever "easier check" you've been quietly weighing against real work, and say the true belief out loud: *I would rather earn my provision honestly than be carried by someone else's pity for my comfort.* Then send one honest invoice, estimate, or piece of real work today — proof of the belief, not just the words.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, thank You for hands capable of honest work. Keep me from mistaking comfort for wisdom when an easier road is offered at the cost of what I actually owe the people who trust me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
