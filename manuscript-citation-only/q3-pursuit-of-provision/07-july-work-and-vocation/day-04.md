# Day 4 — No Small Jobs

Carl stood in the back of the men's group meeting on Wednesday night, coffee going cold in his hand. He listened to a guy named Elliot talk about the mission trip his church had just sent him on. Two weeks building a well in Guatemala. Kids running up to hug him at the airport. The whole thing felt important in a way Carl's own week hadn't come close to. Carl had spent that same stretch on the graveyard shift at a distribution warehouse, driving a forklift in circles, moving pallets of paper towels and dog food from one end of a concrete building to the other.

Nobody asked Carl what his week looked like. He wouldn't have known what to say if they had. "Moved some boxes" doesn't land well in a room that just heard about a well and a village and grateful kids. Driving home that night, he found himself doing math he'd never put into words before. He was ranking the value of a man's hours by how much they looked like ministry. A missionary's week counted. A pastor's week counted. A forklift at 2 a.m., moving inventory nobody would ever know his name for — that barely seemed to register at all.

He didn't say it to anyone, but the thought was there the whole drive home: work only matters to God when it looks like serving Him directly. Everything else is just a paycheck, invisible to heaven the same way it's invisible to the men in that meeting room.

St. Paul writes to a church full of people arguing over exactly this kind of ranking — what food is holy enough to eat, what practices matter enough to count. In 1 Corinthians 10:31, he cuts through the whole debate by telling them that even the most ordinary acts, eating and drinking, are meant to be done for God's glory. Whatever you do. Not whatever ministry you do. Not whatever visibly spiritual task you happen to be assigned. Eating and drinking are about as ordinary as human activity gets, and Paul puts them in the same sentence as the whole of a man's life. All of it can be offered to God, no matter how it stacks up against someone else's mission trip.

That means the offering has always been the measure, not the task. A well built in Guatemala for applause glorifies no one. A forklift run at 2 a.m. as an honest day's work, offered to God with no one watching, can glorify Him just as fully. God never split the ledger into "ministry" on one side and everything else on the other. There's one ledger, and it isn't sorted by how impressive the task looks from the back of a meeting room.

Carl had been measuring meaning by visibility instead of by who the work was for. His job was never actually short on meaning.

**Today:** Pick the most ordinary, least impressive task on your schedule today — the one you'd never mention out loud in a room like Carl's. Do it deliberately as an offering, and say this before you start it: *This counts. Every task I do can glorify God, not just the ones that look like ministry.*

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for ranking my work against other men's and finding mine small. Every task You've placed in my hands today, however ordinary, is Yours to receive if I offer it that way. Teach me to stop measuring my week by how it would sound out loud in a room and start measuring it by You. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

1 CORINTHIANS 10:31
