# Day 9 — The Deal He Shouldn't Have Made

Porter had been chasing the Ferris account for six weeks when the call finally came, and the moment the decision-maker asked how fast his shop could turn the rebrand around, he heard himself say ten days. He knew what a full rebrand actually took — closer to five weeks, even moving fast, even with the two contractors he leaned on for overflow. But a competing shop had already quoted three weeks, and Porter's mouth moved before his math did. Ten days, he said again, like repeating it would make it true. The client signed that afternoon.

By day four, Porter was staring at a logo concept he wasn't proud of, a style guide half-built, and a client asking for a progress call he wasn't ready to take. He hadn't lied about his skill. He'd lied about his calendar, and it was the same thing, dressed differently.

The belief running under that sales call never got said out loud, but it drove the whole conversation: *if I tell him the real timeline, he walks, and somebody less careful gets the account instead of me.* That belief treats a promise as a negotiating tactic — something said to win the room, then adjusted later once the ink dries. It works right up until the moment delivery comes due and the words spoken stop being negotiable.

Scripture is blunt about promises made carelessly to gain something. Ecclesiastes 5:4-5 warns a man not to sit on a vow he's made — pay it, and pay it promptly, because God takes no pleasure in a fool who makes a promise he doesn't keep, and it would have been better never to have promised at all than to promise and then default. The original setting is a man at the temple, promising something to God he hasn't thought through. The mechanism doesn't stay inside the temple walls. A client contract, a deadline given over the phone, a number promised to a boss in a meeting — these are vows too, made to another person made in the image of God, and God doesn't grade them on a curve just because they happened in a Tuesday sales call instead of at an altar.

The verse tells Porter that silence beats a broken promise. If he'd said "I need three weeks to give you something I'm proud of," and lost the account to the faster bidder, he'd have kept his word intact and lost nothing that actually belonged to him. Instead he kept the account and spent the next month paying interest on ten days he never had.

A man's yes is supposed to mean something before anyone checks whether he delivered. The moment "I'll get it done by Friday" becomes a number chosen to win the conversation rather than a number he actually intends to hit, his word stops functioning as a word. It becomes marketing.

**Today:** Call the person you're behind on delivering to — client, boss, spouse, it doesn't matter — and give them the real timeline instead of managing the conversation. Say plainly: "My word is worth more to me than this deal, and here's the truth about where things actually stand."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, I have said yes to things I hadn't thought through, hoping I could make the math work later. Forgive the promises I made to win a room instead of to keep my word. Give me the courage to tell the truth about a timeline even when it costs me the account. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

ECCLESIASTES 5:4-5
