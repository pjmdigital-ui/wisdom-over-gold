# Day 6 — Skillful in His Work

Ivan stood in a half-framed bathroom addition on a Thursday afternoon, staring at a stud bay that needed fire blocking he hadn't ordered enough of. He had two options: drive forty minutes to the supply yard and lose the rest of the day, or leave the gap and close the wall up anyway. Nobody would ever see it. The inspector had already signed off on framing two days earlier. The homeowner would never open that wall again in her lifetime, let alone check whether every code requirement behind the drywall had been met to the letter.

He stood there with the drill still in his hand, running the math a contractor runs a hundred times a year on a hundred small decisions like this one. Margins were tight on the job already. The client was happy, the schedule was tight, and the gap he'd be leaving was maybe eight inches — nothing structural, nothing anyone could point to and call dangerous. *It's behind the wall,* he told himself. *Nobody will ever know.* He'd used that sentence, or some version of it, more times over twenty years than he wanted to count, and it had never once failed to get the job closed up on schedule.

Proverbs doesn't size up a craftsman by asking "will anyone ever check." Solomon says, in Proverbs 22:29, that a man who is truly skilled at his work ends up serving people of real consequence, not staying stuck working for nobodies who'll never notice the difference. The verse measures a man by what kind of craftsman skillful work eventually reveals him to be, whether or not anyone happened to be watching the day he did it — the kind whose standard doesn't drop just because the audience shrinks to nobody at all. A man who does his best work only in front of inspectors and homeowners hasn't actually built a habit of excellence. He's built a habit of performing for an audience, which is a different thing entirely and tends to show itself eventually, one way or another.

The kings in that proverb stand for the standard a man holds himself to when the only witness left in the room is God and his own conscience, not literal royalty. Obscure men — the ones who'll never open that wall, never know the difference, never think to check — are exactly the audience Ivan had spent two decades building his real standard around, without noticing he was doing it. Proverbs says the craftsman worth naming is the one whose habits don't shift based on who's obscure and who's watching.

Ivan made the drive to the supply yard that afternoon. He drove because he decided, standing in that stud bay, that he didn't want to be a man whose quality depended on an audience — not because the eight-inch gap would have collapsed the house, and not because he suddenly feared getting caught — and the only way to stop being that man is to stop building the habit one hidden decision at a time.

**Today:** Find the one place in your current work where you're tempted to do less because no one would ever check — the file no one audits, the report no one reads closely, the corner nobody but you will ever know was cut. Do the fuller version anyway, and say why: *My standard stays the same whether a king is watching or nobody is.*

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, You see every wall I've ever closed up too fast because I thought no one would check. Forgive me for letting my standards shift based on who was watching instead of holding to one standard because You always are. Make me the kind of craftsman whose hidden work matches his visible work. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
