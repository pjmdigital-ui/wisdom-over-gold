# Day 26 — The Rhythm of Rest

Troy runs his own electrical business. One truck, one apprentice, a phone that never really turns off. Friday night, 9:40, he'd just finished a full week: five job sites, a permit inspection that ran long, an apprentice to train on top of it all. Then a customer texted about a tripped breaker. He could have told her it would keep until Monday. Instead he grabbed his tool bag off the porch, because *I could use the money* and *get ahead while I can* had become the two sentences running his whole week without him ever deciding to let them.

His wife stood in the doorway and asked him to just stop for the weekend. He said one more job, like he'd said the Friday before, and the Friday before that. He sat in the truck afterward at almost eleven, doing paperwork by the dome light instead of walking inside to a house where everyone else had already gone to bed.

Troy would tell you rest is what's left over once the work is finished. The trouble is the work is never finished. There's always one more breaker, one more bid, one more thing that would use the money. That belief doesn't get argued out of a man. It gets commanded out of him, and Scripture states the command with a weight that's easy to skip past. Exodus 20:9-10 gives six days for labor, real work, fully expected, and then sets the seventh apart entirely for rest, a sabbath to the Lord, with no work done on it at all. Notice the six days aren't optional either. Labor is commanded, and work has real dignity here. But the stopping carries the same weight as the working. God didn't suggest a day off for men who'd earned it. He commanded rest for everyone, including the ones with a truck full of unfinished jobs.

St. Benedict of Nursia built an entire way of life around that same insistence that work and rest aren't rivals. His monks lived by a rhythm he called *ora et labora*, prayer and work, with fixed hours for each. Neither one crowded out the other, and rest was built into the schedule rather than earned by exhaustion at the end of it. Benedict didn't trust monks to know when to stop, any more than God trusted Israel to know when to stop gathering manna on the sixth day. He built the stopping into the structure, because willpower runs out and a rhythm doesn't.

Troy doesn't need to work less to honor that. He needs to stop treating the seventh day as a suggestion he'll get to once the backlog clears. The backlog never clears. That was the whole point of Benedict's rule, and God's command before him.

**Today:** Pick a specific stopping time tonight and actually stop — phone off, tools in the truck, nothing "just this once." Say the true belief out loud before you do: *Rest is what God commanded before He ever asked me to work at all, not something I earn when the work runs out.*

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, forgive me for treating every stopping point as optional. Teach me to trust that the work will hold without me, the way You trusted Israel to stop gathering and still have enough. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
