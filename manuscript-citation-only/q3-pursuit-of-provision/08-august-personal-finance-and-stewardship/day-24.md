# Day 24 — Though the Fig Tree Does Not Blossom

Everett found out the deal was dead from a two-line text, not a phone call: the permits fell through, the buyer walked, and the forty thousand dollars he and his wife had wired eight months earlier into a rental-property flip wasn't coming back in any form worth counting on. He read it twice standing in his garage, keys still in his hand, and did the math he'd been avoiding for a week — the number that used to sit in "aggressive but smart" now just sat in "gone."

He'd prayed over that investment before he made it, asked God, more or less, to bless the decision and make it work. Standing in the garage with the phone still lit in his hand, the thought arrived before he could stop it: *If I'd really been walking right with God, this wouldn't have happened to me.* Not spoken out loud, not even fully formed, but running underneath everything — the quiet conviction that real financial loss is proof of some hidden failure, some crack in his faith God is punishing or at least declining to cover.

The prophet Habakkuk stood in a version of that same math, only worse — an economy about to fall apart entirely, not one bad deal. He names it without softening a single line, in Habakkuk 3:17-18. Even if the fig tree never blooms, the vines bear no fruit, the olive crop fails, the fields produce nothing, and every animal in the fold and stall is gone, he will still rejoice in the Lord. He will still find his joy in the God who saves him. Every source of provision a man in his world could count on is gone in the same sentence, and his response is rejoicing, aimed at God directly, without waiting for the fig tree to recover first.

That's the piece Everett's math had left out. Habakkuk is rejoicing because his confidence was never actually staked on the crops in the first place. The Lord was the ground he stood on, and the fig tree was just weather passing over it. Everett had reversed the order without noticing. The account balance had become the ground he stood on, and God had become the weather he assumed would cooperate if he behaved well enough.

St. Elizabeth Ann Seton learned this the hard way and at far higher cost. She married into wealth, then watched her husband's shipping business fall apart, then watched him die of tuberculosis in a foreign quarantine station, leaving her a widow at twenty-nine with five children and almost nothing. She didn't get the money back. She didn't get her husband back. What she built instead — a religious community, the first American parochial school system — came out of the ruin, not around it. Seton's confidence had to move to the same ground Habakkuk stood on, because every other ground had already given way under her.

Everett isn't owed his forty thousand dollars back, and no amount of correct praying was ever going to guarantee the deal. What he's owed, if he wants it, is the God the deal was never actually able to touch.

**Today:** Say the true sentence out loud, even if it doesn't feel true yet: *My peace with God was never supposed to depend on this account balance.* Then do one ordinary act of trust today that costs you nothing to prove — the tithe check, the honest budget conversation, whatever you've been avoiding since the loss — instead of waiting to feel recovered first.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, this loss has made me wonder whether You're still for me, as if Your love had to show up on a balance sheet to be real. Teach me to rejoice in You before the recovery comes, not after. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

HABAKKUK 3:17-18
