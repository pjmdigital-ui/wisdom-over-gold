# Day 23 — What You Leave Behind

Hank's oldest daughter graduated college that spring with no debt, a car he'd paid off outright, and an apartment he'd covered the deposit on without her asking. He'd worked hard for twenty-two years so his kids would never have to carry what he'd carried at their age — the two jobs through school, the years of a car that broke down more than it ran, the slow climb out of nothing. Watching her walk across that stage, he felt like the plan had worked exactly as intended.

Three months later she called asking for help with a car payment she'd taken on for a vehicle well beyond what her entry-level salary could support, the fourth time in as many months she'd called about money without once mentioning a budget, a side job, or a plan of her own. Hank paid it again, the way he had the previous three times, and hung up the phone with a knot in his stomach he couldn't quite name. He'd spent two decades making sure she'd never have to struggle. It hadn't occurred to him, until that call, that the struggle might have been part of what would have taught her how to hold money once it was hers.

The belief Hank had built his whole strategy on was never stated as plainly as it deserved: *the most loving thing I can do for my kids is make sure they never have to face what I faced.* It sounds like devotion. Followed far enough, it produces a grown adult who has never once had to solve a financial problem of her own, because her father always solved it first.

Scripture draws the line differently than Hank's instinct did, and in the opposite direction he expected: "Here for the third time I am ready to come to you. And I will not be a burden, for I seek not what is yours but you; for children ought not to lay up for their parents, but parents for their children" (2 Corinthians 12:14, RSV-CE). St. Paul's point was the direction of the obligation, not that parents owe their children an unlimited safety net stretching well into adulthood — laying up isn't a debt owed indefinitely, on demand, regardless of what it teaches the child receiving it. A father who never lets his adult children face a consequence ends up laying a foundation nobody ever has to learn to stand on.

Hank told his daughter, the next time she called, that he loved her and wasn't going to cover the car payment this time — that he'd help her build a real budget instead, sit with her through it, but the check wasn't coming. It was the hardest phone call of his year. It was also the first time in a long while he'd given her something more useful than money.

**Today:** Identify one place where providing for someone you love — a child, a sibling, a parent — has quietly slid into rescuing them from a consequence they need to learn from. Have the conversation today about what real help looks like going forward, even if it's harder than writing another check.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for confusing rescuing with loving when it comes to the people I'm responsible for. Give me the wisdom to provide what actually builds them up, even when it's harder than simply paying the bill. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
