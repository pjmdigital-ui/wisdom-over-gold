# Day 19 — The Liberal Hand

Jim's brother-in-law called on a Sunday evening to ask for help covering a car repair — six hundred dollars he needed by Wednesday or he'd lose the job that required the car to get there. Jim had it. Not loosely, not carelessly, but he had it: a fully funded emergency account, a retirement balance that had grown steadily for a decade, no debt beyond the mortgage. By any honest accounting, six hundred dollars would not move the needle on his family's security even slightly.

He heard himself say he'd "have to check with Diane and get back to him," which was technically true and also a stall tactic he recognized the moment it left his mouth. He hung up and sat with a familiar tightness in his chest, the same one that showed up whenever a number left his account that he hadn't already budgeted for, no matter how much sat behind it. He ran through reasons his brother-in-law had gotten himself into this spot, reasons that were mostly true and entirely beside the point.

Jim didn't say no. He also didn't say yes for two more days, long enough that his brother-in-law found the money elsewhere and didn't need him after all. Jim told himself it had worked out fine. What actually happened is that his hand had closed instinctively around what he had, the way a hand closes around anything it's afraid of losing, whether or not losing it was ever a realistic risk.

The belief driving that closed hand rarely announces itself with numbers attached: *if I give this away, I'll have less, and less is dangerous — no matter how much "more" I'm currently sitting on.* Scarcity runs on a feeling that has nothing to do with the actual figures, not on whatever the bank statement says, which is exactly why a man with a healthy savings account can hoard just as easily as a man with none.

Scripture draws the opposite conclusion from the one Jim's instinct reached. Proverbs 11:24-25 says one man gives freely and ends up richer for it, while another holds back more than he should and ends up worse off. The generous man ends up enriched himself, the way a man who waters others' fields ends up watered in return. This is a plain, unsentimental claim about how open hands and closed hands actually work over a lifetime. The giver ends up watered, and the one who holds back ends up wanting, no matter what the spreadsheet said on the day of the decision.

Jim called his brother-in-law back the following week, after the crisis had already passed, and told him the truth: he'd hesitated out of a fear that didn't match his actual finances, and he was sorry it took him two days to offer what he could have offered in an hour. He set aside a standing amount now, specifically earmarked for exactly this kind of call, so the next time his phone rang, his hand wouldn't have to fight itself before it opened.

**Today:** Look honestly at whether your hesitation to give — money, time, or help — comes from an actual constraint or from a scarcity fear that doesn't match your real situation. If it's fear, give something today anyway, to someone specific, before you've had time to talk yourself out of it.

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for the times I've withheld out of fear when I actually had enough to give. Loosen my grip on what You've entrusted to me, and make me a man whose hand opens before it closes. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
