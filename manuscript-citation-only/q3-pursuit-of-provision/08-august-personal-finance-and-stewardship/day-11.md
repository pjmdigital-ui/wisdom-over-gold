# Day 11 — Two Masters

Corwin's daughter played her last soccer game of the season on a Saturday morning he'd promised her, months earlier, he would be there for. He missed the first half taking a call from a client who wanted a proposal reworked before Monday, and he told himself it was one call, not a pattern — the kind of thing any businessman would do for an account that size. By the time he got to the field, the game was in its final minutes and his daughter had already stopped scanning the sideline for him.

That night, going back through his calendar, Corwin noticed something he hadn't let himself add up before: seven of the last ten weekends had a work call somewhere in them. Every one of those calls had felt urgent in the moment. None of them, looked at together, had actually required his personal presence at the exact hour his daughter was playing. He'd told himself for years that the extra hours were for his family — the tuition, the vacations, the security he was building for them. He'd never once asked what it cost them for him to build it.

Jesus doesn't soften this one. In Matthew 6:24 He says plainly that no man can serve two masters — he'll end up loving one and hating the other, devoted to one and dismissive of the other — and that a man cannot serve both God and money. The word mammon names money treated as a master, something a man organizes his loyalty, his time, and his attention around. Corwin never decided to serve money over his family or over God. He just kept answering the phone, one reasonable call at a time, until the pattern of what he actually served had already been set by his calendar rather than his convictions.

**On St. Matthew:** before he became an apostle, Matthew sat at a tax booth collecting money for Rome, a job that required exactly this kind of loyalty — to the ledger, to the collection, to whatever kept the coins moving toward him. When Jesus called him to follow, Matthew got up from that booth and left it behind without negotiating a transition period (Matthew 9:9). He didn't stop needing money after that. He stopped needing it to be his master. A man who has served mammon up close, the way Matthew did for a living, is a credible witness to how quietly that service starts and how completely it can end.

Corwin can't undo the missed games. He can decide which master answers the next Saturday call.

**Today:** Look at your calendar for the next two weekends and name one commitment to your family that work has already been allowed to override before. Protect it today — tell your team or your client directly that you're unavailable that hour, no exceptions — and say to yourself: "My family is the reason the work exists, not something I fit in around it."

**Prayer:** Lord God Almighty, forgive me for letting money set the terms of my time without ever deciding it should. Show me where mammon has crept into the seat that belongs to You alone. Give me the courage to protect the hours that matter most, even when work makes a reasonable case for taking them. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
