# Day 8 — Counting the Cost

Quinn had never actually built a budget in twelve years of marriage — not really. He and his wife paid the bills that showed up, kept a rough sense of what was in checking, and avoided looking much closer than that. Twice a year, usually around tax season, he'd open his banking app with some resolve to finally get real numbers on paper, scroll through three months of transactions, feel a familiar tightness build in his chest, and close the app again before he'd written down a single total.

Quinn knew how to build a spreadsheet — he'd built plenty of them at work, tracking budgets for projects worth more than his entire net worth. The avoidance was specific to his own numbers, and specifically to what an honest total might tell him — that they were spending more than he wanted to admit on things that didn't matter much, that the "emergency fund" was more idea than reality, that the gap between what he assumed and what was actually true might be bigger than he could stand to look at directly.

*As long as I don't look too closely, I can keep believing things are basically fine* — that was the actual rule running Quinn's twice-a-year budget attempts, dressed up as busyness rather than what it was: fear of a number he hadn't yet had to face.

Christ ties this exact impulse to something surprisingly practical in the Gospel of Luke. Teaching a crowd about the cost of following Him, He reaches for an image every builder in the crowd would recognize: "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?" (Luke 14:28, RSV-CE). The warning that follows is about a man who skips that step and ends up with a half-built tower and a crowd laughing at the foundation he never finished. Christ is describing the specific kind of foolishness that skips honest math because it might be uncomfortable, and builds forward anyway on hope instead of numbers — not merely a lesson about towers.

Counting the cost is a form of faith, not its opposite, whatever the anxious avoidance likes to tell a man — a willingness to look directly at reality because reality, however uncomfortable, is the actual ground a man has to build on. A budget Quinn refuses to write is a tower he's building blind, one month at a time, hoping the foundation holds without ever checking it, not a budget he's spared himself from at all.

Quinn sat down that weekend, opened three months of transactions, and wrote every number down without closing the app once.

**Today:** Sit down for thirty minutes today and write out one honest number — total spending last month, actual balance across every account, or the gap between income and expenses. Whatever it is, write it down before you let yourself look away.

**Prayer:** Father in Heaven, I've avoided the honest numbers of my own finances because I was afraid of what they'd tell me. Give me the courage to count the cost plainly, trusting You with whatever I find. Teach me to build on truth instead of hope alone. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
