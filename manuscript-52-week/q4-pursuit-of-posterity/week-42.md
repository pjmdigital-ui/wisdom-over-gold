# Week 42 — How You Speak to Your Wife Matters

A man can be respectful all day and still come home and speak carelessly to the person he claims to love most.

He is patient with the customer who asks the same question three times. He watches his tone with his boss. He explains himself calmly to a coworker who made a mistake. He smiles at the cashier even when the line is slow.

Then he walks into his own house and gives his wife the version of himself that nobody else would tolerate.

The sarcastic comment.

The unnecessary correction.

The joke that has been made too many times.

The sharp answer because he's tired.

The tone that says, *You should already know this.*

Most of the time, he doesn't think of himself as disrespectful. If you asked him whether he loves his wife, the answer would be obvious.

That's part of what makes careless speech so easy to excuse.

**She knows I love her.**

So the comment shouldn't matter that much.

She knows I didn't really mean it.

She knows I had a difficult day.

She knows that's just how I talk when I'm frustrated.

But knowing that someone loves you does not make every way they speak to you harmless.

St. Paul gives Christians a demanding standard in Ephesians. He tells them not to let destructive speech come out of their mouths, but to use words that build up the person who hears them.

That's a useful standard for marriage because it moves beyond one question:

**“Was what I said technically wrong?”**

And asks another:

**“What did my words do to my wife?”**

Did they strengthen her?

Did they communicate respect?

Did they help solve the problem?

Or did they embarrass, diminish, discourage, or wound her?

That doesn't mean every sentence you speak to your wife must be flattering.

Love requires honesty.

Sometimes your wife needs you to say something difficult. Sometimes you disagree. Sometimes a real problem has to be confronted, and avoiding the conversation would be irresponsible.

But truth does not require contempt.

Correction does not require humiliation.

Frustration does not give you permission to become cruel.

A man should be able to say something hard without making his wife pay for the fact that he is angry.

That is self-control.

And marriage tests it because familiarity lowers our guard.

You know your wife isn't likely to disappear because of one rude comment. You don't have to impress her the way you once did. You know which topics irritate her, which insecurities hurt, and which sentence can end an argument quickly if you're willing to use it.

That knowledge can make you more loving.

It can also make you more dangerous.

A stranger doesn't know enough about your wife to wound her precisely.

You do.

That gives your words more responsibility, not less.

The closer the relationship, the higher the standard should become.

Yet men often reverse it.

The stranger gets manners because the relationship is fragile.

The wife gets carelessness because the relationship feels secure.

That makes no sense when you think about it.

Your wife will hear thousands more words from you than almost anyone else will. A stranger may forget your impatient comment by tomorrow. Your wife may hear the same criticism from you for ten years.

Repeated words shape a marriage.

If your normal language toward your wife is affectionate, respectful, and appreciative, that creates something over time.

If your normal language is correction, sarcasm, impatience, and criticism, that creates something too.

You may not notice either process happening because both happen slowly.

One comment rarely defines a marriage.

A pattern can.

This is especially important with humor.

A man can hide a lot of disrespect behind, **“I'm only joking.”**

Maybe you joke about your wife's driving.

Her cooking.

How long she takes to get ready.

How much she talks.

How she spends money.

Something she did years ago that still gets brought out whenever there's an audience.

Everyone laughs.

Maybe she does too.

But humor does not automatically make disrespect harmless.

A useful question is whether the joke consistently requires your wife to be the smaller person so you can get the laugh.

If it does, stop making it.

The same applies to how you talk about her when she isn't there.

Men sometimes speak about their wives differently in a group of other men than they ever would in front of them. Complaints become entertainment. Marriage becomes a punchline. A wife's weaknesses become stories.

You can participate so casually that you don't even notice what you are training yourself to think.

The way you talk about your wife when she cannot hear you still affects your marriage because those words reinforce a picture of her in your own mind.

Honor has to exist in both rooms.

That does not mean pretending your marriage has no problems or never seeking counsel. There may be times when you need to speak honestly with a trusted friend, priest, counselor, or another person capable of helping.

But seeking help is different from using your wife's weaknesses as material.

One protects the marriage.

The other slowly cheapens it.

Your tone matters too.

Sometimes the words themselves are harmless, but everyone in the room knows what was actually communicated.

“Fine.”

“Whatever.”

“I said I'll do it.”

“Do what you want.”

The dictionary meaning may be neutral.

The message isn't.

A husband can become very skilled at saying something technically defensible in a tone designed to punish.

That isn't honest communication.

It's aggression with plausible deniability.

If something is wrong, say what is wrong.

If you are angry, own the anger without making your wife decode it through sarcasm and tone.

If you need time to calm down, say that.

The goal isn't to become a man who never experiences frustration.

It's to become a man whose frustration does not automatically become somebody else's wound.

There is another side to this too.

Building up your wife requires more than removing bad speech.

A marriage can become emotionally starved without either spouse saying anything cruel.

You simply stop saying the good things.

You notice something but never mention it.

You appreciate what she does but assume she knows.

You admire something about her but keep it inside your head.

You once told her she was beautiful without needing a reason. Now you think it but rarely say it.

You once thanked her for ordinary things because you noticed them. Over time, they became expected.

Words that build up have to actually be spoken.

Your wife should not have to reconstruct your love entirely from your intentions.

Say it.

Thank her.

Tell her what you admire.

Tell her what she did well.

Tell her what you noticed.

Tell her why you're still grateful she is your wife.

Not because marriage needs constant praise to survive, but because gratitude left permanently unspoken helps nobody.

Your words are part of what you bring into the marriage every day.

They can make home safer.

Or they can make everyone brace themselves before you speak.

They can make your wife feel seen.

Or constantly evaluated.

They can remind her that she is loved.

Or make her wonder whether the man who speaks kindly to everyone else has simply stopped believing she deserves the same care.

How you speak to your wife matters.

Not only when the conversation is important.

Especially when it isn't.

Because the ordinary words are the ones she hears most.

### This Week

Pay attention to your speech toward your wife for one week.

Choose **one thing you say regularly**—a sarcastic comment, criticism, dismissive phrase, joke, or tone—that you would be embarrassed to use with a stranger or coworker.

Stop using it.

If you've already used it recently, apologize specifically.

Then replace it with one sentence that actually builds your wife up. Tell her something you appreciate, admire, or are grateful for and would normally leave unsaid.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, forgive me for the times I have given strangers more patience and courtesy than I have given my own wife. Guard my mouth from sarcasm, contempt, careless criticism, and words spoken simply because I am frustrated. Teach me to tell the truth with love, to speak with respect even when we disagree, and to say the good things I too often leave unspoken. Let my words make my marriage stronger and my wife more certain that she is loved and honored by me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**EPHESIANS 4:29**
