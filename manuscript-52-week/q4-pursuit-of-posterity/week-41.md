# Week 41 — Stop Keeping Score

A marriage can become a competition without either person ever saying so.

You start noticing who did more. Who apologized last. Who initiated affection. Who handled the kids. Who cleaned up. Who made the sacrifice. Who gave in during the last argument.

At first, the score stays private.

Then resentment starts using it.

You tell yourself you are only being fair. You are simply noticing that the effort is uneven, that you keep giving while your wife keeps receiving, or that you are always the one expected to change.

Maybe some of that is true.

But once you start keeping score, something important changes.

Your wife stops being your partner and starts becoming the person on the other side of the ledger.

That is not what you promised.

Malachi describes marriage as a covenant made before God. He speaks of the wife of a man's youth as his companion and his wife by covenant, with God Himself standing as witness to the promise.

That word—**covenant**—changes the entire structure of marriage.

A contract says, “I will do this if you do that.”

A covenant says, “I have given myself to you.”

Contracts have their place. They define obligations and protect people when one side fails to perform.

Marriage is different.

You did not stand at the altar and promise fidelity only if your wife remained easy to live with. You did not promise patience only during the years when she was patient first. You did not promise love as long as the division of labor remained perfectly even.

You gave your word before God.

That doesn't mean behavior doesn't matter.

It doesn't mean one spouse can behave however she wants while the other silently absorbs everything. Serious problems need to be addressed. Sin should not be renamed love. Hurt should not simply be ignored because somebody said vows years ago.

Covenant does not eliminate truth.

It changes the posture from which you approach it.

Instead of asking, **“Has she done enough for me to owe her something back?”** you begin with a different question:

**“What does faithfulness require from me here?”**

That is harder.

Scorekeeping feels safer because it gives you a reason to withhold.

She hasn't apologized, so I won't.

She hasn't been affectionate, so neither will I.

She hasn't asked about my day, so why should I ask about hers?

I did the last three things around here. This one is hers.

Each individual calculation can sound reasonable.

Together, they create a marriage where both people gradually stop giving until the other person goes first.

Then both sit waiting.

And resentment grows in the space between them.

The problem with keeping score is not merely that your math may be wrong.

It usually is incomplete.

You know exactly what your sacrifices cost you because you felt them.

You do not always know what hers cost.

You remember the night you got up with the child because you were exhausted the next morning. You may not remember the five smaller things your wife handled while you were thinking about something else.

You know when you swallowed your frustration.

You don't necessarily know how many times she did the same.

That doesn't mean the effort is always equal.

It means marriage was never meant to survive by requiring constant equality before either person gives again.

Sometimes one spouse carries more because the other is sick.

Sometimes one carries more because work is crushing the other.

Sometimes one is grieving.

Sometimes one is simply having a terrible season.

If every difficult period has to be repaid before the marriage can feel balanced again, neither person is being loved through weakness.

They're being invoiced for it.

That is where covenant becomes real.

A vow matters most precisely when the other person is not performing at her best.

Anyone can be generous when everything feels even.

The harder question is whether you can remain faithful when the week feels unfair.

That doesn't mean pretending you aren't tired.

It may mean saying, “I need help.”

It may mean telling your wife that something has become unsustainable.

It may mean having the difficult conversation you've avoided because you didn't want a fight.

But that conversation should be about restoring the marriage, not collecting what you believe you are owed.

Those are different motives.

One says, “We need to fix this because we're on the same side.”

The other says, “You need to make this up to me.”

You can feel the difference.

Scorekeeping also changes the way you argue.

Instead of dealing with the thing that happened, you bring the ledger.

The dishes somehow become about Christmas.

A late text becomes about the vacation three years ago.

The disagreement about Saturday becomes evidence in a case you've been building for months.

Now you aren't solving a problem.

You're prosecuting your wife.

And when that happens, being right can become more important than being united.

There are moments when you actually will be right.

You remembered correctly.

You did say it.

She did forget.

You have the text message.

You can prove your case.

The question is what you do with that victory.

If proving that you were right leaves your wife feeling like she just lost to her own husband, something important has gone wrong even if your facts were perfect.

Marriage does not require you to become indifferent to truth.

It requires you to remember who is standing in front of you while you are telling it.

She is not your opponent.

St. Paul tells Christians to reject selfish ambition and pride, to look beyond their own interests, and to take on the mind of Christ.

That doesn't come naturally during conflict.

Your instinct is to defend yourself.

Explain yourself.

Correct the record.

Make sure she understands exactly why you were justified.

Sometimes the more Christlike act is to stop building the case long enough to hear what the situation felt like from her side.

You may still disagree afterward.

But now you're having a marriage instead of a trial.

And there is another form of scorekeeping that can be even harder to notice.

You do something good and immediately expect something back.

You clean the kitchen, then feel irritated when nobody praises you.

You plan something thoughtful, then become disappointed because your wife doesn't react the way you imagined.

You make a sacrifice, but somewhere inside you've already decided what it should purchase.

That's not always generosity.

Sometimes it's a trade you never told the other person she had agreed to.

Love becomes freer when you stop attaching hidden invoices to it.

You do something because it is good for your wife and your marriage.

Not because it earns a credit.

Not because now she owes you one.

Not because you plan to mention it during the next disagreement.

You give because you gave yourself to her already.

That is what covenant keeps asking of you.

Not that you never notice imbalance.

Not that you never address a problem.

That you stop turning every act of love into a transaction.

Marriage will never stay perfectly balanced.

Some weeks you'll carry more.

Some weeks she will.

Over a lifetime, there will be seasons when both of you depend heavily on the promise you made before you were capable of imagining how much it would eventually be asked to carry.

That is why the promise matters.

If your marriage only works when the score is even, the score has become more important than the covenant.

Stop keeping it.

### This Week

Identify **one thing you have been silently keeping score of** in your marriage.

A chore.

An apology.

Affection.

Time.

A sacrifice you think should have been noticed.

Something you believe your wife owes you because of what you have already done.

Don't bring it up as a charge.

Ask yourself first:

**“Am I trying to solve a real problem, or am I trying to collect a debt?”**

If there is a genuine issue, talk about it as something the two of you need to address together.

If it is simply a private score you've been carrying, let it go without requiring anything in return.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, forgive me for the times I have treated my marriage like a transaction and my wife like someone whose performance determines what I owe her. Teach me to honor the covenant I made before You, to address real problems honestly without turning them into a scorecard, and to give without attaching hidden conditions. Give me the humility to stop trying to win against the woman I promised to love and help me remember that we are on the same side. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**MALACHI 2:14**
