# Week 52 — What Outlives You

Most men eventually think about what they will leave behind.

Maybe it starts with money. A house. Life insurance. A business. Investments. Something your wife and children will have because you worked and planned well.

Those things matter. Providing beyond your own lifetime can be an act of love.

But an inheritance and a legacy are not exactly the same thing.

An inheritance is something people receive from you.

A legacy is something of **you** that remains in them.

That difference becomes clearer when you think about what people actually remember after a man is gone.

They may know what he earned. They may inherit what he owned. His name may remain on a business, a building, or a document.

But eventually the numbers lose their meaning.

What tends to remain is different.

He always kept his word.

He treated people with dignity.

He prayed.

He was there.

He loved his wife.

He apologized when he was wrong.

He gave when it cost him something.

He didn't disappear when life became difficult.

He made people feel that they mattered.

Or, sometimes, the opposite remains.

He was always working.

You never knew what mood he would be in.

Nothing was ever good enough.

He talked about faith but we never really saw it.

He provided for us, but we barely knew him.

Those are inheritances too.

Proverbs tells us that a good name is worth more than great wealth.

That does not mean fame.

A good name is not having thousands of people recognize you.

It is what the people who actually knew you can truthfully say when there is nothing left for you to give them and nothing left for them to gain from flattering you.

That kind of name is built slowly.

One decision at a time.

One kept promise.

One honest answer.

One conversation where you put the phone down.

One moment where you could have protected your pride and chose to apologize instead.

One ordinary Sunday when your children watched you practice the faith you told them mattered.

That is how a man becomes remembered.

Not primarily through one great act at the end of his life, but through the repetition of who he was while nobody thought they were watching history being made.

That is one of the great misunderstandings about legacy.

We imagine it as something we build later.

After the business is successful.

After the children are older.

After we have more money.

After we finally have enough time to think about what we want to leave behind.

But posterity is being built now.

Your children are already inheriting you.

Your wife is already living inside the marriage you are creating.

The people you work with are already learning whether your word means something.

The men around you are already discovering whether friendship with you has any depth.

The people who know you are already collecting the evidence that will one day become your reputation.

You do not start building a legacy when you write a will.

You have been building it for years.

That can be uncomfortable because it means legacy is not completely under your control.

You cannot write your own eulogy and make it true.

You cannot demand that your children remember you the way you hope to be remembered.

You cannot purchase respect after the fact.

All you can do is live in such a way that the words become true before anyone has to say them.

That takes us back to the heart of this entire pursuit.

Posterity is not mainly about being remembered.

It is about handing something worth carrying to the people who come after you.

Faith.

Character.

Wisdom.

Courage.

A picture of marriage worth imitating.

A picture of fatherhood worth continuing.

A way of handling money, anger, work, temptation, failure, and suffering that points beyond you.

Sometimes what gets handed down is remarkably small.

A child remembers that his father prayed every morning at the same chair.

A daughter remembers that Dad always came back and apologized after losing his temper.

A grown son realizes he speaks to his own children with a phrase his father used to bless him.

A granddaughter practices a Catholic habit she never knew began several generations earlier.

You may never see most of that.

That is part of posterity.

A man plants things whose full effect belongs to a future he will not control.

Psalm 71 contains the prayer of a man asking God not to abandon him in old age until he has declared God's strength to the next generation.

There is something beautiful in that.

He is not asking merely for more years.

He wants enough time to hand something down.

That gives aging a different purpose.

You are not simply trying to stay alive longer.

You are trying to become increasingly worth learning from.

And that means posterity does not begin with your children.

It begins with the man they are watching.

If you want them to inherit faith, practice it.

If you want them to inherit integrity, tell the truth.

If you want them to become men and women who apologize, let them hear you do it.

If you want them to understand marriage as covenant, love their mother accordingly.

If you want them to know money is a tool and not a god, let them see generosity.

If you want them to trust God in difficult seasons, they need some memory of watching you trust Him in one.

Instructions matter.

Example tends to travel farther.

You may forget most of the speeches your own father gave you.

Yet you probably remember things he did.

The way he reacted.

The things he valued.

What happened when he was angry.

Whether he showed up.

Whether his faith was real.

What he did when nobody important was watching.

That is how deeply example works.

It can move through a family without anyone formally deciding to preserve it.

Which means the question at the end of this year is not:

**“What do I want people to remember about me?”**

That question is still too centered on you.

A better question is:

**“What do I want to still be happening because I was here?”**

Do you want your children praying with their children?

Do you want your sons treating their wives with greater tenderness because they watched how you treated theirs?

Do you want your daughters knowing what faithful love looks like because they saw it at home?

Do you want people you served to become more generous because someone once treated them generously?

Do you want faith to continue moving through your family after your own voice is gone?

That is something worth building.

And it brings us back to where everything begins.

Seek first the Kingdom of God.

Because if God is first, the rest of your life begins taking its proper place.

Piety gives the life direction.

Protection shapes the character of the man living it.

Provision teaches him to steward what God places in his hands.

Posterity asks what all of that becomes in the lives that continue after his own.

The four pursuits are not four separate lives.

They are one life rightly ordered.

And you never finish them.

There is no point where you can say you have completely mastered faith, character, stewardship, marriage, fatherhood, or legacy.

You keep returning.

You notice where something has drifted.

You bring it back into order.

You fail.

You repent.

You continue.

That may be the most important thing you hand down of all.

Not the image of a man who got everything right.

The example of a man who kept turning back toward God.

Your children may someday remember your weaknesses more clearly than you would like.

Let them also remember what you did with them.

Dad apologized.

Dad changed.

Dad kept seeking God.

Dad did not quit because he failed.

That kind of legacy leaves room for grace.

And long after the account has been distributed, the house has been sold, the business has changed hands, and the things you worked so hard to acquire belong to somebody else, that may be what still remains.

A life that taught another life how to seek first.

### This Week

Write one sentence answering this question:

**“What do I want to still be happening in my family after I am gone?”**

Do not write about what you want them to own.

Write about who you hope they become and what you hope continues through them.

Then ask:

**“What would they need to see me doing now for that to become likely?”**

Choose one thing and begin doing it more faithfully.

Not for one week.

For as long as God gives you to do it.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, thank You for every person, responsibility, and opportunity You have placed in my life. Keep me from confusing what I accumulate with what I will truly leave behind. Form in me a faith, character, love, and faithfulness worth handing to the people who come after me. Where I have failed, teach me to repent. Where I have drifted, turn me back toward You. Let my life point beyond itself so that long after I am gone, something good is still happening because I sought You first while I was here. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**PROVERBS 22:1**
