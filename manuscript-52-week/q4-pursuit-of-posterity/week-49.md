# Week 49 — Breaking the Cycle

One of the strangest parts of becoming a father is hearing your own father come out of your mouth.

Sometimes it is something good.

A phrase he used. A joke. A way of encouraging you. A habit that once seemed ordinary and now makes perfect sense because you are standing where he once stood.

Other times, it is something you swore you would never repeat.

A sharp sentence.

A certain tone.

The way anger enters the room.

The silence after conflict.

The refusal to apologize.

The need to control.

Maybe your father was rarely present, and now you notice yourself disappearing into work whenever home becomes difficult. Maybe he corrected constantly, and you hear yourself doing the same thing. Maybe affection was almost never spoken, and even though you love your children deeply, saying it still feels strangely uncomfortable.

You can reject a pattern intellectually and still carry it with you.

That is what makes family patterns difficult.

They become familiar long before we are old enough to evaluate them.

A child does not sit down and decide what fatherhood should look like. He simply experiences it. Over thousands of interactions, he learns what fathers do when they are angry, how men handle mistakes, whether affection gets spoken, how conflict ends, and whether vulnerability is safe.

Then that child becomes a man.

Eventually he becomes a father himself.

Under pressure, familiarity often takes over.

You may have spent years thinking, **“I will never be like that.”**

Then your child does something small, you react before thinking, and suddenly you hear the same words in the same tone.

That moment can produce shame.

It can also produce something more dangerous:

Resignation.

**“I guess this is just who I am.”**

**“This is how I was raised.”**

**“I learned it from my father.”**

**“I can't help that this is my first reaction.”**

There may be truth inside those statements.

Your upbringing shaped you.

Some wounds run deep.

Patterns practiced for decades do not disappear because you decided you dislike them.

But explanation cannot become permission.

Ezekiel makes an important distinction when he teaches that a son does not carry the guilt of his father's sin, and a father does not carry the guilt of his son's. Each person answers before God for his own life.

That principle matters here.

Your father's failures may explain something about you.

They do not own you.

You are not sentenced to reproduce them.

Whatever he got wrong does not get the final word on what you hand to your children.

But there is another side to this.

Breaking the cycle does not always mean doing the exact opposite of what your father did.

Sometimes men overcorrect.

A father grows up under harsh discipline and decides his children will never experience anything like it. So he avoids consequences altogether because every firm boundary feels too much like the father he resented.

Another man grows up with an absent father and decides he will never miss anything. He then becomes so involved in every decision his children make that presence turns into control.

A man who grew up poor may become determined that his children will never want for anything, then give them so much that they never learn responsibility.

You can still be controlled by the old pattern while running in the opposite direction from it.

Your father's mistake should not determine your parenting either way.

Your child needs you to respond to **him**, not to the memory of the childhood you are still trying to correct.

That requires more than promising yourself you will be different.

You have to identify the pattern precisely.

Not:

**“My dad was difficult.”**

What did he actually do?

Maybe he mocked weakness.

Maybe he never apologized.

Maybe he became silent whenever he was angry.

Maybe nothing was ever good enough.

Maybe he made promises and did not keep them.

Maybe he was generous materially but emotionally unavailable.

Maybe he never said, “I love you.”

Once the pattern has a name, you can start noticing when it appears in you.

That matters because these habits often move faster than conscious thought.

The words are already coming out before you recognize where they came from.

So breaking the cycle sometimes means deciding ahead of time what you will do instead.

If the inherited sentence is:

**“You're not even trying.”**

Choose a different response before the next mistake happens.

Ask what happened.

Let the child explain.

Correct the behavior without attacking his character.

If the inherited pattern is silence after conflict, decide that you will return to the conversation.

If it is refusing to apologize, practice saying, **“I was wrong.”**

If affection was absent, speak it.

Breaking a cycle happens in concrete substitutions.

One different sentence.

One different reaction.

One apology that your father never gave.

One boundary you keep without becoming cruel.

One moment where you stay instead of leaving.

That may feel small compared with the weight of everything you inherited.

It isn't.

Patterns are built through repetition.

They are usually changed the same way.

And there may be things you cannot untangle alone.

Some wounds from childhood are more than habits. There may be real trauma, abandonment, addiction, violence, or years of emotional damage underneath the reactions you are trying to change.

There is no shame in getting help with that.

A good priest or confessor can help you bring sin, resentment, and forgiveness before God honestly. A qualified counselor can help you understand patterns that sheer determination has not been enough to change.

Asking for help is not surrendering responsibility.

It is taking responsibility seriously enough to stop pretending that willpower has solved something it clearly has not.

You may also have to forgive your father.

Forgiveness does not mean pretending what happened was acceptable.

It does not require you to deny the damage.

It does not necessarily restore trust or erase appropriate boundaries.

It means refusing to let the wrong done to you remain the controlling force in what you now do to others.

That can take time.

Sometimes a long time.

But your children should not have to inherit a wound simply because you inherited it first.

That may be one of the most important forms of posterity.

We usually think about what we want to pass down.

Faith.

Wisdom.

Tradition.

Character.

There is another question:

**What needs to stop with me?**

What does not deserve another generation?

Maybe this is where it ends.

The temper.

The silence.

The humiliation.

The distance.

The inability to say I love you.

The belief that men never apologize.

The idea that affection must be earned.

Someone has to be the first man in the family to interrupt it.

That man can be you.

Not because your past did not affect you.

Because it does not own the future.

### This Week

Identify **one specific pattern from your own upbringing that you catch yourself repeating with your children—or overcorrecting against.**

Name it clearly.

Then write down what you want to do instead.

Not a vague intention like, **“I'll be more patient.”**

Choose the actual replacement:

**“When my child makes a mistake, I will ask what happened before I accuse.”**

**“When I am wrong, I will apologize.”**

**“I will say ‘I love you’ instead of assuming they know.”**

**“I will give a consequence without humiliating them.”**

Put the new response somewhere you will see it.

The next time the old pattern appears, do the new thing once.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, You know what I received from the men who came before me, both the good and the painful. Thank You for what was worth inheriting, and help me recognize what should go no further. Free me from repeating patterns that wounded me, but do not let my past become an excuse for the choices I make today. Give me humility to seek help where I need it, courage to change what has become familiar, and patience to keep beginning again when I fail. Let what I hand to my children be better because I allowed You to change what came through me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**EZEKIEL 18:20**
