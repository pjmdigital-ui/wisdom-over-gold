# Week 48 — Let Them See You Apologize

There is a particular kind of pride that shows up in fatherhood.

You know you were wrong.

You raised your voice more than the situation deserved. You made an accusation before hearing the whole story. You embarrassed your child. You reacted out of stress rather than wisdom.

Later, once you've calmed down, you know exactly what happened.

And still, something in you resists saying the words.

**“I was wrong. I'm sorry.”**

Part of the resistance can come from a mistaken idea about authority.

A father is supposed to lead. He is supposed to be respected. He is supposed to teach his children what is right.

So admitting that you were wrong can feel like stepping down from that position.

You may worry that apologizing will weaken your authority or give your child permission to ignore you the next time you correct him.

It usually does the opposite.

A child already knows when you were wrong.

He heard the tone.

She saw the reaction.

They experienced the unfairness.

Your refusal to acknowledge it does not preserve the image of a flawless father. It simply teaches them what you do when your own behavior does not match the standard you expect from everyone else.

Proverbs warns that hiding wrongdoing does not lead a man anywhere good. The better path is to acknowledge the wrong and turn away from it.

That principle belongs inside fatherhood.

Your children do not need a father who never fails.

They need to see what a man does **after** he fails.

That may be one of the most useful lessons you ever give them.

Because they are going to fail too.

They are going to lose their temper.

They are going to speak carelessly.

They are going to hurt people they love.

They are going to make decisions they regret.

What will they do next?

A large part of their answer may come from what they watched you do.

If you defend yourself whenever you're wrong, they learn to defend.

If you change the subject, they learn to avoid.

If you become nicer the next morning but never actually name what happened, they learn that enough time can replace repentance.

If you blame your reaction on what someone else did first, they learn that an apology is really just another way to explain why the other person caused the problem.

But if you say plainly:

**“I raised my voice at you, and I was wrong.”**

They learn something else.

They learn that being wrong does not have to become a crisis of identity.

They learn that strength can include confession.

They learn that authority does not make a man exempt from the standards he teaches.

They learn that relationships can survive mistakes when people tell the truth about them.

That matters.

Especially because fathers sometimes apologize badly.

We say:

**“I'm sorry I yelled, but you really weren't listening.”**

Everything after the word **but** changes the apology.

Now you are not simply owning what you did.

You are negotiating responsibility.

You are apologizing for your reaction while making sure your child understands that the real cause of it was still him.

There may be a separate conversation about his behavior.

Have it.

But let the apology be an apology first.

**“You were wrong to disobey me. The consequence still stands. But I was wrong in the way I spoke to you. That part belongs to me, and I'm sorry.”**

A father can hold both truths at once.

Your child's wrongdoing does not erase yours.

Your wrongdoing does not erase your child's.

That is maturity.

It also protects discipline from becoming hypocrisy.

If you tell your children that honesty matters, they should see you tell the truth when the truth makes you look bad.

If you teach them that Christians repent, they should see repentance somewhere besides church.

If you expect them to apologize to their siblings, they should eventually hear an apology from you.

Otherwise, the lesson becomes:

**People with less authority apologize to people with more authority.**

That is not Christianity.

All of us answer to God.

Fatherhood gives you responsibility over your children. It does not place you above repentance.

In fact, your position makes repentance more important because your children are watching what power does when it is wrong.

Does Dad protect himself?

Does he explain?

Does he pretend it didn't happen?

Or does he tell the truth?

That may shape how your children understand leadership long after they leave your house.

This is also why apologies should be specific.

“Sorry about earlier” is better than nothing.

But it can still hide.

What exactly are you sorry for?

Name it.

**“I embarrassed you in front of your brother.”**

**“I accused you before I heard what happened.”**

**“I was angry about work, and I took it out on you.”**

**“I spoke to you in a way I would not want anyone speaking to me.”**

Specific apologies are harder because they leave less room for self-protection.

That is also why they repair more.

A vague apology allows everyone to move on without anyone really naming the wound. A specific one tells your child that you saw what happened clearly enough to understand your part in it.

Then comes the part many fathers find even harder:

Ask for forgiveness.

Not because your child now gets to decide whether you are still his father.

Because forgiveness is part of the Christian life you are trying to teach him.

**“Will you forgive me?”**

That question places you in a position of humility in front of your own child.

That is not a loss of authority.

It is authority under God.

There is another reason your children need to see this.

Eventually they will discover that you are not who they thought you were when they were five.

Little children can see Dad as almost invincible. You know how things work. You fix what breaks. You know where to go. You make the decisions.

Then they get older.

They see the contradictions.

They notice when your temper gets the better of you. They recognize when you were unfair. They begin understanding that you have weaknesses, fears, and sins of your own.

You cannot prevent that discovery.

What you can influence is what they discover **alongside** it.

Do they discover a father who cannot tolerate being seen as imperfect?

Or a father who knows exactly where to take his imperfection?

That is where Christian fatherhood becomes powerful.

You are not trying to convince your children that their father never sins.

You are showing them what repentance looks like when a man does.

That may ultimately point them beyond you.

Because one of the dangers in fatherhood is forgetting that you are not your child's final Father.

You are an imperfect man trying to reflect something of the Fatherhood of God.

You will distort that reflection sometimes.

When you do, repentance becomes part of the reflection too.

Your child sees that Dad needs mercy.

Dad needs forgiveness.

Dad answers to Someone higher than himself.

That lesson may be as important as anything you teach through your successes.

It also changes the atmosphere of a home.

When the father can apologize, everyone else receives permission to stop defending themselves so aggressively.

Your wife can admit a mistake without worrying that it will be stored against her.

Your children can tell the truth faster because being wrong is not treated like humiliation.

Conflict can move toward repair instead of becoming a contest over who gets blamed.

The father sets some of that tone.

If your home requires everyone to protect their image, honesty becomes expensive.

If your home allows someone to say, **“I was wrong,”** without that person being crushed for it, truth becomes easier.

You can help create that kind of house.

Not by giving speeches about humility.

By practicing it where your children can see it.

And sometimes the apology needs to happen sooner than you feel ready.

Pride likes to delay.

You'll do it after dinner.

Tomorrow morning.

Once everyone has calmed down.

Maybe the moment will pass and things will feel normal again.

But normal is not the same as repaired.

Your child may stop acting upset.

That does not mean the lesson disappeared.

What happened is still part of what he is learning about you.

Go back.

Even if several days have passed.

You can say:

**“I've been thinking about something I did earlier this week, and I need to apologize.”**

It is rarely too late to model repentance.

You may even need to apologize for something much older.

Perhaps your children are adults now.

Maybe there are ways you fathered them years ago that you understand differently today.

You cannot rewrite their childhood.

You can tell the truth about your part in it.

There is no guarantee the conversation will go exactly the way you hope.

They may still be hurt.

They may remember more than you do.

They may need time.

An apology is not a tool for controlling their response.

It is an act of truth.

You own what belongs to you.

You ask forgiveness.

Then you allow the other person to respond honestly.

That may be one of the hardest parts.

A real apology gives up control of the outcome.

But that is also what makes it real.

Your children don't need to watch you maintain the illusion that you always knew what you were doing.

They need something better.

They need to see a man strong enough to tell the truth when he failed.

Because one day they will need that strength too.

### This Week

Think of **one specific moment where you owe one of your children an apology**.

Do not give a general apology.

Name exactly what you did.

Say:

**“I was wrong when I ________. I'm sorry. Will you forgive me?”**

Do not add a **but**.

Do not explain what they did first.

Do not use the apology to reopen their behavior.

Let your part stand on its own.

If your children are grown, the same practice applies. Go back to something you now understand differently and tell the truth about it.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, forgive me for the times pride has kept me from apologizing to my own children. Give me the humility to name my failures plainly instead of hiding behind explanations or authority. Teach me to show my children that repentance is not weakness, that confession is part of Christian strength, and that every father still answers to You. Help me repair what I can, ask forgiveness when I need it, and let my children learn what mercy looks like by watching me receive and practice it. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
