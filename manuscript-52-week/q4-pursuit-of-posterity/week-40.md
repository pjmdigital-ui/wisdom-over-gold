# Week 40 — One Flesh

Marriage begins with a promise that most men understand differently after they have lived it for a while.

On the wedding day, the words are clear. You are choosing this woman. You are committing your life to her. You are beginning something together that did not exist before.

Then ordinary life begins.

Schedules collide. Money gets tight. Families interfere. Children arrive. Work becomes demanding. You discover habits in each other that neither of you noticed while dating. Some seasons are joyful, and some are exhausting.

Eventually, marriage stops feeling like the wedding day.

That doesn't mean something went wrong.

One of the most dangerous assumptions a man can bring into marriage is that its success should be measured primarily by how happy he feels inside it.

Happiness matters. Marriage should contain affection, friendship, laughter, attraction, peace, and enjoyment. Those are real goods.

But Scripture gives marriage a foundation deeper than mood.

Genesis begins with God recognizing that man was not meant to remain alone. Then Scripture describes a man leaving his father and mother, joining himself to his wife, and the two becoming one flesh.

That language goes far beyond two people agreeing to make each other happy.

**One flesh describes a union.**

Your wife isn't simply a person you decided to live with because the relationship made life better. Marriage joins your lives so deeply that what happens to one of you now affects the other.

Her burdens become relevant to you.

Your decisions become relevant to her.

Money becomes ours.

Time becomes ours.

The household becomes ours.

The future becomes ours.

That can sound beautiful until two people disagree about what “ours” should look like.

Then one flesh starts costing something.

Sometimes it costs getting your way.

Sometimes it means allowing your wife's perspective to change a decision you were already sure about. Sometimes it means telling her something difficult instead of protecting your image. Sometimes it means carrying a burden together that you would rather solve privately.

Marriage asks a man to keep moving from **me** toward **us**.

That movement does not finish at the altar.

Genesis describes a man leaving and joining, but anyone who has been married long enough knows that some parts of leaving take years.

You may have left your parents' house physically while still allowing their expectations to shape your marriage more than your wife's needs do. You may still instinctively take your family's side in a disagreement. You may still compare the way your wife does something with the way your mother did it.

The wedding happened in a day.

Learning where your primary earthly loyalty now belongs can take much longer.

The same is true of joining yourself to your wife.

You made the promise once, but you live it repeatedly.

You join yourself to her when you tell the truth after making a mistake. You join yourself to her when you stay present during a difficult season rather than withdrawing into work or distraction. You join yourself to her when you make a decision with her rather than simply informing her afterward.

These aren't dramatic acts.

That is why they matter.

A marriage is rarely strengthened by one great gesture while being neglected the other 364 days of the year. One flesh is lived in ordinary decisions that keep saying:

**“Your life is not separate from mine.”**

That perspective becomes especially important when the feelings change.

Feelings toward your wife will not remain exactly the same throughout a lifetime.

There will be seasons of tremendous closeness. There may also be seasons where the relationship feels routine, tense, tired, or distant.

Those feelings are real.

They should not automatically become a verdict on the marriage.

If happiness becomes the primary measure, every difficult season begins looking like evidence.

*Maybe something is wrong.*

*Maybe we've grown apart.*

*Maybe this isn't what marriage is supposed to feel like.*

The Christian understanding of marriage gives you something sturdier to stand on.

The covenant remains while the feelings move.

That doesn't mean you ignore problems because you're married. The opposite is true. Covenant gives you a reason to address them.

If communication has deteriorated, work on it.

If affection has disappeared, tend it.

If resentment has accumulated, deal with it.

If you've hurt your wife, apologize.

If the marriage genuinely needs outside help, get it.

Covenant isn't an excuse to neglect the relationship.

It is the reason you keep tending it when tending it becomes difficult.

That is where love begins growing beyond the version that depends on everything going well.

Anyone can feel affectionate when the relationship is easy.

Marriage eventually asks whether you can love your wife when she disappoints you, when you're tired, when you disagree, and when you aren't receiving exactly what you hoped for in return.

That is where “one flesh” becomes more than wedding language.

It becomes a way of living.

And this is why marriage belongs at the beginning of Posterity.

Posterity is about what remains after you.

We usually think immediately about children and legacy. But what gets handed down through a family often begins with the marriage at its center.

Children learn something about love by watching how their parents treat each other.

They notice whether disagreements become contempt.

They notice whether apologies happen.

They notice whether affection remains after years together.

They notice whether Dad speaks about Mom with respect when she isn't in the room.

They notice whether Mom and Dad still seem to be on the same side.

What you build with your wife doesn't remain contained between the two of you.

It becomes part of the environment your children grow inside.

That gives ordinary marital faithfulness a significance you may not see while you're living it.

You may never know how much a child learned simply from watching his father remain faithful to his mother through an ordinary difficult year.

You may never know what your daughter learned about marriage because she heard you apologize when you were wrong.

You may never see the full effect of choosing your wife again and again after the wedding day.

That's posterity.

Not merely leaving something behind.

Becoming part of what shapes the people who come after you.

Marriage is one of the first places that happens.

Two people become one flesh, and then spend a lifetime learning what that promise actually requires.

The wedding begins it.

The ordinary years reveal it.

### This Week

Think about one area of your marriage where you have been thinking more like **“me”** than **“us.”**

It might involve money, your schedule, a family decision, a struggle you've been keeping to yourself, or something you've assumed your wife should simply accommodate.

Ask yourself:

**“What would one flesh look like here?”**

Then have one conversation with your wife about it this week.

Don't solve the entire issue.

Bring her fully into it.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, thank You for the woman You have joined my life to and for the covenant of marriage You have given us. Teach me to understand one flesh as more than words spoken on our wedding day. Help me keep choosing unity in the ordinary decisions of our life, especially when my preferences, pride, or emotions pull me back toward myself. Teach me to love my wife faithfully in the easy seasons and the difficult ones, and let the marriage we build become something worth handing down. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
