# Week 50 — The Cost of Letting Go

There comes a point in fatherhood when protecting your children begins to look different.

When they are young, protection is obvious. You decide where they go, what they are allowed to do, who watches them, what boundaries they live inside, and which dangers they are not mature enough to evaluate for themselves.

Then they grow.

The decisions become larger, and eventually some of them stop belonging to you.

That transition can be harder than a father expects.

You spent years teaching your children how to make good decisions, and then one day they begin making decisions that you would not have made for them.

Maybe it is the college they choose.

The career they pursue.

The person they marry.

The city they move to.

A vocation that changes the future you imagined.

Sometimes their choice is genuinely unwise, and a father should say so. Love does not require silence when you see danger.

But sometimes the difficulty is not that the choice is wrong.

It is simply not **your** choice.

That can be harder to admit.

A father can spend years building a picture of his child's future without realizing how attached he has become to it. You imagine the career, the house, the grandchildren, the holidays, where everyone will live, and how life will look ten or twenty years from now.

Then your son or daughter begins walking toward something different.

The instinct is to call that concern.

Sometimes it is concern.

Other times, concern is carrying something else underneath it:

**I need your future to remain recognizable to me.**

That is where letting go becomes costly.

King David gives us a useful picture of fatherhood at this stage.

David had spent years preparing for the building of the Temple, gathering resources for something he deeply wanted to see completed. But the work itself would belong to Solomon.

David could prepare.

David could instruct.

David could provide.

He could not live Solomon's obedience for him.

So his prayer for his son was not simply that Solomon remain safe or make David proud. David asked God to give Solomon a wholehearted devotion to carrying out what God had placed before him.

That is a different kind of fatherly prayer.

It says:

**“Lord, make my child faithful to You, even if that faithfulness takes him somewhere I would not have chosen.”**

That prayer costs something.

Because it requires admitting that your child's life belongs to God before it belongs to you.

St. Louis Martin knew that cost personally.

After the death of his wife, Zélie, he was raising their daughters as a widower. His youngest, Thérèse, eventually told him that she believed God was calling her to Carmel.

She was only fifteen.

A cloistered Carmelite life meant something far more concrete than supporting a daughter's religious interest. If she entered, Louis would be releasing his youngest daughter into a life where he would see very little of her.

He wept.

That matters.

Sometimes we tell stories of saints in a way that makes obedience sound emotionally effortless, as if holiness means the loss stops hurting.

Louis did not need the loss to feel easy before he could bless his daughter.

He gave her a small white flower as a sign of his belief that God's hand was in her calling. Later, he supported her effort to enter Carmel early, even accompanying her on the journey to Rome where she appealed directly to Pope Leo XIII.

He did not get rid of the cost.

He accepted it.

That is an important distinction.

Letting go does not mean you stop loving your children enough to worry about them.

It does not mean you become indifferent to whether their decisions are wise.

It does not mean a seventeen-year-old suddenly needs no guidance or that an adult child should never receive counsel from his father.

It means counsel eventually has to stop pretending it is ownership.

You can tell your daughter what you think.

You can warn your son.

You can ask hard questions.

You can pray.

You can make wisdom available.

But there comes a point when love has to leave room for the other person to stand before God and make a decision.

Even if you would choose differently.

That is especially difficult when the choice carries real cost.

Your child moves far away.

He takes a job that makes less money but believes it is where he should be.

She follows a vocation that changes the family life you expected.

He marries and now another relationship rightly becomes central in his earthly life.

She becomes a mother and begins building a household that is no longer an extension of yours.

The goal of fatherhood was never to keep your children orbiting you forever.

It was to help form people capable of standing without you.

That is one of the strange tensions of being a father.

If you do the job well, much of what you spend years building eventually becomes something you have to release.

You teach them to think so that they can think without you.

You teach them to pray so that they can seek God without you standing beside them.

You teach them responsibility so that they can carry responsibilities you cannot carry for them.

You teach them courage so that they can take roads you may be afraid to watch them take.

Eventually, some of your success looks like becoming less necessary.

That can hurt.

A father may say he wants independent children while quietly needing to remain the one they depend on for every major decision.

Being needed feels like love.

But love is not measured by how dependent another person remains on you.

Sometimes love says:

**“I trust what God is doing in you enough to let you go.”**

That does not mean every path they choose is God's will.

Children can make serious mistakes.

Adult children can walk away from the faith, enter unhealthy relationships, misuse freedom, or reject good counsel.

Letting go is not pretending those things are good.

It means recognizing where your authority ends.

You cannot force an adult child into faith.

You cannot control his conscience.

You cannot guarantee that she will choose wisely.

At some point, the father's work shifts from directing the path to praying over the person walking it.

That may be one of the hardest transitions in parenthood.

When your child was small, you could intervene.

Now sometimes all you can do is speak truth clearly, remain available, and entrust what happens next to God.

That can feel like doing nothing.

It is not nothing.

David's prayer for Solomon reminds us that there are places a father can reach only through God.

You may not be able to control your child's decision.

You can ask God to give him a wholehearted heart.

You can pray for courage, wisdom, obedience, repentance, protection, and clarity.

You can ask God to lead your child where you cannot.

And you can make sure your own fear does not become the voice your child mistakes for God's.

Sometimes the most loving sentence a father can say is not:

**“Here is what I think you should do.”**

It is:

**“I want you to follow God, even if that leads somewhere I did not expect.”**

That sentence may cost you something.

Say it anyway.

### This Week

Identify one area where you are holding tightly to a particular outcome for one of your children.

It may involve work, school, marriage, location, faith, vocation, or the kind of life you pictured for them.

Ask yourself:

**“Am I trying to help my child discern what is right, or am I trying to keep their life inside the picture I created?”**

Then pray specifically for that child without asking God for your preferred outcome.

Ask instead for wisdom, wholeheartedness, and the courage to follow what God actually asks of them.

If the time is right, tell your child that too.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, thank You for the children You have entrusted to me, and forgive me for the times I have confused loving them with controlling the direction of their lives. Give me wisdom to counsel them when they need counsel, courage to warn them when something is truly wrong, and humility to release decisions that no longer belong to me. Help me want faithfulness to You for my children more than I want a future that is comfortable or familiar to me. Give them wholehearted hearts for whatever You have actually called them to do, and give me the grace to bless them when following You costs me something too. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
