# Week 46 — Blessing Before Correction

Fathers are good at noticing what needs improvement.

The missed assignment. The lazy effort. The attitude. The room that still isn't clean. The mistake made for the third time after you already explained it twice.

Correction matters. Children need standards, discipline, instruction, and consequences. A father who refuses to correct because he wants to be liked is not doing his children a favor.

But correction cannot be the only voice your children hear from you.

That becomes a problem because correction naturally gets more attention than blessing. When something goes wrong, you react. When something needs fixing, there is an obvious reason to speak.

When everything is fine, you may say nothing.

Over time, a child can begin hearing from his father primarily when something needs to change.

He brings home an improved grade, and you notice the points he still missed. He plays a good game, and the conversation afterward focuses on the mistake in the third inning. He cleans most of his room, and your attention goes immediately to what was left on the floor.

You may think you're helping him improve.

He may gradually hear something different:

**Dad mostly notices what's wrong with me.**

That may not be what you believe at all.

You may love your child fiercely. You may be proud of him. You may enjoy who he is and thank God regularly for giving him to you.

But children cannot hear what you never say.

Numbers gives us a beautiful example of blessing spoken plainly. God instructs Aaron and the priests to speak words of blessing over His people—asking that God protect them, show them favor, remain gracious toward them, and give them peace.

What is striking about that blessing is what isn't there.

No correction.

No condition.

No performance review.

No reminder of what Israel needs to improve before the blessing applies.

The blessing stands on its own.

That matters for fathers.

Sometimes your child needs to hear something good from you that is not attached to achievement.

Not:

**“I'm proud of you because you got an A.”**

Not:

**“Great game—you finally played the way I know you can.”**

Not:

**“You're doing much better lately.”**

Those can all be sincere.

But they still connect approval to performance.

There should also be moments when your child hears:

**“I love being your father.”**

**“I'm glad you're my son.”**

**“I love the person you are.”**

**“I'm proud to be your dad.”**

Nothing attached.

That's blessing.

And blessing creates a stronger place for correction to land.

A child who knows his father is for him can hear, **“This behavior isn't acceptable,”** without automatically translating it into, **“I'm not acceptable.”**

Those are very different messages.

A father may know the difference intellectually, but children don't always separate them easily. Especially when correction greatly outweighs affirmation, the behavior and the identity can start blending together.

You meant:

**“You made a bad decision.”**

They heard:

**“I disappoint Dad.”**

You meant:

**“You need to work harder.”**

They heard:

**“What I do is never enough.”**

You meant:

**“I know you're capable of more.”**

They heard:

**“He isn't happy with who I am now.”**

That doesn't mean you stop correcting.

It means you become more intentional about what surrounds the correction.

St. Paul warns fathers not to provoke or discourage their children. That warning is important because discouragement does not require cruelty.

A father can discourage a child while believing he is motivating him.

The standards may be reasonable.

The correction may even be accurate.

The problem may simply be that good news is never allowed to remain good for thirty seconds before the next improvement is introduced.

Your daughter raises her grade from a 71 to an 84.

You want her to reach a 90.

There's nothing wrong with that goal.

But let the 84 be good first.

Tell her she worked hard.

Tell her you noticed the improvement.

Let her experience your pleasure before you turn the moment into the next assignment.

You can talk about the 90 later.

Not every positive moment needs to become a coaching opportunity.

That can be difficult for competent men because improvement is how we often show care.

We see potential.

We see what could be better.

We know that discipline matters and that life will eventually demand things from our children that are harder than anything we are asking now.

So we push.

There is a place for pushing.

But your child also needs a father who sometimes looks at him and doesn't see a project.

A father who simply enjoys him.

That is an important distinction.

Delight says:

**“I am happy that you are mine.”**

Correction says:

**“There is something here that needs to change.”**

A healthy father can say both.

But if the second message becomes constant and the first becomes assumed, your child may eventually stop assuming it.

This matters even more when your child is struggling.

It's easier to speak blessing over the child who is succeeding.

The grades are good.

The attitude is good.

The choices are good.

Everything gives you material to praise.

But blessing has to go deeper than performance precisely because there will be seasons when performance is poor.

Your child may struggle academically.

He may make a bad decision.

She may go through a difficult stage.

He may disappoint you.

There may be serious behavior that requires real consequences.

Even then, your child needs to know the difference between your judgment of the behavior and your love for him.

**“What you did was wrong, and we are going to deal with it. But you are still my son, and I still love you.”**

Those sentences belong together.

Christian fatherhood gives us the deepest reason why.

Our relationship with God is not built on earning our identity every morning.

We obey God because we belong to Him, not so that perhaps one day we might perform well enough to become His children.

That order matters.

Identity first.

Formation follows.

Your children need something similar from you.

They should know whose they are before every mistake becomes a referendum on whether you are pleased to have them.

And this requires words.

You may think your children already know.

Maybe they do.

Say it anyway.

A child should not have to reconstruct his father's love entirely from working overtime, paying bills, driving him to practice, and fixing things around the house.

Those actions matter.

But sometimes the blessing needs a voice.

Say what you feel.

Tell them what you see.

Let them hear that your delight in them is not a paycheck they receive only after performing well enough.

There will be plenty of opportunities to correct your children.

Fatherhood guarantees that.

Make sure there are also moments where nothing is wrong, nothing needs fixing, and you speak anyway.

### This Week

Choose each of your children and say **one thing that is not correction and is not tied to something they accomplished.**

Keep it simple and direct.

Tell them something about who they are to you.

**“I'm grateful God gave me you.”**

**“I love being your father.”**

**“I admire the kind of person you're becoming.”**

Do not add a lesson afterward.

No “but.”

No next goal.

Let the blessing stand on its own.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, thank You for the children You have entrusted to me. Forgive me for the times I have been quicker to correct than to bless and have assumed my children already knew the things I never said aloud. Give me wisdom to discipline faithfully without discouraging them, and teach me to speak love, delight, and blessing over them without requiring achievement first. Let my children know clearly that I am for them even while I help form them into the people You are calling them to become. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
