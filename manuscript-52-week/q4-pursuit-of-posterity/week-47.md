# Week 47 — Discipline Without Discouragement

A father has authority in his home, and part of that authority is correction.

Children need boundaries. They need consequences. They need someone willing to tell them when they are wrong, require more of them, and teach them that choices have consequences.

The problem is not discipline.

The problem is how easily discipline can become an outlet for the father's frustration.

A child disobeys, breaks something, talks back, ignores an instruction, or makes the same mistake again. The father reacts immediately, and the size of the response is determined less by what the child needs to learn than by how angry the father already is.

That can still look like discipline.

There is a punishment. There is an explanation. Maybe there is even a legitimate reason the child needs correction.

But underneath it, something else may be happening.

The father is relieving himself.

Hebrews describes God's discipline very differently. God's correction is directed toward our good. It may be painful, but its purpose is formation. It is aimed at producing something better in the person receiving it.

That gives fathers an important standard.

Before correcting your child, ask:

**“Is what I'm about to do for his good, or for the relief of my anger?”**

Those two things can feel almost identical in the moment.

Your son ignores you after you've told him twice.

Your daughter speaks disrespectfully.

Something gets broken after you specifically warned them not to do exactly what they just did.

You feel the anger rise, and because the child really was wrong, everything that comes next can feel justified.

But being right about the child's behavior does not automatically make your reaction right.

That distinction matters.

A consequence can be appropriate while the way you deliver it is wrong.

Your child may genuinely need to lose a privilege. He may need to repair what he damaged. She may need to apologize. There may need to be a serious conversation about disrespect, dishonesty, or irresponsibility.

None of that requires humiliation.

None of it requires screaming.

None of it requires turning one bad decision into a judgment about who your child is.

There is a difference between saying, **“What you did was irresponsible,”** and, **“You are irresponsible.”**

Between, **“You didn't listen,”** and, **“You never listen.”**

Between addressing a behavior and defining a person by it.

Children hear those differences.

This is where St. Paul's warning to fathers becomes important. He tells fathers not to treat their children in a way that leaves them discouraged.

That does not mean never upsetting them.

Your children will not always like your decisions. A real consequence may make them angry with you. Sometimes doing the right thing as a father means tolerating their unhappiness without reversing a boundary you know is necessary.

Discouragement is different.

A discouraged child begins to believe there is no point trying because nothing will ever be enough.

Every improvement is followed immediately by the next criticism. Every failure becomes evidence of a larger flaw. Every mistake produces a reaction so strong that the safest thing to do is hide mistakes in the future.

That is not what discipline is supposed to produce.

Good discipline should teach.

A child should come away understanding something more clearly than he did before: why the behavior was wrong, what the consequence is, and what he should do differently next time.

If all he learned was that Dad becomes frightening when he is angry, the most important lesson may have been missed.

This is one reason discipline sometimes needs a pause.

Not because consequences should disappear, but because a father's first reaction is not always his wisest one.

Sixty seconds can change a lot.

Enough time to lower your voice.

Enough time to separate the broken rule from the terrible day you were already having.

Enough time to ask whether the punishment you are about to give fits the behavior or simply fits the level of anger you feel.

That is self-control.

And self-control makes discipline more credible.

A child notices when consequences change based on Dad's mood. One day something earns a warning. Another day the same behavior creates a huge punishment because Dad is exhausted, stressed, or angry about something unrelated.

That unpredictability does not teach morality well.

It teaches children to monitor the father.

*What kind of mood is Dad in?*

That should not be the standard determining right and wrong in your house.

Rules should make sense before you're angry.

Consequences should have some relationship to the behavior.

And whenever possible, correction should point toward repair.

If something was damaged, help repair it.

If trust was broken, rebuild it.

If someone was disrespected, apologize.

If a responsibility was ignored, complete it.

The purpose is not merely to make the child suffer enough that he remembers.

The purpose is to form him.

That is much closer to the pattern Hebrews gives us.

God's discipline is not described as divine irritation spilling over onto His children. It has direction. It is ordered toward growth.

A father should want the same.

That also means knowing when the lesson has ended.

Some fathers continue correcting long after the consequence has already landed.

The privilege is gone. The child understands why. The apology has been made.

Then comes the lecture.

And another lecture later.

And a sarcastic remark the next morning.

And one more reminder three days afterward.

At that point, the father may no longer be forming the child. He may simply still be angry.

There should be a way back.

A child needs to know that discipline has an end.

Once the issue has been addressed, the relationship should not remain under punishment indefinitely. You can return to warmth without weakening the consequence.

In fact, that return may teach something important.

**You did something wrong. We dealt with it. I still love you. We move forward.**

That is a healthier lesson than making your child wonder how long your affection will remain withdrawn.

The discipline of fatherhood therefore includes disciplining yourself.

Your volume.

Your words.

Your timing.

Your need to make the child feel how angry you are.

You may be completely justified in correcting the behavior and still need to repent of the way you corrected it.

That does not undermine your authority.

It strengthens it.

A father who can say, **“The consequence still stands, but the way I spoke to you was wrong,”** teaches two lessons at once: actions have consequences, and authority does not excuse sin.

Your children need both.

Discipline is not the opposite of love.

Done properly, it is one expression of love.

But discipline must remain focused on the good of the child, not the emotional release of the father.

That difference can shape an entire household.

### This Week

The next time one of your children does something that genuinely needs correction, wait sixty seconds before deciding what the consequence will be.

Ask yourself:

**“What does my child need to learn from this?”**

Then ask:

**“Is the response I'm considering shaped by that lesson, or by how angry I feel right now?”**

Give the correction that serves the first answer.

And if you realize that a recent correction became more about your anger than your child's good, go back and say so. You do not have to remove an appropriate consequence to apologize for an inappropriate reaction.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, give me wisdom when I discipline my children. Keep my frustration from becoming the measure of my correction, and help me remember that discipline is meant for their good rather than the relief of my own anger. Teach me to set real boundaries, give appropriate consequences, and correct without humiliating or discouraging. Give me enough humility to apologize when my own reaction becomes part of the problem, and help me form my children with the same patient purpose You show toward me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
