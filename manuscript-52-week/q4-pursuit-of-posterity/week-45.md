# Week 45 — Presence Is One of the Basics

A man can provide almost everything his children need and still miss something they cannot replace.

He can keep a roof over their heads, put food on the table, pay for school, drive them where they need to go, make sure the bills are covered, and step in whenever there is a real problem. From the outside, he may look like a father doing exactly what he is supposed to do.

And much of that is exactly what he is supposed to do.

But children need more than what a father provides for them.

They need **him**.

That sounds obvious until you look at how easy it is to treat presence as something that comes after the important work is finished.

Once work calms down, I'll have more time.

Once we get through this financial stretch, I'll be more available.

Once they're a little older and can actually talk to me, I'll connect with them more.

Once I'm less tired.

Once I'm less distracted.

Once life settles down.

The problem is that fatherhood is happening while you're waiting.

Psalm 103 uses the compassion of a father toward his children to help us understand something about God's compassion toward us. That comparison carries weight because a child's experience of fatherhood begins with an actual man.

Before your children understand theology, they understand whether you come when they need you.

They understand your face.

Your voice.

Your patience.

Whether you look up when they speak.

Whether your body turns toward them or remains aimed at whatever you were doing before they interrupted you.

Long before a child could explain what presence means, he knows what it feels like.

That is why presence cannot be treated as a bonus after the basics are handled.

**Presence is one of the basics.**

A father can be in the house and still be largely absent from it.

We have more ways to do that than ever.

You can sit at the dinner table checking a score. You can nod while your daughter tells you about something that happened at school and realize halfway through that you haven't heard the last three sentences. Your son can ask you to do something with him, and you can say, “Give me a minute,” while continuing to scroll through something that won't matter tomorrow.

Technically, you were home.

But children know the difference between your location and your attention.

That does not mean every moment has to belong to them.

You have work to do. You need rest. Adults have responsibilities children do not understand yet. Sometimes the answer really does have to be, “Not right now.”

Presence does not mean becoming endlessly available.

It means that when you are with your children, there are regular moments when they get **you**, not merely whatever attention survives after everything else has taken its share.

That can be surprisingly difficult for a man who is used to solving problems.

Your child is upset, and your instinct is to fix whatever caused it.

Sometimes that is exactly what is needed.

Other times, the problem you can solve is not the thing your child is asking for.

A young child wakes frightened at night. You can check the room, explain that everything is safe, and send her back to bed. All of that may be true.

But sometimes she is not reaching for an explanation.

She is reaching for her father.

That is an important distinction.

Fathers can become so focused on making the problem go away that we miss the opportunity to simply stay.

Sit beside her.

Listen to the story.

Let him finish explaining even though you already know what you want to say.

Allow the emotion to come down before giving the lesson.

There are moments when your presence is part of the answer.

Jesus shows us something about this in the way He received children.

When the disciples tried to move them away, Jesus did not treat the children as an interruption to more important work. He turned toward them and received them.

That does not mean Jesus had no mission, no demands on His time, or no adults waiting for Him.

It means children were not beneath His attention.

A father needs to remember that because children often ask for attention at inconvenient times.

They rarely schedule their important moments.

Your son may finally tell you what is bothering him when you're trying to get out the door.

Your daughter may want to talk when you're exhausted.

The question that has been sitting in your child's head all day may appear at bedtime, exactly when you hoped the conversation was over.

You will not always be able to stop everything.

But some of those interruptions are fatherhood.

If you keep postponing them until you are perfectly rested, calm, and available, you may discover that eventually your children stop asking.

That is why presence also requires giving up the idea that you need to arrive as the perfect version of yourself.

You may come home stressed.

You may still be thinking about work.

You may be tired enough that getting down on the floor with a small child is the last thing you feel like doing.

You do not need to become a different man before you can turn toward your child.

Sometimes you simply need to put the phone down and sit.

Your children do not need a father who is endlessly cheerful.

They need one who is reachable.

A man can even tell his child the truth: “Dad had a hard day. Give me ten minutes to change and settle down, and then I'm yours.”

Then ten minutes later, be theirs.

The follow-through matters.

Because “in a minute” repeated often enough eventually means **probably not**.

Presence gets built through those small follow-throughs.

The game you watched even though you were tired.

The ride where you didn't fill every silence with the radio.

The question you asked and actually waited for the answer.

The night you put the phone somewhere else.

The time your child interrupted you and you turned your chair around instead of answering while staring at the screen.

None of those moments feels like legacy while it is happening.

It feels like Tuesday.

But a great deal of fatherhood is built on Tuesdays.

Your children may not remember the specific conversation. They may not remember which game you attended or exactly what you said when they were frightened.

They will remember, in a deeper way, whether you were available to them.

Whether approaching Dad felt safe.

Whether you noticed.

Whether you stayed.

That matters because one day your children will face problems much bigger than the ones they bring you now.

If they learn while they're young that you are too busy for the small things, they may not suddenly decide you are available for the big ones.

Presence builds the road they may need later.

And you cannot build that road all at once when they turn sixteen.

You build it a little at a time.

This does not require becoming a perfect father.

It requires becoming a father who turns toward his children often enough that they know where to find him.

The roof matters.

The food matters.

The bills matter.

The protection matters.

But do not spend twenty years providing everything for your children except the one thing they were trying to reach for all along.

You.

### This Week

Choose one regular period of time this week when your children usually have only part of your attention.

Dinner.

The hour after work.

A drive.

Bedtime.

Put the phone away and give them your full attention during that time.

Don't create an activity unless one naturally happens.

Simply be available.

Look at them when they speak. Ask a question. Stay long enough to hear the real answer.

And when one of your children asks for your attention at an inconvenient moment this week, before automatically saying, “In a minute,” ask yourself:

**“Does this actually need to wait?”**

If it doesn't, turn toward them.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, thank You for the children You have entrusted to me. Forgive me for the times I have confused providing for them with being fully present to them, or assumed that being in the same house meant they had my attention. Teach me to turn toward my children, to listen when they speak, to stay when they need me, and to stop waiting for some calmer or more perfect version of myself before I give them my time. Let my presence help them understand something of Your own fatherly compassion. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**PSALM 103:13**
