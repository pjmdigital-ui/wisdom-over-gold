# Week 37 — Leading Without Controlling

Leadership at home can become control faster than a man realizes.

The motive may even be good. You want the schedule to work. You want the kids to make good choices. You want money handled responsibly. You want problems solved before they become bigger problems.

So you organize, decide, correct, and step in.

After a while, it can start to feel obvious that this is what leadership means: **see the problem, determine the solution, make sure everyone follows it.**

That approach may work well at work.

A household is different.

Your wife is not an employee. Your children are not a project. Your family is not a system whose success is measured by how efficiently everyone carries out your decisions.

They are people entrusted to your care.

That changes the job.

St. Peter tells the leaders of the early Church to shepherd the people entrusted to them without lording authority over them. They are to lead by example.

That distinction matters inside a home too.

A manager focuses heavily on outcomes.

Did the task get done?

Was the schedule followed?

Did the child obey?

Did everyone do what was expected?

A shepherd cares about those things, but he also cares about what is happening **inside the people** he is leading.

Does my wife feel heard?

Does my child understand why this matters?

Am I modeling the standard I'm enforcing?

Am I creating trust, or merely compliance?

Those questions require more patience.

Control is faster.

You can make the decision yourself.

You can tell everyone what to do.

You can correct whatever doesn't match your plan.

The house may even run more smoothly in the short term.

But efficiency is not the highest goal of a family.

A perfectly organized home can still become a place where nobody feels free to disagree.

That's not leadership.

It's management.

The difference becomes especially clear in marriage.

A husband sees a problem with the household calendar, spending, a decision involving the children, or plans for the weekend. He thinks through the options, makes what he considers the best decision, and then tells his wife what the plan is.

He may genuinely believe he is serving her by removing the burden.

But he never asked what she thought.

He solved a problem that belonged to both of them by himself.

St. Paul begins his household teaching in Ephesians by calling Christians to mutual submission out of reverence for Christ. Whatever else leadership in marriage requires, it does not reduce a wife to someone who receives decisions after her husband makes them.

Partnership takes longer.

You ask.

You listen.

Sometimes you change your mind.

That's the part control doesn't like.

Control is comfortable when your judgment remains final.

Leadership requires enough humility to admit that another person may see something you missed.

The same principle applies with children, although the roles are different.

Children need authority.

They need boundaries.

They need correction.

A father who refuses to lead because he wants his children to like him isn't doing them any favors.

But authority should be aimed at formation, not domination.

There is a difference between teaching a child obedience and training a child to avoid upsetting you.

One prepares him to exercise judgment someday.

The other teaches him to watch your mood.

That's an important distinction.

If the main reason your children obey is that they know your reaction will be unpleasant, you may be getting the behavior you want without forming the people you hope they become.

Christian leadership asks more of you.

It asks you to model what you require.

If you expect honesty, they should see you tell the truth when it costs you.

If you expect patience, they should see what you do when you're frustrated.

If phones are put away at dinner, yours should not be the exception.

If apologies matter, your children should hear you give them.

That is leadership by example.

It gives authority credibility.

Control usually moves in the opposite direction.

It creates exceptions for the person in charge.

Everyone else adjusts to his schedule.

Everyone else works around his mood.

Everyone else follows rules he allows himself to bend.

He thinks the family is respecting his authority.

They may simply be learning how to manage him.

That is not the kind of leadership Christ gives us.

When St. Paul tells husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the Church, the model is self-gift. Christ's authority moved outward in service. He did not use those under His care to make Himself more comfortable.

That means leadership at home should regularly cost the leader something.

His preference.

His time.

His need to be right.

His desire to have the last word.

Sometimes his plan.

This doesn't mean a father never makes a final decision or that every family choice requires a committee meeting.

There will be moments when you need to act.

But if your normal pattern is to decide first and ask questions later, something is wrong.

A leader who never receives input eventually stops seeing what everyone else can see.

That's dangerous because control often hides behind competence.

You may actually be good at solving problems.

You may genuinely see the answer faster than everyone else.

That does not mean doing everything yourself is best for the people around you.

Sometimes the wiser form of leadership is slowing down long enough for another person to participate.

Ask your wife what she thinks before the decision is made.

Ask your child what happened before deciding why it happened.

Ask a question before giving the correction.

Allow somebody else to solve the problem differently than you would.

Not every difference needs to be fixed.

That's hard for a man who likes order.

But the goal of leadership is not to reproduce yourself in everyone around you.

Your wife has gifts you don't have.

Your children will eventually become adults who need to think, choose, fail, recover, and make decisions before God without you standing beside them.

If your leadership never leaves room for them to develop those abilities, control may actually weaken the people you were trying to strengthen.

A shepherd leads somewhere.

He doesn't drag the flock.

He walks ahead.

He stays close enough to notice danger.

He corrects when necessary.

But the pattern Scripture emphasizes is example, service, and care.

That's a much harder kind of leadership because you cannot simply demand the result.

You have to become the kind of man others can trust enough to follow.

### This Week

Choose one household decision you would normally make quickly on your own.

Before deciding, ask someone else who is affected by it what they think.

If it's a decision involving your wife, let her answer actually influence the outcome rather than asking after you've already decided.

If it involves one of your children, listen long enough to understand what is happening before moving immediately to correction.

Then ask yourself:

**“Am I trying to lead this person, or am I trying to control the result?”**

You may still reach the same decision.

But arrive there as a shepherd, not a manager.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, teach me to lead my household without trying to control everyone in it. Give me the humility to listen, the wisdom to correct when correction is needed, and the patience to let the people I love grow rather than forcing every outcome. Keep me from using authority for my own comfort. Help me lead by service, example, and the kind of self-giving love Christ has shown me. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**1 PETER 5:2–3**
