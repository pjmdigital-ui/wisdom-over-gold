# Week 34 — The Gift That Costs Something

Generosity is easy when you don't have to give up anything you wanted.

You round up at the register. You put a few dollars in the collection. You donate something you were already planning to replace. You help when the amount is small enough that you'll barely notice it later.

Those things can still be good. The problem comes when we use them to convince ourselves that we've become generous without ever allowing generosity to interfere with our own plans.

That is where giving becomes more revealing.

St. Paul tells the Corinthians that giving should be decided deliberately, not forced out of a man by guilt or pressure. He contrasts the reluctant giver with the cheerful one and uses the image of sowing: the man who scatters little should not expect the same harvest as the man willing to sow generously.

The emphasis isn't simply on the size of the gift. It is on the posture of the giver.

That matters because two men can write the same check and give in completely different ways. One gives freely because he sees what he has as something God entrusted to him. The other gives while mentally calculating everything the gift is taking away from him.

One hand is open. The other technically lets go while remaining clenched.

Most of us discover which kind of hand we have when the gift starts interfering with something we wanted for ourselves.

Suppose you've had a particularly good month at work. Maybe the business had a strong quarter. Maybe a bonus came in that you weren't counting on. You already know what you'd like to do with it, and there is nothing wrong with enjoying some of what your work produces.

Then you become aware of a real need.

Now stewardship becomes personal.

The question is no longer whether you believe in generosity. Most Christian men would say they do. The question is whether someone else's need has any claim on money you had already started spending in your imagination.

That's a harder test.

It exposes the difference between thinking of a good season as a **private reward** and seeing it as a **harvest that may have more than one purpose**.

Again, that doesn't mean every surplus belongs somewhere else. Providing for your family is part of stewardship. Saving is part of stewardship. Planning for future needs is part of stewardship.

But if every increase automatically expands your own lifestyle, generosity will always be waiting for a leftover that never arrives.

There will always be something else you can buy.

Something you can upgrade.

Something you can justify.

That's why the order matters.

A generous man doesn't have to respond to every request. He shouldn't give foolishly just because a need exists. Wisdom still matters, especially when other people depend on him.

But he should be willing to be interrupted.

His money cannot be so completely committed to his own plans that there is no room for another person.

And when God puts a legitimate need directly in front of him, he should be able to ask a difficult question:

**“What am I willing to give up so that I can help?”**

That question gets closer to sacrifice.

You may have planned to spend the money on yourself.

You may have wanted the upgrade.

You may have earned it through real work.

None of that makes generosity unnecessary. In fact, sometimes those facts are exactly what make the gift meaningful.

David understood this principle when he refused to offer God something that had cost him nothing.

The instinct is important even beyond that particular moment. A sacrifice ceases to mean much if the giver experiences no sacrifice at all.

Christian generosity follows that pattern.

The standard is not financial pain for its own sake. God isn't impressed because you made yourself miserable. Recklessness toward your own household is not holiness.

The issue is whether love ever changes what you would otherwise have done.

Does another person's need ever alter your budget?

Does your parish ever receive something before all your wants have been funded?

Does the person struggling beside you ever benefit from the good season God allowed you to have?

If the answer is always no, then what we call generosity may simply be money we didn't need anyway.

There is another danger too: giving just enough to settle your conscience.

You see a need and feel uncomfortable. So you give an amount small enough to make the discomfort go away without changing anything else.

Again, the gift may still help someone.

But there is value in asking what your giving is doing inside **you**.

Is it teaching you to hold money more loosely?

Is it reminding you that you are a steward?

Is it making another person's well-being matter enough to affect one of your own choices?

Or are you simply purchasing the feeling that you did something?

St. Paul doesn't present giving as a tax on Christians. He describes a giver who can be cheerful.

That cheerfulness becomes possible when giving stops feeling like someone is taking what belongs to you.

If everything is ultimately God's, then generosity is not losing ownership.

It is participating in what the Owner wanted done with what He placed in your hands.

That doesn't make the cost imaginary.

You may still feel it.

The purchase gets delayed.

The account balance gets smaller.

The dinner out doesn't happen.

The money goes somewhere other than where you originally intended.

That's exactly why the decision means something.

A gift can cost you and still be given cheerfully.

In fact, some of the most meaningful generosity happens when both things are true at once: you genuinely would have enjoyed keeping the money, and you genuinely are glad that someone else received it instead.

That is a different relationship with money.

You aren't afraid of it.

You aren't ashamed to enjoy it.

And you aren't owned by it.

You can earn it, save it, spend it, and when love requires it, release some of it.

That is stewardship with an open hand.

### This Week

Choose one act of generosity that will cost you something you will actually notice.

It does not need to be dramatic, and it should not put your household in a foolish position. But don't make it the amount you could give without thinking.

Help someone with a real need. Give more to your parish or a ministry you believe in. Pay for something another person is struggling to cover. Redirect money you had already planned to spend on yourself.

Then don't announce it.

Simply notice the cost and give anyway.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, everything I have has first come through Your hands. Forgive me for the times I have called myself generous while giving only what I would never miss. Give me wisdom so that my generosity is responsible, and loosen my grip enough that another person's need can sometimes change my own plans. Teach me to give freely, deliberately, and cheerfully, remembering that I am a steward of what You have placed in my hands. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**2 CORINTHIANS 9:6–7**
