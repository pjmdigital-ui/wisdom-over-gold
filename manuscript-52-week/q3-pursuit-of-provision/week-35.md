# Week 35 — As for Me and My House

A man can provide for his family without ever really asking where he is leading them.

The bills are paid. The lights stay on. The kids have what they need. Nobody is afraid of him. Maybe he works hard, keeps his word, and does his best to be present.

Those things matter.

But they still leave one question unanswered:

**What direction is this house actually moving in?**

Joshua forces that question into the open.

Near the end of his life, he stands before Israel and tells the people to choose whom they will serve. Then he makes the decision for his own household clear: whatever everyone else decides, his house will serve the Lord.

That statement is often displayed on a wall.

It sounds strong framed over a doorway.

But Joshua wasn't giving his family a slogan.

He was declaring a direction.

That's what makes the passage challenging for a husband and father.

Your home already has a direction.

Every home does.

If you don't choose it deliberately, the household doesn't remain neutral. It simply begins moving toward whatever is easiest, loudest, most convenient, or most repeated.

The schedule sets a direction.

The television sets a direction.

The phones set a direction.

The way money gets spent sets a direction.

What gets talked about at dinner sets a direction.

What gets ignored sets a direction too.

Faith works the same way.

A man may sincerely believe that God matters while allowing almost every expression of that belief inside the home to be initiated by someone else.

His wife reminds everyone about Mass.

She starts grace.

She makes sure the children receive religious education.

She brings up confession.

She answers questions about faith.

She keeps track of feast days, prayers, and Church life.

The husband may agree with all of it.

He may even be grateful that she does it.

But agreement isn't the same thing as leadership.

There is a difference between living in a Catholic home and helping establish one.

That doesn't mean the father has to become the family theologian.

He doesn't need to know every answer.

He doesn't need to turn dinner into a catechism lesson every night.

He doesn't need to force long prayers on children who are half asleep.

He does need to make his own faith visible enough that nobody in the house has to wonder whether it matters to him personally.

That can be much simpler than men imagine.

Start the prayer.

Ask the question.

Bring up something from the Sunday Gospel.

Tell your children why you believe what you believe.

Let them hear you ask God for help when something is difficult.

Let them see you go to confession.

Let them watch you admit that you don't know the answer to something and then care enough to find out.

Faith that is never visible at home can easily become something children associate mainly with a building they visit once a week.

But Scripture puts the work much closer than that.

When Moses teaches Israel how faith is to be handed down, he tells parents to keep God's words in their own hearts and speak about them in the ordinary movements of family life—at home, along the road, at the beginning of the day, at the end of it.

The point isn't to create a household where every conversation sounds religious.

It's to create one where God is not treated like a separate subject.

He belongs in the actual life of the family.

In the worry about money.

In the argument that needs forgiveness.

In the decision about work.

In the sickness nobody understands.

In the success everyone is celebrating.

In the question your child asks when you don't have a polished answer.

That is part of what it means for a household to serve the Lord.

It also means that leadership cannot be measured only by what a man prevents.

Sometimes we set the bar too low.

I don't cheat on my wife.

I don't abandon my children.

I don't drink myself into oblivion.

I don't scream at everyone.

I provide.

Good.

Those are meaningful things.

But Christian leadership isn't simply the absence of serious failure.

Joshua doesn't say, “As for me and my house, we will try not to ruin anything.”

He names what they are **for**.

That matters because a household needs more than a list of things it avoids.

It needs a positive direction.

What are we trying to become?

What do we value?

What comes first here?

What do we do when we're wrong?

What does success mean in this family?

Where does God fit when life is busy?

Those questions will be answered somehow, whether you ever sit down and answer them intentionally or not.

Your children will watch.

They will learn what matters by noticing what gets protected.

If work interrupts prayer every time, they notice.

If sports always win over worship, they notice.

If you tell them God matters but never speak to Him yourself, they notice.

If you talk about honesty and then bend the rules whenever it benefits you, they notice that too.

This isn't a reason to become anxious about every mistake you make.

Your children do not need a perfect father.

They need an honest one.

In fact, one of the strongest ways you can set the direction of a home is by letting them see what you do after you fail.

You apologize.

You correct yourself.

You ask forgiveness.

You begin again.

That teaches something important about serving God.

It shows them that faith isn't a performance by people who always get it right.

It's a direction people return to.

That's what Joshua's words ultimately require.

Not one dramatic family declaration that solves everything.

A direction reinforced through ordinary decisions.

Who starts the prayer tonight?

Who changes his tone after a hard day?

Who turns the phone over during dinner?

Who says, “I was wrong”?

Who tells the children, “This is what we believe, and here's why it matters”?

Who makes it clear that Sunday Mass isn't something the family squeezes in if nothing else gets in the way?

Those are small decisions.

Together, they tell a family where it is going.

And this belongs inside the Pursuit of Provision because provision was never supposed to end with money.

You work so there is something to bring home.

You steward money so the household has stability.

But eventually you have to ask what kind of household all that work and money are actually supporting.

A man can fund a house beautifully and never lead it anywhere.

The goal is more than keeping everyone comfortable.

It's building a home ordered toward God.

You don't have to do that perfectly.

You do have to do it on purpose.

### This Week

Ask yourself one question:

**“If my family followed the direction I personally set this week, where would we be headed?”**

Don't answer based on what you believe in principle. Look at what you actually initiated.

Did you pray?

Did you make room for Mass?

Did you bring God into a conversation?

Did your family see you practice the faith you want them to inherit?

Choose **one simple action** that sets the direction of your home toward God this week. Start grace yourself. Pray with your wife or children. Bring up something from Scripture or Mass. Ask your family a question about faith and listen to the answers.

Don't try to overhaul the spiritual life of your house in seven days.

Just stop leaving its direction to accident.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, You have entrusted me with more than providing for a household. You have called me to help lead it toward You. Forgive me for the times I have assumed that keeping the bills paid and avoiding obvious failures was enough. Give me the courage to make my faith visible in my own home, to initiate what matters, and to lead by the example of the life I actually live. Let my family see in me a man who is still learning, still returning, and still choosing You. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**JOSHUA 24:15**
