# Week 5 — Sacrifice Is Part of the Life

One of the easiest mistakes to make is assuming that difficulty means you chose the wrong path.

We do this everywhere.

The marriage becomes hard, so something must be wrong with the marriage.

The work becomes demanding, so maybe the job is wrong.

Prayer becomes dry, so maybe nothing is happening.

Training hurts, discipline is inconvenient, sacrifice costs more than expected—and the discomfort itself begins arguing that we should stop.

But difficulty is not proof that a path is wrong.

Sometimes difficulty is simply part of the path.

James tells believers to recognize that trials can produce endurance and maturity. St. Paul describes suffering producing endurance, character, and hope.

Neither man is pretending suffering is pleasant.

The point is that hardship can do something in you that comfort cannot.

That matters because modern life gives us thousands of ways to reduce friction.

Some of those are genuine improvements.

But convenience can also train a man to interpret every cost as a problem to remove.

If something is uncomfortable, change it.

If something demands patience, speed it up.

If a commitment begins requiring more than expected, renegotiate it.

If a discipline hurts, find an easier version.

Eventually the man does not merely prefer comfort.

He starts believing comfort is evidence that things are going correctly.

Christianity destroys that assumption at the Cross.

Jesus never promised that obedience would always feel smooth.

Sometimes doing the right thing costs more precisely because it is the right thing.

Forgiveness costs pride.

Honesty can cost money.

Faithfulness can cost opportunity.

Fatherhood costs time.

Marriage costs preference.

Prayer costs attention.

Discipline costs comfort.

Generosity costs something you could have kept.

If you remove every cost, you may remove the very conditions in which virtue is formed.

The trial may be doing something in you. If you spend your life finding ways around every hard thing, you may also avoid the patience, endurance, courage, or humility that could have been formed through it.

This is not an argument for suffering unnecessarily.

Christianity does not ask you to create pain so you can feel holy. If a problem can be solved wisely, solve it. If a dangerous situation needs to be left, leave it. If you need help, ask for it.

The point is simpler.

Do not treat discomfort as God's automatic stop sign.

Sometimes the hard part is where the work is.

Anyone who has trained physically understands this.

A muscle does not grow because you kept it perfectly comfortable. A skill does not improve because you stopped whenever the repetition became frustrating.

Character is not identical to training a muscle, but the comparison helps: repetition under resistance changes capacity.

There are forms of patience you cannot learn until something takes too long.

Forms of courage you cannot learn until fear is present.

Forms of faithfulness you cannot learn until leaving becomes attractive.

Forms of trust you cannot learn until you do not know the outcome.

Sacrifice is not an interruption to life.

It is part of a life ordered toward something greater than comfort.

### This Week

Name one discomfort you have been avoiding simply because it is difficult.

Ask:

**“What might God be forming in me if I stop treating this difficulty as a reason to run?”**

Then take one concrete step into the thing you know you should do.

### Prayer

Form endurance, character, humility, and courage in me through the hard things I cannot simply remove. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**ROMANS 5:3–5**
