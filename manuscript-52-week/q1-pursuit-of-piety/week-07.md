# Week 7 — Turning Struggle Into Offering

Catholics sometimes use a phrase that can sound almost dismissive if it is spoken carelessly:

**Offer it up.**

Said badly, it can sound like: stop complaining, suffer quietly, and move on.

That is not what the idea means.

To offer suffering is not to deny that it hurts.

It is to refuse to let the suffering be spiritually useless.

St. Paul tells the Romans to present their bodies as a living sacrifice. Elsewhere he speaks of his sufferings in connection with the Body of Christ.

The Christian idea is that what we endure can be given to God rather than merely endured inside ourselves.

That changes the posture.

Pain is still pain.

But now the question becomes:

**“Can I give this to God for someone?”**

Maybe you are carrying physical pain.

Maybe it is a difficult season at work.

Maybe you are fasting.

Maybe you are caring for someone and the responsibility is exhausting.

Maybe you are waiting on something that refuses to move.

You do not have to manufacture additional suffering.

You already have enough material.

The practice is to take what is already present and attach love to it.

That is what makes Christian sacrifice different from mere toughness.

A man can endure pain because he is stubborn.

He can endure it because he wants to prove something.

He can endure it because he is afraid to ask for help.

None of those things is automatically holy.

The offering becomes Christian when it is joined to love and given to God.

St. Maximilian Kolbe gives the clearest extreme example. In Auschwitz, he offered his life in place of another prisoner. Most men will never face a sacrifice anything like that.

But the principle is not limited to dramatic martyrdom.

Love lays life down in pieces long before anyone is asked to lay it down all at once.

You stay up with the sick child.

You give the time you wanted for yourself.

You fast and pray for someone who is struggling.

You carry the inconvenience without making sure everyone knows how inconvenienced you are.

You accept a legitimate burden for the good of another person.

And you give it to God.

There is an important balance here.

Offering suffering does not mean refusing help.

Galatians tells us to bear one another's burdens. If you need help, allowing another man to carry part of the burden may be exactly what Christian community requires.

The goal is not to maximize pain.

The goal is to redeem the pain already present by turning it outward in love.

That can be as simple as naming the person you are offering it for.

A difficult workout.

A fast.

A sleepless night.

A frustrating responsibility.

Instead of repeating, **“I hate this,”** the entire time, you can pray:

**“Lord, I offer this for him.”**

The suffering may not change.

You do.

You stop being the only person in the picture.

That movement from self toward love is at the center of sacrifice.

### This Week

Choose one hardship that is already part of your week.

Do not add a new one.

Choose one person who needs prayer and offer that hardship to God for that person whenever the difficulty becomes noticeable.

Keep the prayer short:

**“Lord, I offer this for ______.”**

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, I waste suffering when I turn inward and make the pain only about me. Teach me to place the struggles already present in my life into Your hands and attach love to them. Keep me from chasing pain for its own sake, but give me the generosity to offer what I cannot avoid for the good of another person. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**ROMANS 12:1**
