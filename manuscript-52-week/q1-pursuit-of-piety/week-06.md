# Week 6 — The Cross Wasn't a Mistake

There are forms of suffering you can understand after enough time.

You look back and see what you learned.

You recognize the relationship that needed to end, the discipline that needed to be built, or the weakness you finally addressed because circumstances forced you to.

Other suffering does not become that neat.

The person dies.

The prayer is not answered the way you wanted.

The opportunity disappears.

The diagnosis remains.

You search for a reason and find none that feels sufficient.

Christian faith does not give you a formula that explains every individual pain.

It gives you the Cross.

From the human point of view, the Crucifixion looked like the collapse of everything the disciples thought was happening.

The Messiah was killed.

The movement appeared defeated.

The man they had followed was placed in a tomb.

If you had judged God's faithfulness on Friday afternoon alone, you could have concluded that the entire plan had failed.

It had not.

The Resurrection was not God repairing an unexpected mistake.

The Cross was not proof that the Father had lost control.

That does not mean every terrible thing in your life should be treated as though the terrible thing itself is good.

Christianity does not ask you to call cancer good, betrayal good, death good, or injustice good.

It tells you that suffering does not automatically mean God has abandoned the story.

That distinction matters in hard seasons because pain creates interpretations.

If God loved me, this would not have happened.

If I had enough faith, this prayer would have been answered.

If I were on the right path, it would not hurt this much.

Those conclusions can feel logical while you are inside the suffering.

The Cross tells you they are not reliable.

The Son of God was perfectly faithful and still suffered.

That fact changes what suffering is allowed to prove.

It cannot, by itself, prove that God has left you.

Sometimes you will understand later.

Sometimes you may not understand in this life.

Faith then becomes less about possessing the explanation and more about deciding whether God is trustworthy before the explanation arrives.

That is difficult because men like solutions.

We want the reason.

We want the thing to fix.

We want the lesson we can write down and use later.

But grief does not always need a lesson before it needs presence.

There are times when the faithful response is simply to remain with God inside the thing you cannot explain.

Hebrews tells us that Christ endured the Cross with a joy beyond it, not because the Cross ceased to be painful, but because pain did not define the entire reality.

Friday was real.

Sunday was also real, even before anyone standing at the Cross could see it.

You will have Fridays in your life where Sunday is invisible.

The Christian task is not to pretend you can already see it.

It is to refuse to declare the story over while God is still God.

### This Week

Choose one unresolved pain in your life.

Stop trying, for one week, to force an explanation for why it happened.

Bring the pain directly to God instead.

Pray honestly about what you lost, what you wanted, and what still hurts.

Then end with one sentence:

**“I do not understand this, but I will not treat my lack of understanding as proof that You have left me.”**

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, I want explanations for the things that hurt, and sometimes I do not receive them. Keep me from turning pain into evidence that You have abandoned me. Teach me to remain faithful on the Friday when I cannot yet see Sunday, and let the Cross remind me that suffering never placed Your plan outside Your hands. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.

**HEBREWS 12:2**
