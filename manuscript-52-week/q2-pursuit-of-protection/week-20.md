# Week 20 — Slow to Anger

Being slow to anger does not mean being a slow man.

You can make fast decisions, speak directly, protect a boundary, and confront something wrong without allowing anger to set the speed of your response.

James tells believers to be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger because human anger does not reliably produce God's righteousness.

That order matters.

Listen first.

Speak second.

Anger does not get to go first.

Most regrettable reactions reverse the order.

You hear half the sentence.

You assume the rest.

You answer the intention you assigned to the other person rather than what was actually said.

Then ten minutes later you discover information you would have known if you had waited thirty seconds.

Slowness is therefore not weakness.

It is information gathering.

It lets the situation become clearer before your reaction becomes part of the situation.

This is especially important at home because the people closest to you rarely get to choose when they encounter your stress.

You come through the door already carrying work.

A child asks the wrong question at the wrong second.

Your wife brings up something legitimate while your mind is still somewhere else.

The anger looks like it belongs to the current moment.

Sometimes it began hours earlier.

Slow to anger means learning to notice what was already present before someone else accidentally touched it.

Am I angry at this person?

Or am I tired?

Afraid?

Embarrassed?

Worried about money?

Carrying something from work?

A man who does not know what he is carrying will hand it to whoever stands closest.

Delay creates room for honesty.

You can say:

**“I am too worked up to answer this well right now. Give me ten minutes and I will come back.”**

Then come back.

That is not avoidance.

It is a disciplined pause with a promised return.

The goal is not to become impossible to provoke.

The goal is to stop treating provocation as permission to react badly.

### This Week

When anger rises this week, delay your first response long enough to identify what is actually happening.

Ask three questions:

**What happened?**  
**What am I already carrying?**  
**What response will I respect tomorrow?**

Then respond.

### Prayer

Father in Heaven, make me slow enough to hear before I answer and honest enough to recognize the pressure I was already carrying. Keep other people from becoming targets for anger that began somewhere else. Give me the patience to pause without avoiding and the discipline to return to the conversation with wisdom. I make this prayer in the almighty name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and Savior. Amen.
