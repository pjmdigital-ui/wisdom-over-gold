# Week 15 — The Small Shortcuts

Most compromises do not announce themselves as compromises.

They sound reasonable.

**Just this once.**

**It is only a few dollars.**

**Nobody is being hurt.**

**I will make it right later.**

That is what makes small shortcuts dangerous. They rarely feel large enough to deserve a moral decision.

Jesus teaches that faithfulness in small things matters because small things reveal what a man does with responsibility before the stakes become dramatic.

We usually imagine character being tested under pressure.

Often it is tested under convenience.

You say a project was delayed for a reason that is mostly true but leaves out your own part in it.

You exaggerate the timeline so you sound more prepared than you were.

You quietly use an age, category, or technicality in your favor because nobody is likely to check.

Each individual choice is small enough to explain away.

The problem is that behavior trains character whether the amount is large or small.

There is no separate part of you that handles “exceptions that don't count.”

The man making the small decision is the same man who will eventually face a larger one.

That is why Scripture pays attention to the little things.

A small act of faithfulness is not meaningless because the stakes were low.

It is a rehearsal.

So is the shortcut.

One of the lies behind compromise is the idea that the result determines whether the decision mattered.

Nobody found out, so nothing happened.

The number was tiny, so it did not matter.

The person never knew, so there was no damage.

But something did happen.

You practiced becoming a man who makes exceptions when the exception benefits him.

That matters even if nobody else saw it.

The reverse is true too.

Small integrity compounds.

You tell the complete truth when nobody would have known the difference.

You return the money.

You correct the mistake in the other person's favor.

You show up when cutting the corner would have been easier.

Nobody applauds.

That is fine.

You are building the kind of man who can be trusted when the stakes eventually rise.

### This Week

Pay attention to the phrase **“just this once.”**

When you hear it in your own reasoning, stop.

Ask:

**“Would I be comfortable if this became my normal way of handling things?”**

Then do the completely honest version, even if the difference seems small.

### Prayer
